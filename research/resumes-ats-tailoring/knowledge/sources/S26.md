## Learned points
1. Defines "AI self-preference bias": an LLM evaluator favoring content it generated itself over human-written content (LLM-vs-Human) or over another LLM's content (LLM-vs-LLM). Mechanism proposed: "self-recognition", a model's implicit ability to recognize its own outputs; larger models show more of it. — [Research]
2. Method: resume correspondence experiment on a public dataset of LiveCareer resumes written before widespread generative AI (2,484 resumes; 2,245 after cleaning; 24 occupational categories). Only the executive summary was swapped for an LLM-generated version; experience, skills and education were left unchanged so factual content was held constant. — [Research]
3. Authors deliberately did not let LLMs edit structured sections (work history, skills, education) because that "could lead to hallucinations or factual inaccuracies"; the summary was chosen because it is the most subjective, stylistically flexible section. — [Research]
4. Models tested as generators/evaluators: GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4-turbo, LLaMA 3.3-70B, Mistral-7B, Qwen 2.5-72B, DeepSeek-V3, plus small LLaMA 3.2-1B and 3.2-3B. Generated summaries were length-constrained (30–80 words, the human 1st–3rd quartile) to control verbosity bias; A/B order was randomized to control position bias. — [Research]
5. Evaluator prompt was job-agnostic: pick the stronger of two summaries "based on relevance, skills, and experience", answer "A" or "B" only. So results describe a generic LLM screener, not a specific commercial ATS. — [Research]
6. LLM-vs-Human, statistical parity (raw): 8 of 9 models favored their own summary, 26%–98% (e.g., GPT-4o 97.6%, LLaMA-3.3-70B 96.3%, Qwen 95.9%, DeepSeek-V3 95.5%, Mistral-7B 57.0%, LLaMA-3.2-3B 26.1%, LLaMA-3.2-1B -3.2%). — [Research]
7. LLM-vs-Human, equal opportunity (controlling for content quality via conditional logistic regression on LIWC features, BERTScore, ROUGE-L): large models 67%–82% (GPT-4o 81.9%, LLaMA-3.3-70B 78.9%, Qwen 78.0%, DeepSeek-V3 71.6%, GPT-4o-mini 67.9%, GPT-4-turbo 66.9%); Mistral-7B 28.0%; LLaMA-3.2-3B 11.6%; LLaMA-3.2-1B not significant (-1.4%). — [Research]
8. Human-annotation check (18 Prolific annotators, 30 pairs each, 3 annotators per condition): GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3 and LLaMA 3.3-70B each picked their own summary over the human one even when annotators judged the human summary better. Small annotation sample. — [Research]
9. LLM-vs-LLM self-preference is weaker and model-specific: DeepSeek-V3 favored its own outputs (raw 69% vs LLaMA-3.3-70B, 28% vs GPT-4o; quality-controlled 15.5% and 12%); GPT-4o actually favored DeepSeek-V3's outputs (-39.1% raw); annotation-based estimates were not statistically significant. — [Research]
10. Robustness: when LLMs merely revised/polished the human summary (a realistic job-seeker use case) rather than writing from scratch, LLM-vs-Human self-preference persisted, attenuated (quality-controlled 46.9%–68.7% for large models; raw still above 90% for some). — [Research]
11. Hiring simulation: 24 occupations × 30 runs; pools of 10 (5 human, 5 evaluator-generated versions of the same candidates) for 4 interview slots; candidates using the evaluator's own LLM were 23%–60% more likely to be shortlisted; largest gap in sales, accounting, business development, finance; smallest in agriculture and automobile. CIs excluded zero in every category. — [Research]
12. Authors warn of a "lock-in" effect: stylistic patterns of dominant LLMs could become entrenched in applicant pools, and uneven LLM access across socioeconomic or linguistic groups could amplify inequity. — [Research] (authors' projection, not measured)
13. Mitigation: a system-prompt instruction to ignore whether text is human- or AI-written cut quality-controlled bias GPT-4o 82→61, LLaMA-3.3-70B 79→30, DeepSeek-V3 72→60 (17%–62% relative); majority vote with two small models cut it to 30/23/29 (60%–71% relative). — [Research]
14. Practical implication for candidates (Inferred): a human-written resume may be disadvantaged if an employer screens with an LLM, and which LLM an applicant uses matters; but the job seeker cannot know which model an employer uses, and LLM-vs-LLM effects are inconsistent, so "use model X to beat screener X" is not a reliable strategy. — [Inferred]
15. Implication for an AI resume assistant (Inferred): AI polishing of free-text sections (summary) is the use case studied; the paper does not show that AI-generated content improves substantive fit, and it explicitly kept facts fixed. An assistant should keep facts fixed and change only expression, consistent with the study design. — [Inferred]
16. Cites related work: Wiles et al. (2025, Management Science) found algorithmic resume-writing assistance improved writing quality and increased hires; Zheng et al. (2023) and Panickssery et al. (2024) on LLM-as-judge self-preference. Not read directly here. — [Reported]
17. Policy suggestions from the authors: disclosure of AI use in resume screening and safeguards; third-party audits incorporating self-preference metrics. — [Research]

## Key quotes
- "LLMs consistently prefer resumes generated by themselves over those written by humans or produced by alternative models, even when content quality is controlled." — https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.00462
- "candidates using the same LLM as the evaluator are 23% to 60% more likely to be shortlisted than equally qualified applicants submitting human-written resumes" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.00462
- "this bias rewards access to specific generative technologies and penalizes those without it, even when applicants are otherwise equally qualified." — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.00462
- "You should not consider or infer whether the resumes were written by a human or by AI. Focus only on the quality of the content." — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.00462 (mitigation prompt)

## Limitations / bias
- Only the executive summary was varied and evaluated (evaluators saw only the two summaries, not full resumes); results may not generalize to whole-resume screening or to commercial ATS products.
- Job-agnostic evaluation prompt (no job description), so no evidence about keyword/job-fit matching.
- Models are 2024–2025 vintage; authors note newer models will differ. Summaries were LiveCareer pre-AI resumes, some of low writing quality (e.g., objective statements), which may exaggerate the human-vs-AI gap even after controls.
- Human-annotation ground truth is small (18 annotators); LLM-vs-LLM annotation results not significant.
- Preprint, not a final journal article; "more likely" figures are percentage-point differences in selection rates, easy to misreport.

## Best for
- Evidence that LLM screeners can favor LLM-styled text, plus a model of how an AI resume assistant should edit expression only while holding facts constant; useful caution against overpromising "beat the AI screener" outcomes.
