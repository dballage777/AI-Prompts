## Learned points
1. Reports a study of 246 U.S. working-age adults (Media Psychology, published August [2025]; University of Kansas researchers): participants accepted decisions by a hiring manager or an algorithm-aided manager claimed to be unbiased, but doubted claims that an algorithm alone was unbiased. — [Reported] (study not read directly)
2. A separate survey found college students rated AI higher on fairness and objectivity than working-age adults; researcher suggests students may be naive because they have less job-search experience. — [Reported]
3. Ethical stance quoted: employers should be transparent about AI use in reviewing applications but wary of claiming AI is unbiased. — [Reported]
4. An HR software (Pinpoint) executive says many recruiters use AI to automate tasks such as rejection emails triggered by basic criteria like location or experience; AI making independent nuanced hiring decisions is "the exception, not the norm." — [Reported] (vendor voice)
5. Cites a 2024 Resume Builder survey of business leaders: nearly 70% planned to use AI in hiring in 2025; almost all believed AI produces biased recommendations; 23% said AI conducts interviews; 71% said their organization lets AI reject candidates without human oversight. — [Reported] (survey not read; note it conflicts with S28's claim that no major ATS rejects without human involvement)
6. Most employers are said to be less transparent than they should be; minimum suggested: disclose on careers sites and applications where and how AI tools are used. — [Reported]
7. Regulation: employers in places like Colorado and New York City are more proactive about transparency because of regulatory requirements; more city/state AI disclosure requirements expected. Some companies lack internal clarity about where AI is deployed. — [Reported]
8. Mid-level AI uses: scanning resumes to identify transferable skills; ranking applicants against job profiles; risk arises if employers cannot explain why someone was screened out; hiring decisions should sit with a human. — [Reported]
9. Employer examples: one tech company discloses AI's role to every applicant, uses AI to detect fraudulent applications, says it does not let AI make hiring decisions, and audits its AI; another discloses AI use, explains handling of applicants' AI-generated data, and audits for gender and age bias. — [Reported]
10. Pro-AI argument quoted: AI used consistently against the same success criteria can reduce bias relative to manual review. — [Reported]
11. Implication for job seekers/assistant: AI screening practices vary widely and are often undisclosed, so an assistant should not promise how a given employer screens; fraud detection exists, so fabricated or inconsistent applications carry risk. — [Inferred]
12. Implication: an assistant can tell users that, where laws require it (e.g., NYC, Colorado as referenced), employers may disclose AI use, and users can look for such notices; specifics of those laws are not given in this article. — [Inferred]

## Limitations / bias
- Secondary reporting; key numbers come from a Resume Builder survey (a resume-product company) and a small academic study, neither read directly.
- Quoted experts include HR-tech vendor employees with commercial interests.
- Employer-transparency focus; little direct resume-writing guidance.
- Survey claim of 71% auto-rejection without human oversight conflicts with vendor/recruiter claims in S28; treat both as contested.

## Best for
- Evidence that AI use in screening is widespread, inconsistently disclosed, and contested, so a resume bot should avoid confident claims about how any employer's AI screens and should stress honest, verifiable content.
