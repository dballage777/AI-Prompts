## Learned points
1. The CPDC frames generative AI as "a starting point, a companion for reflection, or a tool to organize your thinking." It says AI should never replace the user's judgment, authentic voice, or decisions. — Documented
2. The CPDC "strongly discourages" using chatbots to generate most or all of a resume's bullet points. The stated reason is to avoid "homogenized, disingenuous content." — Documented
3. The page gives a table of prompt styles to avoid and to use. Avoid "demand" prompts ("Build me a resume...", "Draft my bullet points...", "Make this sound professional..."). Use collaborative, feedback-oriented prompts instead ("Give me targeted feedback on my draft resume...", "Highlight areas where my bullet points could be clearer...", "Provide examples of stronger action verbs and offer links to sources..."). — Documented
4. The page says GenAI can legitimately help users get past writer's block, compare phrasings, and tailor materials. — Documented
5. Job search: the CPDC advises against using third-party apps to automate application submissions. Its stated reason is that employers flag or filter AI-autonomous submissions. This is relevant to auto-apply features. — Documented (the claim about employer behavior is the CPDC's assertion, with no data given)
6. Research: users should always ask chatbots for links or citations and cross-check findings against employer sites, CPDC resources, and professional contacts. — Documented
7. Privacy: before using any AI platform with personal information, application materials, or sensitive content, users should check that the tool meets university policy. — Documented
8. The page defines three kinds of AI in job platforms. "Chatbot": a conversational tool such as ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini. "Feature": built-in helpers such as resume helpers, skill suggesters, and job-description summarizers. "System": background algorithms for search ranking, skill matching, and recommendations. — Documented
9. Interviews: never use live AI tools during an actual interview. AI is fine for practice, mock questions, and follow-up brainstorming. — Documented
10. Networking: AI can outline messages, but the final message should keep the person's own story, humor, and curiosity. — Documented
11. Tools named on the page: VMock (machine-learning resume scoring, formatting analysis, and job-description matching through its "Resume Optimizer"), Handshake (job recommendations, natural-language search), BigInterview (AI feedback on eye contact, filler words, speech rate, and tone), and CareerShift (outreach drafting). — Documented
12. VMock workflow on the linked page: upload a PDF of the resume, review the feedback, and edit "to get a green score." The CPDC then encourages a review with a human career consultant. Automated scoring is treated as a first pass, not a final verdict. — Documented
13. The page calls itself evolving guidance ("a starting point rather than a fixed set of instructions"). — Documented
14. Implication for a resume bot: draft or improve from the user's own facts, explain why each change is made, and keep the user's voice. Do not generate a whole resume from scratch, and do not submit applications for the user. — Inferred

## Limitations / bias
- Written for CMU students and tied to CMU-licensed tools and policies. No date is given, and none of the claims (for example, that employers filter AI submissions) come with data.
- The page promotes CMU-partnered vendor tools (VMock and others) without evaluating them.
- The sample-resume and quick-tips PDFs listed on the hub page were not opened.
## Best for
- Setting the bot's collaboration stance: feedback-first editing in the user's own voice, no wholesale AI-written bullets, no auto-apply, and verified citations.
