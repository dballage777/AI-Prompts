# S15 — Mind your key: An Empirical Study of LLM API Credential Leakage in iOS Apps (Gao et al.)
- URL: https://arxiv.org/html/2606.12212 (other pages read: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12212 for submission metadata)
- Access status: OPENED · Date read: 2026-11-25
- Pages read: https://arxiv.org/html/2606.12212 (full HTML text v3, sections 1–7 plus references); https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12212
- Published / last updated: v1 submitted 10 Jun 2026; v3 dated 23 Jun 2026 (arXiv cs.SE). Abs page comments: "12 pages, 4 figures, 4 tables" (no venue stated). App data collected Oct 2025; re-test 90 days after disclosure.
- Type: paper (preprint, not stated as peer-reviewed)

## Learned points
1. [Research] Method: 38,520 candidate apps from US App Store iTunes search queries (model names incl. "Claude"/"Anthropic", AI-feature keywords, combos) → 5,619 unique (de-duplicated 25 Oct 2025) → stratified sample of 1,092 (99% confidence, 3.5% margin) → 444 apps with testable LLM features after manual screening by six paid student evaluators in pairs on iOS 18 devices (exclusions: 45 download failure, 63 launch failure, 318 paywall/registration/bring-your-own-key, 222 no observable LLM feature; 4% disagreement).
2. [Research] Tool "LLMKeyLens": mitmproxy on a host with a custom root CA installed on the physical iPhone (no jailbreak, no binary decryption); sends a fixed test prompt and logs request/response pairs; apps that bypass the system proxy (e.g. Apple NWConnection) are retested via a VPN client (Potatso) routing to mitmproxy in transparent mode. Keys matched by provider rules (api.openai.com + `sk-` bearer; generativelanguage.googleapis.com + key; api.anthropic.com + `x-api-key`). Backend proxies detected from responses containing a known `model` field or a `usage` object with `prompt_tokens`/`completion_tokens`.
3. [Research] Validation: each credential replayed once with a benign payload (e.g. `max_tokens=10`, or a free model-listing endpoint). Of 421 candidate apps, 282 actively exploitable; 139 not (66 servers gone, 44 restricted e.g. 401/403/429/402/region, 29 inactive).
4. [Research] Headline: 282 of 444 apps (64%) leaked exploitable LLM credentials in traffic, across at least 10 LLM providers and 5 cloud platforms; 146 (52% of leakers) fully exploitable; 13 app categories; Health & Fitness highest rate (47%, 7/15), Productivity 40% (142/355), Entertainment 34 apps; Finance and Medical had none. Median rating count of vulnerable apps 16, mean 12,851; 15% had over 1,000 ratings; the most popular had over 2,305,613 ratings.
5. [Research] Pattern 1 — JWT/bearer token to a backend proxy (136 apps, 48%, "Limited exploitability"): tokens issued by Firebase Authentication, custom OAuth, or own backends could be captured and replayed with arbitrary prompts. Example: `authorization: Bearer eyJ...` to a third-party chatbot API with body `{"message":"Hello","bot_id":"..."}`.
6. [Research] Pattern 2 — unauthenticated backend proxy (92 apps, 33%, "Full"): key correctly moved server-side, but the endpoint has no access control, making an open relay. Example: `POST https://us-central1-[project].cloudfunctions.net/chatStream` with no Authorization header and a client-chosen `"model":"gpt-5.1"`. Case study: a Cloud Function `/openAIProxy` accepted a client-supplied model, system prompt and `max_tokens: 400`, returned HTTP 200 (189 tokens billed to the developer); still open 90 days after disclosure.
7. [Research] Pattern 3 — plaintext provider key sent directly from the app (54 apps, 19%, "Full"): e.g. `Authorization: Bearer sk-proj-...` plus `OpenAI-Organization` header to api.openai.com; Gemini keys as URL query parameters. 28 of these 54 also exposed the system prompt in the same request, so one interception gives both free inference and the app's prompt logic.
8. [Research] Providers/infrastructure (Table 1): direct-API leaks: OpenAI 42 apps, Google Gemini 7, others 11 (Baidu ERNIE 3, OpenRouter 2, DeepSeek, Mistral, Poe, Zhipu AI, DeepAI, Writesonic 1 each). Cloud-platform backends 67 apps: Firebase 27 (20 JWT, 7 no-auth), Google Cloud Run 11, AWS 5 (all no-auth), others 24 (Supabase 7, Azure 3, Heroku, Vercel, Railway, Fly.io, RapidAPI, App Engine 2 each). Custom backends 155 apps (92 JWT, 63 no-auth). Anthropic does not appear among the listed leaked providers.
9. [Research] Client-side anti-interception (RQ3): 143 of 444 apps (32%) used at least one. HTTP proxy bypass alone (59 apps) was bypassed 81% of the time via the VPN fallback; custom encryption 15% (2/13); network-request failure on detection 45%; packet-capture resistance, anti-detection, WebSocket-only 0%. Combined mechanisms (43 apps) had 7% bypass vs 55% for single; 85 of 143 protected apps resisted all interception, so prevalence is a lower bound.
10. [Research] Remediation (RQ4), re-test 90 days after disclosure via App Store contact channels: 78 of 282 (28%) remediated (HTTP 401/403): 15 revoked plaintext keys (12 OpenAI, 3 Gemini), 12 added auth to open proxies, 51 now reject expired JWTs. 83 (29%) unreachable/delisted; 9 rate-limited (429); 66 (23%) still exploitable. Abstract frames it as "only 28% … remediated, while 72% remained exploitable" — the body's numbers show 23% confirmed still exploitable, the rest unreachable/errors.
11. [Research] Still-exploitable root causes: 31 of 36 unremediated full cases were unauthenticated proxies (needs an architecture change, not a key swap); 30 JWT apps remained exploitable: 13 static/opaque tokens with no expiry, 6 JWT lifetimes of 100 days or more (one `exp` set to year 2125 = 36,525 days), 5 servers accepting expired JWTs, 5 JWTs with no `exp` claim, 1 user-session JWT.
12. [Research] Case study: an app using Firebase anonymous auth (1-hour JWTs) through a Zuplo API gateway to OpenAI accepted a token 128 days past expiry and returned HTTP 200; response headers also exposed the upstream OpenAI organization and project IDs. Lesson: short TTLs mean nothing if the proxy does not verify `exp` and signature.
13. [Research] Developer recommendations (authors): route all LLM calls through authenticated server-side proxies; enforce access control on the backend endpoint itself; revoke leaked keys immediately. Providers should label client-side integration insecure, publish reference authenticated-proxy implementations, and add anomaly detection that flags a key used from many distinct clients. Apple should add dynamic credential-leak analysis to App Store review. Also: prefer defense-in-depth over single client-side tricks.
14. [Research] Background claim: provider API keys (e.g. `sk-proj-...`) are long-lived bearer tokens that do not expire until revoked; cites a Sysdig "LLMjacking" report of potential losses over $46,000/day from stolen LLM keys (secondary citation, not verified here).
15. [Inferred] For this Expo app: the iOS binary's FairPlay encryption is no protection, because a MITM proxy with a user-installed CA sees any key or token the app sends. The Claude key must live only on the server, and the server must also (a) authenticate every request with a properly verified short-lived token, (b) fix model, system prompt and `max_tokens` server-side rather than accepting them from the client, and (c) rate-limit per user. Pattern 2 (client picks model and system prompt) is exactly the open-relay design to avoid.
16. [Inferred] Anonymous sign-in (e.g. Firebase anonymous auth) only identifies a device; it does not stop a scripted attacker from minting tokens. Pair it with per-token quotas, App Attest/App Check-style attestation, and spend caps.

## Limitations or bias
- Preprint, not stated as peer-reviewed. iOS only, US App Store only, free apps with reachable LLM features, snapshot from Oct 2025. Keyword search may miss apps.
- The measurement is a lower bound (59% of defended apps resisted interception), and it only sees traffic, not on-device storage or server-side bugs.
- There is a small internal inconsistency: the abstract says "72% remained exploitable", while Table 3 shows 66/282 still returning HTTP 200 and many unreachable. Also, "30% of protected apps" vs "around 10% of evaluated apps" use combined defenses (43/143 vs 43/444; both consistent).
- Client-side "defenses" like custom encryption only slow interception; the paper measures resistance to its pipeline, not to a determined reverse engineer.
- The paper's own listing exposes an upstream org identifier; it is not repeated here.

## Summary-only items
- None. The full text was read. The cited Sysdig $46,000/day figure was not opened (secondary citation).

## Best used for
- Hard numbers on how mobile LLM apps leak credentials, and why a server proxy without authentication or with broken JWT handling is still exploitable. A checklist of JWT failure modes (no `exp`, 100-year `exp`, expired tokens accepted, static tokens). Evidence that client-side obfuscation and proxy-bypass are weak. Remediation persistence data.
