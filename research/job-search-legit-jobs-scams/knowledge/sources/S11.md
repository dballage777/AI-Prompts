## Learned points
1. How it works: a stranger sends a check, often for more than owed, and asks you to send some money back or on to someone else. Stated reasons include taxes, fees, supplies, or an overpayment refund. The check is fake and the money you send is lost. — [Documented] (summary-only)
2. Fake checks can look like personal or business checks, cashier's checks, money orders, or electronic checks. They often carry real bank names, and some use stolen account information. — [Documented] (summary-only)
3. Job-related variants the FTC names include mystery shopping (evaluate a store that sells gift cards, money orders, or wires, then wire money on), personal assistant (buy gift cards with check money and send PINs to the "boss"), and car-wrap decals (pay "installers" who don't exist). Prize and overpayment variants are also listed. — [Documented] (summary-only)
4. Banks must make deposited funds available quickly, but it can take weeks to discover a check is fake. By then you may owe the bank the money. Funds showing in the account do not prove the check is good. — [Documented]
5. If money was already sent, the FTC advises acting by payment method: gift cards (contact the issuer right away), wire transfers (call the wire company and ask for a reversal, though success is unlikely), money orders (ask the issuer to stop payment), and cryptocurrency (ask the company used to reverse the transaction). — [Documented] (summary-only)
6. Report fake checks to the FTC (ReportFraud.ftc.gov), the U.S. Postal Inspection Service, and the state Attorney General. — [Documented] (summary-only)
7. Hub excerpt: a new employer who mails a first paycheck before any work begins is described as "your cue to stop." The check builds trust before the scammer takes your money. — [Documented] (summary-only)
8. Scammers take outdated ads from real employers, alter them, and repost them on job sites such as Indeed and LinkedIn to collect personal information (May 2023 alert). — [Documented] (summary-only)
9. Red flag: a work-from-home "employer" asks for Social Security and bank account numbers to "deposit salary," or sends a check to buy equipment and asks you to send money back. — [Documented] (summary-only)
10. Verification steps: confirm the opening on the company's official website (careers or jobs section) before applying, and search the company or person's name with "scam," "review," or "complaint." — [Documented] (summary-only)
11. College-student alert (2025): watch for fake personal-assistant ads, unexpected recruiter texts that ask for financial information or payment for starter kits, training, or certifications, and emails that look like they come from a professor or college office and include a check. — [Documented] (summary-only)
12. Contact the professor or office directly. Do not use the contact details in the suspicious message. — [Documented] (summary-only)
13. Absolute rules the FTC states: never pay anyone to get a job, and depositing a check and sending part of it back is always a scam. — [Documented] (summary-only)
14. The FTC's general job-scams article (consumer.ftc.gov/articles/job-scams, also ftc.gov/jobscams) is cross-linked. It was not opened here. — [Documented]
15. Other job-related fake-check alerts listed on the hub include childcare providers (June 2026), mystery shopping (April 2024), caregiving jobs (March 2024), military spouses (July 2023), and "You got the job!" (April 2023). They were not opened. — [Documented]
16. For the bot: any posting or "employer" that sends a check or money before work, or asks for money back, should be flagged as a scam outright, not just as a possible risk. — [Inferred]
