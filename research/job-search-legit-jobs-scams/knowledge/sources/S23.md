## Learned points
1. UW groups red flags into four kinds: Financial Requirements, Personal Information, Communication, and the Job Description/Offer. — Documented
2. Financial red flags: being asked to send money to apply or be hired; give card or bank numbers; cash checks or wire or transfer money; use your bank account for deposits; buy a computer or equipment from a specific website with your own money; or buy and send gift cards. — Documented
3. Personal-information red flags: being asked for copies of personal documents or for your Social Security Number. — Documented
4. Communication red flags: contact by email, text, phone or social media; being asked to switch from your school address to a generic one (Gmail, Outlook, Hotmail); offers that come from a trusted domain (even @uw.edu) or from spoofed addresses of a company's real HR staff; and personalized, professional-seeming texts or calls. Because of this, a legitimate-looking sender domain alone does not prove an offer is real. — Documented
5. Offer red flags: a large salary for little work, poor spelling or grammar, and unsolicited email offers for remote work, which call for more research. UW stresses that a legitimate-looking offer letter and onboarding schedule "does not guarantee legitimacy". — Documented
6. UW passes on FTC steps to take before accepting, and especially before paying. Search the company or person's name with "scam", "review" or "complaint". Research the company on sites such as the Better Business Bureau. Describe the offer to someone you trust, which also buys time. — Documented
7. Neither legitimate employers nor the federal government will ask you to pay to get a job. A check that comes with instructions to send part on or buy gift cards is a fake-check scam. The check bounces and the bank wants the money back. — Documented
8. If you shared personal or financial information: contact police right away (campus police or local non-emergency line). Contact your bank or card company to close the account and dispute charges. File with IC3 or the FTC if the incident happened entirely online. — Documented
9. Institution-specific reporting: report fraudulent Handshake postings to the career center, and send phishing emails, as attachments, to UW-IT. The bot-relevant point is that a school-run job board has its own reporting channel. — Documented
10. The page links to Handshake safety articles (spotting and reporting scams, protecting personal information, flagging an employer, blocking a user) and to identity-theft resources (Privacy Rights Clearinghouse, Identity Theft Resource Center, FTC identity theft). These were not opened. — Documented
11. Disclaimer: the career center reviews Handshake postings for legitimacy. It still says students are ultimately responsible for their own safety and the university is not liable for losses. — Documented
12. For an AI assistant: even curated boards (a university Handshake board) and real-looking domains can carry scams. The assistant should never call a posting "verified" because of where it appeared. It should tell students to check with their career office. — Inferred
