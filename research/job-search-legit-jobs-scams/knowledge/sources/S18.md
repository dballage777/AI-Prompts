## Learned points
1. Purpose: "Protect your community by reporting fraud, scams, and bad business practices." Users can report a scam, a company, or an unwanted call. — [Documented]
2. How it works, in three steps: tell us what happened; get your next steps (what you can do to protect yourself); help stop fraud ("We use and share reports with our law enforcement partners to help with investigations"). — [Documented]
3. The FTC does not resolve individual reports. It uses them to investigate and bring cases, spot trends, educate the public, and share community data. A bot must not imply that reporting will get the user's money back. — [Documented; last sentence Inferred]
4. Data sharing: reports go into the Consumer Sentinel database, which is available to federal, state, and local law enforcement. Home-page text says reports are shared "with over 2,000 law enforcers." The Privacy Act text says Sentinel is used by civil and criminal authorities "worldwide." — [Documented]
5. Privacy Act notice: how much personal information you give "is up to you." The FTC may share it with the subject of the report, other law enforcement or organizations, courts, or for other authorized routine uses. — [Documented]
6. The comment box warns users not to include sensitive data ("SSN, DOB, driver's license numbers, account numbers, medical history"). It asks them to describe what happened in their own words and to paste the scam email or text if they have one. — [Documented]
7. Report form fields include how it started or how they first contacted you, contact method, whether you were asked to pay, amount asked and paid, payment method (including gift card or reload card type and cryptocurrency name), date fraud began, and details about the company or person. A bot can help users gather these facts before filing. — [Documented; last sentence Inferred]
8. FBI IC3 referral: for money transfers, the form asks whether the report can be referred to the FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center. "If you report it quickly, the FBI may be able to help you recover your money." The FBI needs each transaction's date and amount and the bank details. The user can decline and still file with the FTC. — [Documented]
9. The form also asks how much money was sent "in the last 5 days," which signals that recent transfers matter for recovery. — [Documented; interpretation Inferred]
10. An email address is needed only if the user wants a confirmation email. After filing, the user gets a report number, but "The report will not be available after you exit this page," so they should save or print it. — [Documented]
11. Next steps after filing: see FTC refund cases at ftc.gov/refunds, explore local data at ftc.gov/exploredata, and sign up for alerts at ftc.gov/ConsumerAlerts. — [Documented]
12. Impersonation warning on the site: the FTC will never threaten you, say you must transfer money to "protect it," or tell you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it over. — [Documented]
13. Robocall reports are forwarded to the National Do Not Call Registry (DoNotCall.gov). The site also links IdentityTheft.gov for identity theft and TakeItDown.ftc.gov. — [Documented]
14. Job-scam category labels and FAQ answers were not retrievable (they load from a backend). No job-scam-specific text from this source is captured here. — [Documented limitation]
