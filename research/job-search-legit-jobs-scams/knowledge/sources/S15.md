## Learned points
1. North Korea places thousands of state-controlled remote "IT Workers" (ITWs) in western companies. They increasingly use paid facilitators to evade detection. Revenue funds the regime, including its weapons programs, and the North Korean government receives an estimated 85–90% of the revenue. — [Documented]
2. Job-seeker risk: ITWs "strategically target those under financial stress." They recruit Americans, sometimes from economically vulnerable groups, to act as facilitators, often without the person knowing who is behind it. — [Documented]
3. Facilitator tasks ITWs pay for: appearing on camera, sitting job interviews or meetings, verifying fraudulent identities, lending personal social or professional networking accounts, shipping packages, completing urinalysis or fingerprint submissions, and hosting employer laptops at home ("laptop farms"). The FBI states these actions are illegal. — [Documented]
4. Lure format: ITWs post fake job openings on well-known job boards, often as freelance gigs with simple requests such as interviewing on someone's behalf or receiving and hosting laptops. — [Documented]
5. Cover stories: fake companies and official-looking documents. Common backstories are Chinese or East Asian nationals needing a temporary US presence, or Eastern Europeans displaced by the Russia-Ukraine war. — [Documented]
6. FBI advice to individuals: reject any request to provide personal information, financial information, or personal account logins in exchange for payment. If threatened, call 911. — [Documented]
7. Escalation: ITWs have used threats of violence and death threats (including against former managers' families), extortion with stolen corporate data, reputational attacks, and in-person intimidation through US-based facilitators. — [Documented]
8. Identity abuse: most names and addresses ITWs give employers are stolen. They pay facilitators for their identities, or use those identities beyond what was agreed. They buy identity-verification accounts to pre-test stolen PII against background checks. In isolated cases they have extorted the identity owners. — [Documented]
9. ITWs also subcontract work and job applications to freelancers in Pakistan, India, Nigeria, or neighboring countries. They target cleared or government-contract roles and active-duty military members to host laptops. They falsely claim ownership of legitimate professional networking accounts. — [Documented]
10. Employer-side signals (no single indicator is reliable): remote desktop tools (AnyDesk, TeamViewer, RustDesk, Chrome Remote Desktop, Splashtop, RMM), KVM-over-IP hardware (TinyPilot, PiKVM, GL.iNet), OBS Studio with VDO.Ninja, high turnover of bank accounts, addresses, and social accounts, and forged ID documents. The FBI recommends "identity in depth," which means E-Verify, a US passport, multiple IDs, and live verification. — [Documented]
11. The FBI warns aggressive filtering (ban lists, name filters) risks false positives that hurt legitimate applicants and identity-theft victims. — [Documented]
12. Reporting: contact the local FBI Field Office and file at www.ic3.gov. Include street addresses that received devices, identifiers (names, accounts, phone numbers, emails, bank accounts, IPs), screenshots and messages, and any facilitator details. — [Documented]
13. For the bot: warn users that a "job" to host laptops, interview for someone else, lend accounts or identity, or receive and reship packages may be illegal facilitation, not a job. Point them to IC3. Do not profile applicants by nationality. — [Inferred]

## Limitations / bias
- The PSA is mostly employer- and security-oriented. Job-seeker guidance is brief.
- It reflects national-security framing. Nationality-based backstories are reported tactics, not screening criteria. The FBI itself warns against name or ban-list filtering.
## Best for
- Warning users that "easy" remote gigs (hosting laptops, proxy interviews, renting accounts or identity) can be illegal facilitation schemes, and giving them FBI/IC3 reporting steps.
