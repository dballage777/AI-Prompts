## Learned points
1. Scope: in 2025, BBB Scam Tracker published more than 146,000 scam reports from the US and Canada. They are sorted into 32 scam types plus "other" (6.1%). Each report carries a description, the dollar loss, and the contact and payment methods. Demographics are optional. — Research
2. The BBB Risk Index is Exposure × Susceptibility × (Median Loss / Overall Median Loss) × 1,000. Exposure is the share of all reports, susceptibility is the share of reports with a dollar loss, and median loss counts only reports that had a loss. — Documented
3. Employment scams were the No. 2 riskiest scam type in 2025, as in 2024 and every year since 2022. They trailed only investment/cryptocurrency scams. — Research
4. Employment-scam figures, 2025 vs 2024: Risk Index 227.4; exposure 15.8% (up from 14.4%); susceptibility 13.3% (down from 17.2%); median loss $1,000 (down from $1,500). — Research
5. Overall 2025 baseline: susceptibility 39.8% (44.4% in 2024), median loss $93 ($130 in 2024). Employment scams draw many reports with a lower rate of loss than average. When a victim does lose money, the median loss is about 10× the overall median. — Research (ratio is Inferred from the two medians)
6. Age: employment scams were the No. 1 riskiest type for ages 18–24 and 25–34 and No. 2 for ages 35–64. For 65+, romance/friendship ranked No. 2 instead. — Research
7. Contact methods: text messages (51.4%) and email (25.7%) were the most common ways employment scammers made contact. Employment was the most-reported scam type for text messages and internet messaging. It ranked No. 2 for online classifieds and No. 3 for websites, social media and email. — Research
8. Payment: cryptocurrency was the most-reported payment method in employment scams (35.1% of cases). Employment was also the No. 1 scam type among all crypto-payment reports in 2025, ahead of investment scams. It ranked No. 2 for check and cash payments and No. 3 for online payment systems and wire transfers. — Research
9. Tactic mix from BBB's survey research: fake applications that harvest personal information made up 32.4% of employment scams. "Task" scams, in which victims are hired for online marketing or product reviews, paid at first, then told to deposit money or pay fees or "taxes" to withdraw earnings (often in group chats with fake coworkers), made up 25.5%. Fake-check overpayment for training or equipment made up 20.4%. — Research
10. Motivation: 55.1% of survey respondents said flexibility to work from home was the main reason they engaged with the job scammer. Next came higher pay (15.9%), better job focus (5.4%) and flexible hours (5.1%). — Research
11. People who were employed and content but looking for extra income, or open to new opportunities, were more likely to report losing money than active job seekers (Figure 6). — Research
12. 16.4% of survey respondents said the scammer asked them to switch communication channels. BBB explains that scammers steer victims away from platforms that might detect or warn about the scam. — Research
13. Amazon impersonation was used mostly for employment scams (59.4% of Amazon-impersonation reports). These came mainly by text (56%). — Research
14. AI: reports with AI-related references increased. They included employment scams for jobs that supposedly required applicants to "train AI systems". BBB says its keyword method may produce false positives and may miss cases. — Research
15. BBB's job-scam prevention tips: be wary of offers that seem too good to be true. Legitimate offers have no up-front fees. Look for the listing on the company's own website. Beware of offers made too quickly or without an interview. Be wary of being asked to deposit a check and transfer funds. Be wary of vague job descriptions meant to fit anyone. Be especially careful with work-from-home shipping or warehouse, secret-shopper, and online-marketing jobs that pay through "their account". — Documented
16. BBB's glossary definition of an employment scam covers personal information given through a fake application, money paid for "training" or "equipment", fake-check overpayment, and the online-marketing variant where victims must put in their own money before they can collect pay. — Documented
17. A user-submitted case study (edited by BBB) describes a product-review task scam: daily "sets" of 40 reviews, random orders requiring $60–$300 deposits, a "mentor", and a final $3,000 demand. The victim deposited more than $1,800 in 5 days. — Reported
18. BBB estimates that Scam Tracker helped people avoid losing more than $38 million in 2025. The estimate rests on 3.2 million visitors and a January 2025 web-intercept survey (n=2,621) in which 41.3% of those checking a possible scam said it helped. — Research
19. For an AI assistant: give extra warning on remote, work-from-home and task-based "online marketing" jobs. Flag any request to pay in crypto, deposit a check, or move to text or WhatsApp. Younger adults (18–34) are the highest-risk group for this scam type. — Inferred

## Limitations / bias
- All data are self-reported to BBB Scam Tracker. BBB itself says exposure figures do not represent the population. People who report may differ from typical victims.
- Combines US and Canada in the main figures. A separate Canada section exists; employment ranked No. 3 there with 16.1% exposure.
- Tactic percentages come from a follow-up survey of reporters (n=2,820), not from all reports.
- Some chart values were garbled in text extraction, so only values that extracted cleanly are recorded.
- BBB is promoting its own tool, which may color the claims of impact ($38M avoided).
