## Learned points
1. Indeed names three scammer goals: steal identity or financial information, trick you into sending money, and recruit you into committing fraud without your knowledge. Scams appear on job boards, by email, and on social media and messaging apps. — Documented
2. Indeed lists seven scam types: check scams, money laundering, reshipping, fee scams, cryptocurrency scams, identity and ID.me account theft, and the unemployment-benefit or tax-identity fraud that follows. — Documented
3. Check scam pattern: a "personal assistant/caregiver" or "new office" job sends a check for supplies or equipment, or pays you up front, then asks you to send part of it on. When the check bounces, you owe the bank the full amount. — Documented
4. Money-laundering and reshipping pattern: you move money through Western Union, MoneyGram or crypto, make purchases for someone else, or reship packages with prepaid labels. Indeed names the decoy titles "merchandising manager" and "package processing assistant". It warns this may be illegal even when the money isn't yours. — Documented
5. Fee scams charge to apply, interview, use a recruiter, format a resume, train or onboard, or run a background check. Indeed's nuance: a real employer may charge for a uniform or tools, but it usually takes the cost from your paycheck after you start. — Documented
6. Identity-theft requests to watch for: ID.me username, password or codes; driver's license (front and back); passport; SSN; birth date. Indeed says to report unemployment-benefit or tax-identity fraud to the FTC. — Documented
7. To check an email address: real companies use their official domain. Watch for misspellings, extra characters, spoofed names (e.g., `info_@company.net` instead of `info@company.com`) and free addresses (Gmail, Yahoo). — Documented
8. "Bait and switch" check: make sure the job you're offered is the one you applied for. — Documented
9. Always have an in-person or video interview. Be careful with a job offered without an interview or handled only through chat apps. — Documented
10. Never pay to apply. Never send or receive money for an employer (wire transfers, cashing checks). Never accept money before doing any work. Never open accounts or post ads for an unknown company unless you work in hiring or staffing. — Documented
11. To verify a recruiter: visit the company's official careers page, look for a staff directory, search LinkedIn, and call the company's main number. Indeed says a recruiter who discourages you from verifying is a red flag. — Documented
12. To research a company: find its website, physical address and phone, verified social accounts, and recent news or reviews. Talk only through video, verified phone numbers, or official-domain email. — Documented
13. Don't share a driver's license, credit report, banking details or SSN until you have talked with the employer through verified channels. Indeed says real employers won't ask for sensitive financial information early in hiring. — Documented
14. Red flags for fake recruiters: asking you to pay for training, background checks or equipment; text-only or messaging-app interviews; an offer with no interview; generic greetings ("Dear applicant"); offers from free email services; pressure to accept right away; poor grammar or spelling; offers that sound too good to be true. — Documented
15. Self-protection: don't respond to suspicious messages, don't click links or attachments, and save all communications as evidence. — Documented
16. If you've been scammed: stop all contact, share no more information, and call local police or the cybercrime unit if you lost money, gave out sensitive information, or took part in something possibly illegal. Tell your bank or card company. Report to Indeed if the scam came through Indeed. File with IC3. Tell the real company if it was impersonated. Report unemployment-insurance fraud. Follow FTC identity-theft advice. Scan for viruses and change your passwords. — Documented
17. Scam-prone job titles: assistants (office, personal, administrative), receptionist or secretary, delivery driver or chauffeur, warehouse or forklift, and remote roles (customer service, data entry, survey taker, social media manager). — Documented
18. Seasonal scam windows: holiday retail (November–January), summer jobs (May–August; camp counselor, lifeguard, tutoring or childcare), and tax season (January–April; tax preparer, bookkeeping). — Documented
19. Indeed says some countries require employers to prove who they are before posting, and a dedicated team checks for fake posts. It also says no site can promise every job is real. — Documented
20. For an AI assistant: a posting being on a major job board does not make it legitimate. The assistant should tell users to verify independently on the employer's own site and phone line, and should never relay a request for payment or ID documents as normal. — Inferred

## Limitations / bias
- This is a vendor source. Indeed has an interest in presenting its platform as safe, and parts of the guidance point users to Indeed's own reporting tools.
- The pages give no dates and no statistics. Links to the FTC, USPIS and IC3 are mentioned but were not followed here.
- The "Fraudulent Job Policy" and "Spam vs scam" pages were not read.
## Best for
- A concrete, plain-language checklist of scam types, red flags, recruiter-verification steps and "I've been scammed" steps that a bot can turn into user warnings.
