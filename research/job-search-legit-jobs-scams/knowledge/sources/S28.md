## Learned points
1. Method: Censuswide surveyed 8,512 professionals (working full or part time, aged 18+) in the UK, USA, India, Germany, and Brazil, collected 16–30 March 2026. Censuswide is an MRS and British Polling Council member and follows MRS and ESOMAR codes. Results are not broken out by country, so there are no US-only figures. — [Research]
2. Some figures do not come from the survey. The 90% off-platform figure comes from LinkedIn platform data (January 2026). The 98.7% and 99.5% detection figures come from LinkedIn's Transparency Report (January–June 2025). Recruiter figures (*) cover respondents involved in recruiting. "Agree" figures combine strongly and somewhat agree. — [Research]
3. Context the report cites: Global Anti-Scam Alliance research says 70% of adults worldwide encounter scams yearly and 13% are exposed daily. It says FTC data shows job-offer losses reaching "hundreds of millions of dollars most years" (the report gives no precise FTC figure). — [Reported]
4. Legitimacy checks are now routine: 72% stop to think about whether a role is legitimate at least sometimes before applying (29% always). 57% are more likely to question whether a job is a scam than a year ago, versus 11% less likely. 49% of recruiters say candidates have contacted them to check whether a role is genuine. — [Research]
5. Trust signals job seekers rely on: company reputation (29%) and the platform where the job is found (28%). Checks people make: 57% search the company or role online, 47% check the company's own careers site, 32% review the company LinkedIn Page, and about 30% look for verification on the company, recruiter, or posting. — [Research]
6. Recruiters: 68% are taking steps to build candidate trust. 70% say verification of the job, recruiter, or Company Page is a must-have. 36% have been impersonated. 67% say scams make it harder to build trust with candidates. — [Research]
7. When people feel most at risk: 22% feel most worried about scams while browsing jobs, and 21% when they first get recruiter or company outreach. LinkedIn says trust is "won or lost" at first contact. — [Research]
8. Off-platform move is the main signal: 90% of reported scam messages involve moving to private messaging, and over half of off-platform attempts happen in the very first message (LinkedIn platform data). — [Research]
9. Red flags respondents know about: sensitive info asked early (59%), upfront payments or fees (56%), pressure to act quickly (45%), a recruiter profile or job that doesn't look legitimate (42%), and requests to move off-platform (38%). Only 38% recognize the off-platform flag, even though it appears in 90% of reported scams. — [Research] (the gap comparison is [Inferred])
10. Generational gap: 32% of Gen Z say they've been a scam victim, versus 17% of Gen X. 32% of Gen Z admit ignoring warning signs because opportunities feel scarce, versus 21% of Gen X and 8% of Baby Boomers. Gen Z (18–28) versus Gen X (45–60) who question red flags: upfront payment such as buying a laptop 40% vs 58%, pressure to decide quickly 34% vs 47%, a phone call without context 27% vs 37%, and sensitive info early 54% vs 59%. — [Research]
11. Scale of harm: 62% have seen a job they suspected was fraudulent, 21% have been a victim, and 30% report a close call. The most common consequences among those affected are wasted time (31%), stress and worry (27%), and reduced confidence and trust when applying (25%). Scams therefore carry an emotional cost a wellness-oriented bot should acknowledge. — [Research] (bot implication [Inferred])
12. LinkedIn's claimed defenses: 98.7% of detected spam and scam content is removed automatically before members see it, and 99.5% of detected fake accounts are stopped before being reported. It also uses verification badges (recruiter and Company Pages), "Verification on Jobs", required verification for high-risk job posters, reduced visibility of posts with suspicious links, junk-folder spam filtering, and two-factor authentication. — [Reported] (self-reported vendor claims)
13. Checklist for job seekers: (1) stay on-platform for early conversations. (2) Check identity: the company Page, the recruiter profile, whether the role is on the company's own careers site, and verification badges. (3) Pause at the "vulnerability moment"; pressure, vague details, or unexpected outreach are reasons to slow down. (4) Keep sensitive info private, since legitimate employers won't ask for sensitive info, ID documents, or upfront payments early. (5) Report via the three dots above a job posting. — [Documented]
14. Bot implication: a bot can recommend cross-checking any posting against the employer's own careers site. It can name pressure and scarcity as tactics, especially for younger users who say they ignore warnings because jobs feel scarce. It should calmly normalize pausing ("Real opportunities won't disappear..."). — [Inferred]

## Limitations / bias
- This is a vendor-commissioned survey and press release. It promotes LinkedIn's own products (verification, Job Match, Job Tracker, Hirer insights) and its "stay on-platform" message.
- The five-country sample is not broken out, so there are no US-specific numbers. No margin of error or weighting is reported. Victim and red-flag figures are self-reported.
- The 90% figure covers reported scams on LinkedIn only, not all job scams. Detection rates are self-reported transparency figures.
- The FTC loss figure is vague ("hundreds of millions"). Use FTC primary data instead.
- Only the first ~4 PDF pages were extracted. The PDF content matched the press page, with extra generational and trust-signal breakdowns.

## Best for
- Current (2026) survey numbers on how common job scams are, which red flags people miss, and the emotional cost of scams, to justify proactive, calm scam warnings and "check the employer's own careers site" advice in a bot.
