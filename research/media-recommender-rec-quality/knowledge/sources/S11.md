## Learned points
1. [Research] Problem statement: most deployed recommenders are black boxes labelled only "Recommended for you" or "Based on your profile". Users may suspect the list serves the seller's profit, and they can rarely correct wrong or outdated assumptions. The chapter's example is a gift purchase that the system treats as the buyer's own taste.
2. [Research] Nine explanation purposes, ordered by how much attention the literature gives them (Table 1, after Tintarev & Masthoff and Nunes & Jannach): Transparency, Effectiveness (good decisions), Trust, Persuasiveness, Satisfaction, Education, Scrutability (the user can tell the system it is wrong), Efficiency (faster decisions), Debugging.
3. [Research] In a review of more than 200 explanation papers, Nunes & Jannach found only 7 that focused on scrutability. The authors call this a major research gap. Many papers never state the explanation's purpose or never evaluate it systematically.
4. [Research] Four kinds of explanation content: (a) preferences and user inputs ("because you specified you prefer light-weight models"); (b) inference process (the general strategy, e.g., "similar users liked it"); (c) background knowledge (popularity in the community); (d) alternatives and their features (decisive attributes, pros and cons, where one item beats another). Natural-language text is the dominant format.
5. [Research] Explanations that name the input and the item features are the most useful entry point for user control. The user can correct a stated preference or critique an attribute. Explaining the general inference strategy is of "limited use" for control.
6. [Research] White-box explanations come from the model itself. Black-box (post-hoc) explanations are plausible justifications computed separately, for example from mined association rules or reviews, "even though the recommended item was selected in a different way." Matrix factorization and deep models make white-box explanations very hard.
7. [Research] Case study (Gedikli et al., movie domain, 105 participants, 10 explanation types, one per participant). Protocol: participants rated movies from the explanation alone, then rated the same movies again (not told they were the same) after seeing full details and a trailer. A small gap between the two ratings means high effectiveness. A large gap means persuasion.
8. [Research] Findings: content- or feature-based tag-cloud explanations took longer to read but were among the most effective (explanation-based ratings were close to informed ratings). Non-personalized "N users rated this 4+" explanations produced the strongest over-estimation, i.e., positive persuasion. Personalized tag clouds scored highest on transparency and satisfaction. Explanations built on peers' rating behavior scored lowest on satisfaction. A rating prediction with a confidence value gave low perceived transparency.
9. [Research] Design guidelines (Table 3): (1) use domain-specific content data to boost effectiveness; (2) use explanation concepts users already know, which take less effort; (3) raise transparency to raise satisfaction; (4) do not optimize mainly for speed, because users will spend time on explanations that help them decide well.
10. [Research] Framework for user control. During preference elicitation: static preference forms and dialogs (e.g., genre pickers, style quizzes), conversational recommenders, and critiquing ("cheaper", "closer"). During presentation: filtering, sorting, or removing items; choosing or weighting the recommendation strategy; and interactive explanations.
11. [Research] Static forms are easy to understand, but most users do not keep them up to date as their interests drift. Conversational systems adapt their questions and engage users more. Their cost is maintaining explicit knowledge, and they usually serve one-shot sessions without a long-term user model.
12. [Research] Critiquing gives immediate feedback against a reference item but can need many steps. Compound critiques (several dimensions at once) cut the number of steps but raise cognitive load.
13. [Research] Letting users switch algorithms (a MovieLens widget with 4 strategies) or weight hybrid strategies (with a Venn diagram showing which strategy contributed each item) improved user experience in studies. The risk is overwhelming average users, so these controls are mostly academic.
14. [Research] Real-world examples. Amazon explains an item by naming past items the user interacted with and offers "already own", "not interested", and "don't use for recommendations" (useful for gifts). YouTube likewise lets users exclude a watched video from the profile. Shopr shows "because you currently like blue", and the user can click "blue" to revise that preference.
15. [Research] Amazon control-feature survey: over 90% knew they could influence recommendations somehow, only 20% knew the dedicated page, and only about 8% had used it. About 75% found "Don't use for recommendation" clear or guessable. Reasons for not using it: about one third were not interested in recommendations, about one quarter said it was too much effort, about one quarter feared bad or irreversible profile changes, and 19% cited privacy.
16. [Research] Remedies the authors suggest: explain the effect and benefit of control features, add an "undo", and personalize explanation and control complexity to the user's expertise. Users also need incentives to invest effort.
17. [Inferred] For a prompt-based show/movie/anime recommender, every "because you liked X" line should name the specific input title and the shared feature (tone, theme, pacing), not a generic strategy, and the user should be able to say "don't use X" or "I'm no longer into Y". The tool should confirm what changed and offer undo.
18. [Inferred] Avoid persuasive popularity-only justifications ("highly rated by many"), because they inflate expected enjoyment. Prefer feature-level reasons that let the user predict their own reaction accurately.
19. [Inferred] Keep the taste profile editable and visible, and re-ask about drift occasionally, because users rarely maintain profiles by hand.

## Limitations or bias
- 2019 chapter that predates LLM recommenders. Most examples are e-commerce. The case study is a single lab study (105 participants, movies).
- Survey percentages come from cited work [32]; the chapter gives no sample size for that survey.
- The authors review their own prior work (Nunes & Jannach; Jannach et al.), which is a possible selection bias.

- None (full text read). Details of the cited studies [5], [21], [32], [52] come only from the chapter's summaries.
