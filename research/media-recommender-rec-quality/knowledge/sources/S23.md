## Learned points
1. [Reported] The guidelines come from NN/g user research on what people expect from recommendations as personalized content. Participants valued personalized suggestions because they "helped them avoid information overload". The article gives no sample size or method details.
2. [Reported] Guideline 1, prioritize individualized recommendations: personalized content is preferred over generic content but goes unused if placed low on the page. Sephora's "Recommended For You" sat second to last on the homepage, and a participant said they would "probably miss it." Eventbrite placed "Events for you" right under the banner and search area, and users found it easily.
3. [Reported] Guideline 2, clearly state the source of data: name the specific data used (for example, the item the user watched). This adds credibility, makes the personalization obvious, and helps users decide whether a row suits them.
4. [Reported] A Netflix participant used "because you watched X" rows as a mood selector. Remembering that X was "nice and chill and funny" or "very intense, lots of drama" let them choose the row that matched their current mood. The reason given for a recommendation works as a browsing aid, not only as a justification.
5. [Reported] At minimum, state the general source ("Based on Your Past History", "Related to Items You've Purchased"). Avoid vague add-ons like "and more": Amazon Video's "Based on titles you have watched and more" left users wondering what "more" covered.
6. [Reported] Guideline 3, separate categories of recommendations: users preferred specific groupings, such as Spotify's genre-based Daily Mixes, over a broad mixed list. One participant found Discover Weekly's songs "too diverse." When suggestions are too varied (users with many interests), people engage less.
7. [Reported] Mixed contexts hurt relevance. Eventbrite mixed work and leisure events, and Amazon Kindle suggestions became "too unwieldy" across genres. One participant noted that a single star rating cannot express "good technical book" versus "fun read", so one rating scale mixes different purposes.
8. [Reported] Guideline 4, allow users to fine-tune suggestions: provide positive and negative feedback and a way to edit history. Examples are Amazon's "View or edit past browsing history" and removing atypical items from Netflix viewing history (such as gifts, or someone else watching on your profile). Many users will not bother, but loyal or frequent users will, and missing negative feedback frustrated a Spotify user ("You're helpless in the face of songs that you don't like").
9. [Reported] Clear confirmation text helps. Netflix's message that a removed item will no longer be used for recommendations was appreciated.
10. [Reported] Guideline 5, update recommendations quickly and often: users expect feedback, especially negative feedback, to show up immediately. A Hulu user gave a reality show 1 star and expected both that show and reality shows in general to disappear. When nothing changed on refresh, he assumed the system needed "thinking" time. Fast updates (Eventbrite) impressed users: "it shows the software is learning from you."
11. [Reported] Users generalize negative feedback from one item to its category (one disliked reality show means fewer reality shows), so the system should treat negative feedback as feedback about attributes, not only that one item.
12. [Inferred] For a prompt-based show/movie/anime recommender, every pick should carry a specific "because you liked X" anchor and a short note on what kind of experience it is (mood or tone), so the user can choose by current mood. Picks should be grouped into a few clearly labeled lanes (for example by mood or genre) rather than one mixed list.
13. [Inferred] Keep a persistent profile the user can edit: likes, dislikes with a reason, and "ignore this, someone else watched it". Apply negative feedback immediately in the next turn, and widen it to the attribute level ("fewer reality shows") with confirmation.

## Limitations or bias
- Qualitative usability study with no participant count, tasks or method in the article. The evidence is quotes and screenshots of particular sites.
- Dated (2018), before LLM or conversational recommenders. It covers commerce and streaming UIs (carousels), not chat.
- Its point that lists can be "too diverse" is about presentation and grouping. It conflicts on the surface with the diversity-is-good literature: diversity across labeled groups works better than diversity mixed into one list.

## Best used for
- User-facing presentation rules for recommendations: specific reasons ("because you watched X"), grouping into labeled categories, editable history, and fast, generalized handling of negative feedback.
