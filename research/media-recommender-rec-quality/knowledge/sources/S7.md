## Learned points
1. [Research] Definition: a CRS is "a software system that supports its users in achieving recommendation-related goals through a multi-turn dialogue." Two features define it: task orientation, and multi-turn interaction with some form of dialogue state tracking. One-shot Q&A recommenders (for example, asking Siri for a restaurant) are explicitly excluded.
2. [Research] The motivation is that the one-shot "ranked list from past behaviour" paradigm fails in three cases: past behaviour is missing or unreliable, needs depend on context, and users "construct their preferences only during the decision process" while learning about the options. CRS can elicit current preferences, explain suggestions, and take feedback.
3. [Research] Conceptual architecture: input processing (intent detection, named entity recognition), a dialogue management system (state tracker), a user modeling system, a recommendation and reasoning engine, output generation, and knowledge elements (an item database plus domain, background, and dialogue knowledge).
4. [Research] The history goes back to Rich's late-1970s computerized librarian that asked about personality and preferences in natural language. Critiquing (for example, "less $$") dates back to 1982 query reformulation work. In form-based approaches, actions are unambiguous but can feel constraining. In NLP approaches, users have freedom but face recognition errors.
5. [Research] Initiative: almost every system in the literature is mixed-initiative. Purely user-driven systems ("user asks, system responds") were not found. Fully system-driven NLP dialogues ("system asks, user responds") risk disappointing users who want to ask clarification or explanation questions. One cited study found that a mix of natural language and buttons gave the best user experience.
6. [Research] Table 1 lists domain-independent user intents in typical dialogue order: Initiate Conversation, Chit-chat, Provide Preferences, Revise Preferences, Ask for Recommendation, Obtain Explanation, Obtain Details, Feedback on Recommendation, Restart, Accept Recommendation, Quit. The authors offer it as a checklist for spotting gaps in a design. Only 11 papers discussed intents explicitly.
7. [Research] Numbers on intent frequency from cited studies: the restart intent appeared in 5.2% of dialogues in one study, and 36.4% of users had it in another. Nearly 80% of utterances were chit-chat in one study, and chit-chat reduced dissatisfaction. 41.1% of users of a voice movie recommender tried to refine their initially stated preferences. Reject/restate happened in only 1.5% of interactions in another study.
8. [Research] User modeling uses either item-level preferences (ratings, likes and dislikes) or facet-level preferences (for example, genre), often via "slot filling," sometimes with a strength (must vs wish). Some systems combine short-term (session) and long-term profiles. How to weight the two is an open problem, and context such as season, location, or time of day is suggested as a factor. For cold start, a common strategy is to recommend popular items and use the feedback to refine the model.
9. [Research] Four main system actions: Request, Recommend, Explain, Respond. For eliciting feedback on items, informative items may be popular or diverse items in cold start, or "itemsets that represent a balance of popularity and diversity."
10. [Research] Explanations "can increase the system's perceived transparency, user trust and satisfaction" and help users decide faster and better. Even so, little CRS-specific explanation research exists. Examples: critiquing lists labelled by trade-off ("cheaper but heavier"), pros/cons descriptions tailored to feedback from the conversation, graph-path personalized explanations for movies, and social explanation strategies mined from human–human movie dialogues.
11. [Research] Handling failed or overloaded queries: query relaxation removes constraints when there are no results, and "query tightening" adds constraints to avoid choice overload. Some systems relax automatically and tell the user what they did. These techniques are under-explored in NLP-based CRS.
12. [Research] Evaluation dimensions: (1) effectiveness of task support (accuracy, acceptance/rejection, choice satisfaction), (2) efficiency (number of turns; shorter is usually better), (3) quality of conversation and usability (fluency, understandability), and (4) subtask effectiveness (intent recognition). Field tests and A/B tests are rare. BLEU can correlate poorly with user perceptions, so subjective evaluation is needed.
13. [Research] Simulated-user offline evaluations assume users have fixed preferences in advance, which is unrealistic because preferences are constructed during the dialogue. The authors argue offline evaluation alone is "too limited."
14. [Research] Open questions in the outlook: which modality suits which task; "recommendation summarization" for voice; group recommendation; using conversation-analysis theory; what makes a CRS truly helpful and what makes it fail; user tolerance for misunderstandings; and combining learning with structured knowledge for more reliable, predictable systems. The survey predates LLM chat assistants.
15. [Inferred] For a prompt-based show/anime recommender, Table 1 works as a design checklist. The prompt should support revising preferences, asking "why this?", getting details, giving feedback ("seen it" / "not for me"), "show more," and restart. It should take mixed initiative: ask a short clarifying question when the request is thin, but don't force a long interview.
16. [Inferred] Keep a persistent long-term taste profile (a file in the repo) separate from the session's current mood or constraints, and state which takes priority. When the constraints return nothing good, relax them openly and say so ("No 2024 anime fits all three; dropping the 12-episode limit gives...").

## Limitations or bias
- Literature up to about 2020. It predates LLM chat assistants, so the technical approaches (slot filling, critiquing, RNN response generation) are dated, though the intent and evaluation frameworks still apply.
- The intent percentages come from individual small studies cited second-hand, with different domains and setups, so they are not comparable or general.
- Semi-systematic search. The authors' own work (critiquing; Chen and Pu) is well represented.

## Summary-only items
- Section 3.1–3.2 (modalities, application environments), 4.4–4.5 (background knowledge and dataset tables), and the detailed objective/subjective measures in 6.2 were only skimmed. The dataset descriptions noted (ReDial: more than 10,000 AMT dialogues; CCPE-M: more than 500 dialogues with more than 10,000 preference statements) come from the table text.

## Best used for
- A definition of CRS, a reference architecture, the canonical list of user intents to support, the four system actions (Request, Recommend, Explain, Respond), query relaxation/tightening, and the CRS evaluation dimensions (effectiveness, efficiency, conversation quality).
