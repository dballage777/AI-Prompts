## Learned points
1. [Research] Explainable recommendation answers "why" an item is recommended. Evaluating it is harder than other tasks because there is no reliable ground truth and human feelings are hard to approximate.
2. [Research] Four evaluation perspectives (Table 1). Effectiveness: do explanations help users decide more accurately or faster, or make them more satisfied? Effectiveness is used broadly to cover efficiency, satisfaction, and trust, and it serves users. Transparency: do explanations reveal how the model works? This serves model designers. Persuasiveness: do explanations raise click or purchase rates? This serves providers. Scrutability: do explanations correspond exactly to the results, so that changing the explanation changes the list? This serves model designers.
3. [Research] Persuasiveness does not require honesty about the model. Persuasive explanations "have to highlight the features which can promote sales." The perspectives overlap and can conflict, so designers must decide in advance which to prioritize and what to sacrifice.
4. [Research] Note the definition difference: here scrutability means that explanations match outputs (and is model-agnostic). In S11 it means the user can tell the system it is wrong.
5. [Research] Four evaluation methods (Table 2). Case studies: intuitive, but biased (cherry-picked) and unable to compare models. Quantitative metrics: efficient and easy to benchmark, but they may drift from the explanation goals. Crowdsourcing: based on real human judgments and able to cover all four perspectives, but costly. Online experiments: highly reliable, but costly and they disturb production.
6. [Research] Quantitative metrics when user reviews are the ground truth for generated explanations: BLEU and ROUGE (n-gram overlap); Unique Sentence Ratio, Feature Coverage Ratio, and Feature Diversity (to detect repetitive, templated outputs); feature-level precision, recall, and F1 against the features in real reviews; and Feature Matching Ratio (does the requested feature appear?).
7. [Research] Counterfactual faithfulness metrics. Probability of Necessity: if the explanation features are removed, does the item drop out of the list? Probability of Sufficiency: if only those features are kept, does the item stay? Performance Shift: how much accuracy changes when the key history items cited as explanations are removed. Mean Explainability Precision and Recall measure how many recommended items can be explained from the interaction graph.
8. [Research] The weakness of overlap metrics: BLEU "only evaluates the word-level overlaps" and does not tell whether an explanation is reasonable. Heuristic metrics designed by a few people may mislead.
9. [Research] Crowdsourcing designs. With public datasets: annotators judge model outputs, quality is controlled by voting (3 annotators, majority) or Cohen's weighted κ. Question types are model-agnostic (pick the ideal explanation, reusable), single-model (up/down votes, fluency or usefulness, satisfaction), and pair-model (A vs. B: which helps you understand or decide). Injected annotator data: 20 annotators each wrote 15 or more Yelp reviews, so their judgments are real-user feedback. Fully constructed datasets: e.g., 286 annotators (76.3% male, 48.4% PhDs), 20, 152, 240, and 622 annotators across the cited studies.
10. [Research] Online experiments split users into three groups (proposed explanations, baseline explanations, no explanations) and compare CTR (Zhang et al. 2014), or compare conversion and revenue before and after explanations are shown.
11. [Research] Guidelines: use more reliable (human or online) evaluation for high-stakes domains; trade off method costs under a limited budget; combine methods, e.g., case studies early and quantitative metrics once the model is tuned.
12. [Research] Outlook: learn metric functions from a small amount of human-labelled data; build large benchmark datasets with ground truth for effectiveness and persuasiveness; develop task-agnostic evaluation, on the premise that intrinsic preferences are stable across tasks.
13. [Inferred] For an LLM recommender, "because you liked X" should be checked for faithfulness, i.e., a necessity test: if X were absent from the profile, would the pick still be made? Generated rationales can sound plausible while being persuasive rather than true.
14. [Inferred] Cheap self-evaluation for a personal tool: track whether explanations helped the user predict their enjoyment (an effectiveness gap between expected and actual ratings, as in S11). Also check feature diversity across explanations so they do not all read like the same template.

## Limitations or bias
- A short survey with no quantitative synthesis or effect sizes. It predates LLM-generated explanations at scale.
- Many cited works are the authors' own groups' work (e.g., Zhang et al. 2014, Chen et al.). The full comparison table lives on an external spreadsheet (shimo.im) that I did not open.
