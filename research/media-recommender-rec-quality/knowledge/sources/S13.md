## Learned points
1. [Research] Core claim: user behavior data is observational rather than experimental. Biases come from (a) the system's exposure mechanism and users' self-selection, (b) uneven item popularity, and (c) the feedback loop. Fitting this data blindly gives a gap between offline and online results and hurts satisfaction and trust.
2. [Research] The feedback loop has three stages: User→Data (collection), Data→Model (learning), Model→User (serving). Recommendations shape future behavior, which becomes training data again. This produces a self-reinforcing "rich get richer" Matthew effect.
3. [Research] The survey defines seven biases. Four are in the data (selection, exposure, conformity, position), one is in the model (inductive), and two are in the results (popularity bias, unfairness).
4. [Research] Selection bias: users choose what to rate, so ratings are missing not at random. Marlin et al. compared ratings of randomly chosen items with user-chosen items and found that users mostly rate items they like, and rate very bad or very good items more often.
5. [Research] Exposure bias: with implicit feedback, a missing interaction may mean "not interested" or "never saw it." Variants include previous-model bias, user-selection bias, social or community exposure, and popularity (popular items are more likely to be seen).
6. [Research] Conformity bias: users shift ratings toward public opinion or friends. Ratings differ before and after users see public opinion (Krishnan et al.). Position bias: users click top-ranked items regardless of relevance, and eye-tracking shows they examine only the first few items.
7. [Research] Inductive bias is deliberate and often useful: model assumptions such as inner-product embeddings or hard-negative sampling. Not all bias is harmful.
8. [Research] Popularity bias is defined as popular items being recommended more often than their popularity warrants. In a cited figure (Abdollahpouri), the head group holds about 20% of ratings yet dominates what Biased-MF and User-CF recommend. Harms: less personalization and serendipity (especially for niche-taste users), unfairness to good but less-known items, and a Matthew effect from more exposure.
9. [Research] Feedback-loop simulations show popularity bias gets amplified. Popular items become more popular, diversity drops, and users become more alike ("echo chambers" or "filter bubbles"). Conformity, selection, and exposure biases also reinforce themselves through data imbalance.
10. [Research] Debiasing toolkit. Inverse propensity scoring (IPS) reweights observations for selection, exposure, and position bias, but only works if propensities are specified correctly. Other tools: data imputation; models that include conformity features (number of ratings, average rating, rating distribution); causal graphs with back-door adjustment or counterfactual inference; regularizers; adversarial learning; and re-ranking a long list by popularity (Abdollahpouri).
11. [Research] Breaking the loop. Uniform random-exposure data is the "gold standard" for unbiased signal but hurts user experience and revenue, so it is limited to a small share of traffic. Bandits and RL (ε-greedy, UCB, Q-networks, actor-critic) balance exploration and exploitation. Off-policy evaluation of these policies is itself biased.
12. [Research] Future work: estimating propensities in complex settings; one framework for several mixed biases (real data has several at once, e.g., selection plus conformity); unbiased evaluation using a little unbiased data plus a lot of biased data, with standard benchmarks and metrics; knowledge-graph-enhanced debiasing; causal graphs for both debiasing and explanation; dynamic bias that changes over time; and the fairness–accuracy trade-off.
13. [Research] "Double-edged sword": popularity reflects item quality as well as conformity, so using it appropriately can improve recommendations. The goal is not to remove popularity entirely.
14. [Research] Explainable recommendation and debiasing both answer "why was this recommended". The authors suggest causal graphs can serve both.
15. [Inferred] For a prompt-based recommender, the user's watch history is itself biased: they saw what platforms surfaced. "Not watched" is not "disliked." Ask about known-but-skipped titles before treating absence as a negative.
16. [Inferred] Public scores (MAL/IMDb ratings, "trending") carry conformity and popularity bias. Use them as a quality signal, not as the main reason for a pick. Ask the LLM to deliberately include well-matched lesser-known titles.
17. [Inferred] Exploration slots, such as one adjacent-taste "wildcard" per batch with the reason stated, are a lightweight bandit-style way to avoid a taste filter bubble. User feedback on wildcards updates the profile.

## Limitations or bias
- Coverage ends in 2021. Nothing on LLM recommenders. It is heavily oriented to algorithms and training, with little on user-perceived effects.
- Debiasing results are summarized without comparative numbers. The authors note inconsistent metrics across papers.
- Some text (e.g., the position-bias section) calls position bias "popularity bias" by an apparent typo.

## Summary-only items
- Detailed formulas and results for individual debiasing methods (IPS variants, ESAM, DecRS, etc.) were skimmed, not studied.

## Best used for
- A taxonomy and definitions of recommendation biases, the feedback loop and filter bubbles, why popularity bias hurts niche-taste users, and exploration as a remedy.
