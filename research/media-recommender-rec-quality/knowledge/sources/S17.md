## Learned points
1. [Research] Design: online field experiment on an anonymized movie platform ("MovieRec"; GroupLens-affiliated). Eligible users were active in 2023 (at least 12 logins, more than 20 ratings): 3,031 qualified; 449 enrolled, 178 submitted, 160 analyzed. Ran 13 Feb – 14 Mar 2024, no incentives; median completion 17.62 minutes.
2. [Research] Model: Llama-2-7b-Chat hosted locally (for privacy), Gradio chat UI. Between-subject prompt conditions: zero-shot (top 3 genres; n=56), one-shot (plus 1 recently liked, 1 disliked, 1 platform-recommended movie; n=48), few-shot (4 of each; n=56). Liked = top 10% of the user's ratings, disliked = bottom 10%.
3. [Research] The system prompt rules are a useful template: explain why each pick fits the user; give at most 5 recommendations unless asked; give a predicted 1–5 rating; state popularity as High/Medium/Low; avoid movies the user already rated.
4. [Research] Within-subject scenarios (random order): Birthday (ask-for-others), Long Trip (personalization: "movies you haven't seen but are very much in line with your movie taste"), and Niche (tired of popular movies).
5. [Research] Head-to-head vs the classic recommender, the LLM won only on explainability. Users judged the classic system more novel, diverse, accurate and trustworthy across all three scenarios. All pairs were significant (paired t-test, p ≤ .05) except Birthday- and Long-Trip-Explainability.
6. [Research] Free-response coding (N=137) found complaints about lack of novelty (N=56), personalization (N=39), high popularity (N=18) and low diversity (N=10). There was praise for interactivity and explanations (N=49) and reliability concerns (N=42). One user: "It pretty much always stuck to only the most popular movies of all time."
7. [Research] Prompt richness did not matter: no significant differences between zero-, one- and few-shot prompts on any metric. The authors note this aligns with prior work showing more examples don't always help.
8. [Research] Scenario mattered: the Niche scenario was rated more enjoyable and satisfying than Trip, and more novel and serendipitous than both others (ANOVA, e.g., Novelty F=8.461, p<.001).
9. [Research] User experience mattered: users were bucketed by lifetime ratings (light ≤406, medium 406–1522, heavy ≥1522). Heavy raters found the LLM less satisfying, personalized, responsive and serendipitous, and less understanding of their goals. Light raters found it more enjoyable and novel.
10. [Research] Longer conversations went with lower satisfaction (coef −0.079, p=.041) and lower understandability (coef −0.118, p=.002).
11. [Research] Across 417 coded conversation queries (inter-rater kappa 0.652), user-provided Context was the main positive predictor (e.g., Satisfaction coef .464, p=.029; Personalization .584, p=.007; Diversity .748, p=.001). Testing the bot hurt satisfaction (−.938, p=.008). Satisfied users gave like/dislike examples, requirements and feedback, or used chain-of-thought. Dissatisfied ones gave little context, had high expectations, repeated requests, or hit hallucinations.
12. [Research] Hallucination damaged trust. The LLM misstated the user's own profile ("It claimed that I recently disliked the Matrix, that is not true, I liked it"). Users also reported fixation (kept suggesting horror despite dislike), a wish for transparency about the algorithm, and content guardrails that blocked legitimate violent or sexual-theme films.
13. [Research] Design implications from the authors: use RAG or a hybrid with a grounded item database to reduce hallucination and popularity skew. Make the LLM more proactive in asking for context (while keeping conversations short), keep memory within a session, add posters/trailers, give users example queries, and set realistic expectations.
14. [Inferred] For a prompt-based personal recommender: user context and concrete liked/disliked examples matter more than prompt-template elaborateness. So the prompt should actively elicit 3–5 loved titles, disliked titles, and the current mood/occasion, then ask one or two clarifying questions rather than running long dialogue.
15. [Inferred] Out-of-the-box LLM recommendations skew popular and repetitive, especially for heavy viewers (likely including anime fans with long watch histories). The prompt should explicitly exclude already-seen titles, require a popularity label, and reserve slots for lesser-known picks. A "niche" framing seems to play to the LLM's strengths.
16. [Inferred] Keep the "why this fits you" explanation (the LLM's clearest advantage), but ground it in the user's stated examples and never assert profile facts the user did not provide.

## Limitations or bias
- Single small open model (Llama-2-7B, chosen for hardware/privacy reasons); results may not transfer to larger current models.
- Self-selected, highly active users of one platform who are used to a strong collaborative-filtering baseline; comparisons are relative perceptions, not offline accuracy.
- Prompts used only recent ratings; conversation analysis used manual coding by one primary labeler (20% double-coded).
- Unclear venue (template placeholders); single arXiv version.

## Best used for
- Real-user evidence on what LLM movie recommenders do well (explanations, interactivity, niche requests) and badly (popularity skew, low novelty, hallucination, trust).
- Evidence that user-supplied context and like/dislike examples drive satisfaction more than prompt shot count.
- A concrete system-prompt template (explain, max 5, predicted rating, popularity label, exclude seen).
