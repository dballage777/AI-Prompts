- Published / last updated: arXiv v1 26 Jul 2023 (only version). Published at RecSys '23 (17th ACM Conference on Recommender Systems, Singapore, 18–22 Sep 2023), DOI 10.1145/3604915.3608845.
- Type: peer-reviewed conference paper (user-study dataset + offline evaluation)

## Learned points
1. [Research] Research question: can preferences written in natural language ("the sorts of movies I like/dislike") replace item ratings for near cold-start recommendation? And how do LLM prompting methods compare with item-based collaborative filtering?
2. [Research] Data collection (new dataset, movie domain), Phase 1. Raters wrote free-text likes and dislikes (minimum 150 characters), then picked 5 liked and 5 disliked movies via autocomplete over the top 10,000 MovieLens-25M titles, then rewrote their descriptions. Phase 2. Each rater rated 40 movies, shown with a thumbnail and synopsis. They marked whether they had seen each one and gave a 1–5 rating. The 40 came from four pools of 10: random popular (rank 1–1000), random mid-popular (1001–5000), EASE CF top-10, and BM25-Fusion (review-text match) top-10.
3. [Research] Sample: 160 raters were recruited and 153 kept (5 did not finish both phases; 2 gave uniform ratings). Paid contractors, English-only.
4. [Research] Effort: initial "likes" descriptions took a median of 67 s (241 characters) and "dislikes" 38 s (223 characters). Choosing 5 liked items took 174 s and 5 disliked items 175 s. Writing a short description was 3–4× faster than naming 5 items, and the initial descriptions performed as well as the final, rewritten ones.
5. [Research] Rating bias: seen movies averaged 4.29 and unseen movies 3.00. EASE nearly doubled the share of already-seen recommendations (46% seen vs 22% for random popular and 16% for mid-popular). For once-consumed media such as movies, the authors treat the Unseen and Unbiased subsets as the evaluation that matters.
6. [Research] Model and scoring: a 62B-parameter PaLM variant. Each candidate is scored by the log-likelihood of its title (plus an end-of-string token, to avoid penalizing long titles) after a prompt prefix, and all 40 candidates are ranked. This is closed-candidate ranking, not free generation.
7. [Research] Prompt templates: Completion (raw list or description followed by the item). Zero-shot ("I describe the movies I like as follows: … Then I would also like ⟨item⟩"). Few-shot (k = 3 other raters' "User Description → User Movie Preferences" examples). Each was tested for Items-only, Language-only, and Language+Items, plus Pos+Neg variants that add "I dislike the following movies…" or a dislike description.
8. [Research] Main results, NDCG@10 (exponential gain), Unbiased set: LLM Language Few-shot(3) = 0.650 ± 0.026, the best of all methods. Compare WRMF 0.644, Item Few-shot 0.636, Popularity 0.624, BM25-Fusion 0.623, BPR-SLIM 0.617, EASE 0.592, Random 0.532. Unseen set: LLM Item+Language Zero-shot 0.582 was the best, against BPR-SLIM 0.577, WRMF 0.573, Language Few-shot 0.571, EASE 0.559, Popularity 0.534, Random 0.511. On the Full set, EASE (0.673) and BPR-SLIM (0.672) lead, while Language Few-shot reaches 0.640.
9. [Research] The confidence intervals (about ±0.03) overlap heavily, so the paper claims "competitive", not "better". A pretrained LLM with no task-specific supervised training matches CF trained on large rating data in this 5-item near cold-start regime.
10. [Research] Combining language and items gave little benefit over either alone. Few-shot(3) generally beat Zero-shot and Completion, and more than 3 examples did not help.
11. [Research] Dislikes: adding negative preferences (Pos+Neg) gave no meaningful improvement. Using only negative preferences performed at or below the popularity baseline.
12. [Research] On the Unbiased set, the non-personalized popularity baseline (0.624) beat the strong CF model EASE (0.592). Popularity is a hard baseline in near cold-start.
13. [Research] Motivation: natural-language profiles are transparent and scrutable, so users can read and edit what the system "thinks" they like (following Radlinski et al.). This contrasts with inscrutable vector embeddings. The paper also notes the gap between stated intentions and actual consumption (e.g., aspirational podcast subscriptions), which is why both kinds of preference were collected from the same raters.
14. [Research] Ethics and limits as stated: possible unintended item bias (larger studies are needed), 153 raters, English only, and unclear generalization to other languages and cultures.
15. [Inferred] For a personal show/movie/anime recommender in cold start: a ~1-minute free-text description of what the user likes (tone, themes, examples) is a cheap, strong input. Keep it as an editable "taste profile" file the user can correct, since scrutability is the main argument for language profiles. Add a handful of liked titles when available, but do not expect large gains from combining both.
16. [Inferred] Use dislikes as filters (exclude these traits or titles) rather than as the main signal, since negatives alone performed at popularity level. Few-shot examples help, so a prompt can include 2–3 worked "profile → picks" examples. The study ranked a fixed candidate set, so open-ended generation needs separate grounding against hallucinated titles (see S4).

## Limitations or bias
- Small (153 raters, 40 judged items each), movies only, English only. There is no TV or anime data.
- Uses log-likelihood scoring of a fixed 40-item pool, which needs model logits; chat-only APIs cannot do this. Results may not transfer to generate-then-verify chat prompting.
- A single 2023 model (62B PaLM variant). Differences between methods are mostly within confidence intervals. Google authors evaluated a Google model.
- The pools include CF-biased items (SP-EASE). The authors mitigate this by reporting Unbiased and Unseen subsets.
