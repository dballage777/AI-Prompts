# S1 — Explainable Recommendation: A Survey and New Perspectives (Zhang & Chen)

## Learned points
1. [Research] Definition: explainable recommendation means models that "address the problem of why". They return the recommended items plus explanations of why each item was chosen, for users or for system designers. Stated benefits: transparency, persuasiveness, effectiveness, trustworthiness, satisfaction, plus easier debugging for designers.
2. [Research] The survey places "why" in a 5W taxonomy of personalized recommendation: when (time-aware), where (location-based), who (social), what (application-aware), why (explainable).
3. [Research] There are two design philosophies. Model-intrinsic: the model is interpretable, so explanations fall out of how it decides. Model-agnostic / post-hoc: the recommender is a black box and a separate model writes explanations after the decision. The authors link these to human cognition: sometimes we reason first, sometimes we decide first and justify afterwards. They do not say which is correct.
4. [Research] The canonical "because you liked X" explanation comes from item-based collaborative filtering ("the item is similar to your previously loved items"). User-based CF gives "users that are similar to you loved this item". The idea dates to Schafer et al. (1999). Herlocker et al. (2000) studied explanation styles on MovieLens.
5. [Research] Relevant-item explanations are usually more intuitive and credible than relevant-user ones, because users know their own past items. Users know nothing about anonymous "similar users", which lowers trust and can raise privacy problems. Social explanations (friends who liked it) are the workaround.
6. [Research] Six explanation types: (1) relevant user/item; (2) feature-based (genres, actors, directors, tags, e.g., Vig et al.'s 2009 "Tagsplanations" on MovieLens); (3) opinion/aspect-based (aspect–opinion–sentiment triplets mined from reviews, word clouds); (4) sentences, either template-filled or generated by NLG; (5) visual (image region highlights); (6) social. Types 1–3 are tied to particular algorithms. Types 4–6 are about presentation.
7. [Research] Template explanations from the Explicit Factor Model (Zhang et al. 2014a) read "You might be interested in [feature], on which this product performs well". They have a matching "dis-recommendation": "... on which this product performs poorly". User studies found that giving both recommendation and dis-recommendation explanations improves persuasiveness and conversion.
8. [Research] Tintarev (2007) lists seven benefits of explanations: transparency, scrutability, trustworthiness, effectiveness, persuasiveness, efficiency, satisfaction. The survey uses these as evaluation dimensions.
9. [Research] Herlocker et al.'s user-study protocol: participants imagined having "$7 and a free evening". They saw 21 recommendations, each with a different explanation style, and rated on a 1–7 scale how likely they were to see each movie. Vig et al. (2009) measured justification, effectiveness, and "mood compatibility" (whether the item matches the user's mood).
10. [Research] Online evaluation example: Zhang et al. (2014a) ran A/B tests in a commercial web browser with more than 100 million users (26% monthly active). There were three groups: personalized feature-opinion explanations, a baseline "People also viewed" explanation, and no explanation. They measured click-through rate, add-to-cart rate (conversion), and "agreement" (a recommended item added to cart, or a dis-recommended item not added). The survey reports the design, not the effect sizes.
11. [Research] Offline explainability metrics: mean explainability precision/recall (MEP/MER), i.e., the share of top-n items that can be explained, and Model Fidelity = |explainable ∩ recommended| / |recommended|. Generated sentences are scored with BLEU/ROUGE against the user's real review, and with readability indices. The authors still judge that a usable offline explainability measure is lacking.
12. [Research] Two separate evaluation axes. The user perspective asks whether the explanation persuades, informs, and builds trust. The algorithm perspective asks about fidelity, i.e., whether the explanation reveals the real mechanism behind the recommendation. A persuasive explanation can still be unfaithful.
13. [Research] Explanations can hurt. Examples: too much explanation, repeated explanations, explaining the obvious, too much detail, and especially "obviously misleading or even wrong explanations". Per Dietvorst et al. (2015), people lose confidence in algorithms after seeing them mistakenly try to convince.
14. [Research] Social explanations in music (Sharma & Cosley 2013) raised the chance that users checked out a recommended artist, but showed little correlation with the rating users later gave. Persuasion is not the same as satisfaction.
15. [Research] For movies, knowledge-graph explanations ("recommended because you watched many movies starring actor A") are said to have high fidelity. Older content-based matching "lacks serendipity". Other movie-domain examples: crowd-sourced natural-language explanations (Chang et al. 2016), story/narrative-based explanations (Lee & Jung 2018), and generated song-recommendation reasons in XiaoIce that beat manually selected reasons on CTR (Zhao et al. 2019a).
16. [Research] Open directions: diversified explanations help satisfaction (Tsukuda & Goto 2019), but several explanations for one item may be logically inconsistent, and a complete set of explanations is often not what people need (Miller 2019). Others: context-aware explanations, conversational "why?" questions that users ask actively, NLP/pre-trained-LM explanations (2020, pre-LLM era), and links to fairness, diversity, and scrutability.
17. [Research] Explainability and accuracy were traditionally seen as a trade-off (simple and explainable vs complex and accurate). The survey cites evidence that the two need not conflict (Bilgic et al. 2004; Zhang et al. 2014a).
18. [Inferred] For a prompt-based show/movie/anime recommender, the strongest pattern is the item-based "because you liked X" explanation anchored on titles the user actually rated. Add a specific feature or aspect match (tone, pacing, theme). Do not use vague "people like you" claims. Optionally add a short "why you might not like it" note, mirroring the dis-recommendation finding.
19. [Inferred] An LLM explanation is post-hoc by nature, so fidelity is the main risk: the stated reason may not be why the item was picked, and it may cite a liked title or feature incorrectly. The prompt should require that every "because" reference a real entry in the user's history and a checkable property of the recommended title. The survey warns that wrong explanations erode trust faster than no explanation.

- "Usually, relevant-item explanations are more intuitive for users to understand because users are familiar with the items they interacted before." — https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.11192
- "The system also needs to be especially careful not to provide obviously misleading or even wrong explanations" — https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.11192

## Limitations or bias
- Written in 2018–2020, before LLMs. It covers LSTM/GRU sentence generation and BERT only as a future direction, and has no material on LLM-written explanations or hallucinated justifications.
- The survey is heavily model-centric, and the authors admit recent work is "mostly model-driven". The user-study evidence it cites is older (2000–2013) and is mostly summarized without effect sizes.
- E-commerce dominates the examples. Movie and media evidence is mostly MovieLens-based.
- The first author's own work (EFM, JD.com/browser tests) is prominent among the examples.

## Summary-only items
- The model-family details in Ch. 3 (factorization, topic models, graph, deep learning, knowledge graph, rule mining, post-hoc) were skimmed, not studied. Effect sizes of the cited user studies and A/B tests are not given in the survey.

## Best used for
- Taxonomy and vocabulary for explanation types and benefits; the "because you liked X" lineage; evaluation dimensions (persuasiveness vs effectiveness vs fidelity); warnings about misleading or overlong explanations.
