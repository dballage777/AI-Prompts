# S8 — What Are We Optimizing For? A Human-centric Evaluation of Deep Learning-based Movie Recommenders (Sun, Wu, Akella, Kong, Knijnenburg & Konstan)
- URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.11632 (full text: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.11632)
- Access status: OPENED · Date read: 2026-11-14
- Pages read: full PDF v2 (abstract, sections 1–6, tables 1–6, figures 1–3)
- Published / last updated: arXiv v1 21 Jan 2024; v2 1 May 2024. The venue is not stated: the PDF uses an ACM template with placeholder venue text. Authors are from GroupLens (University of Minnesota), MIT, and Clemson.
- Type: academic user study (survey of real users, plus path analysis)

## Learned points
1. [Research] Setup: four deep-learning recommenders that top the ML-1M leaderboard (NCF, BERT4Rec, SSE-PT, GLocal-K) were compared with two classic CF baselines (user-based kNN CF and Funk SVD). They were trained on 3 years of MovieLens ratings (3,537 users, 7,462 movies, 983,376 ratings; movies with fewer than 20 ratings removed). 445 of 3,172 active users responded (14.03%). Each saw four top-12 lists (3 DL lists and 1 CF list).
2. [Research] Seven human-centric metrics, each one 5-point Likert item: Novelty ("How familiar are you with movies in the list?"), Diversity ("How much variety...?"), Serendipity ("How much do the movies match what you would expect a movie recommender to give you?"), Accuracy ("match your interests"), Trustworthiness ("convinced you would enjoy"), Transparency ("understand why these movies are recommended"), and Satisfaction.
3. [Research] Main result: DL models win on novelty and unexpectedness but lose to classic CF on diversity, trust, transparency, accuracy, and satisfaction. SVD had the highest satisfaction (3.500), trust (3.495), transparency (3.587), and accuracy (3.284). GLocal-K was most novel (3.611) and SSE-PT most serendipitous (3.252). SSE-PT had the lowest transparency (2.397). UU-CF had the highest diversity (4.004). Only NCF was statistically level with SVD on the last four metrics.
4. [Research] Path analysis (CFI 0.987, TLI 0.975, RMSEA 0.039): 55.1% of the variance in satisfaction (R² 0.551) runs through Accuracy, Trustworthiness, and Transparency. Accuracy has the strongest effect on satisfaction (β 0.622). Trust is driven by Accuracy, Transparency, and Diversity, and Serendipity lowers trust (β −0.176). Novelty and Diversity raise Transparency, and Serendipity lowers it (β −0.364).
5. [Research] The mechanism the authors propose: low diversity → lower transparency → lower accuracy and trust → lower satisfaction. Too much serendipity "might confuse users and weaken transparency and trust." They cite Kotkov et al.: higher serendipity did not necessarily raise satisfaction. Their advice is to find the right balance with real users rather than "blindly optimizing for a higher value."
6. [Research] User context: how long users had been on the site and how often they used it did not predict perceptions. Users with lower average historic ratings found recommendations more unexpected. Users who rated novelty or diversity as important perceived more novelty or diversity, and this had small positive total effects on accuracy, trust, and satisfaction.
7. [Research] Qualitative themes from 294 free-text answers, coded with grounded theory: accuracy only (N=52); "Accuracy + X" (accuracy plus novelty N=54, plus diversity N=12, plus serendipity N=10); trust builders (well-grounded recommendations N=21, accuracy N=10, a preference for human sources N=3); and demand for transparency (N=27).
8. [Research] Users' own words show the design tension. One wants a recommender that would "challenge my tastes without offending me". One likes seeing already-seen movies in the list as proof "the algorithm is in track." One trusts MovieLens because "I can't tell where the recommendation came from." Another wants control: "only want to see thrillers released after a certain year," and at other times very diverse lists.
9. [Research] Transparency requests include explanations tied to movies the user watched ("Pointing out why it was picked would help me select a movie"; a user cites Amazon Prime's "because you watched" style) and more metadata such as writers. The authors suggest LLM-generated personalized explanations as a future direction.
10. [Research] Design recommendations: add diversity-aware re-ranking, gather subjective user data (rating dispositions, personality), and build trust through diversity and transparency, not accuracy alone.
11. [Research] Limitations the authors state: movies only; active users only, so no cold start; four DL models; single-item measures, so no CFA; a static survey; hyperparameters not tuned for this data.
12. [Inferred] For a prompt-based recommender, each list should mix a few "anchor" picks that clearly match known tastes with a smaller number of stretch picks. Every pick should carry a short "because you liked X / this matches your preference for Y" reason, since transparency and perceived accuracy drive satisfaction more than novelty does.
13. [Inferred] Ask the user once how much novelty and variety they want (and let them change it per session, such as "comfort rewatch mode" vs "surprise me"). Users differ in what they value, and ambitious serendipity without an explanation lowers trust.

## Limitations or bias
- MovieLens users are enthusiastic, long-term raters, not typical streaming viewers. There was no incentive, and the response rate was 14%, so self-selection is likely.
- Each construct was measured with a single Likert item. "Serendipity" was operationalized as unexpectedness relative to expectations, not unexpected-and-liked, so its negative effect may partly reflect mismatched items.
- The lists showed only titles, years, and links, with no explanations, so transparency was judged without any explanation interface.
- Not peer-review status-confirmed: the venue is placeholder text in the PDF. LLM recommenders were not tested.
