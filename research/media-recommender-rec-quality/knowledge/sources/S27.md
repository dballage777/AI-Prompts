## Learned points
1. [Reported] The article groups LLM-era recommender and search changes into four themes: (a) LLM- and multimodality-augmented model architectures, (b) LLM-assisted data generation and analysis, (c) scaling laws, transfer learning, distillation and LoRAs, and (d) unified architectures for search and recommendations.
2. [Reported] Content and language features are mainly used to fix the cold-start and long-tail weaknesses of ID-based models. YouTube's Semantic IDs (content embeddings compressed with an RQ-VAE: 8 levels, 2,048-entry codebooks) beat random hash IDs, "especially in cold-start scenarios". Dense content embeddings used directly did worse than random hash IDs, because rankers rely on memorizing IDs.
3. [Reported] Kuaishou's M3CSR clusters multimodal embeddings (about 1,000 K-means clusters over 10M+ videos) into trainable IDs. A/B results: clicks +3.4%, likes +3.0%, follows +3.1%, cold-start velocity +1.2%, cold-start video coverage +3.6%.
4. [Reported] Text-only LLM recommenders: Google's CALRec fine-tunes PaLM-2 XXS on items written as text ("Attribute name: Attribute description"). At inference it samples multiple candidates, removes duplicates, sorts by log-probability, then maps each generated text to real catalog items with BM25. This grounding step guards against generated items that don't exist. Contrastive alignment added 0.8–1.7%.
5. [Reported] Meta's EmbSum uses Mixtral-8x22B to write interpretable user-interest summaries from engagement histories, then encodes them with T5-small (61M). GPT-4 judged the summaries to be of high quality, but the method was not compared against behavioral recommenders.
6. [Reported] LLMs as quality judges: Bing used GPT-4 to write titles and snippets for 2M pages, distilled into Mistral-7B, and added a quality-scoring cross-encoder. Results: clickbait −31%, low-authority content −35%, duplicates −76%, higher-authority content +18%, cross-medium recommendations +48%. This happened "despite lower-quality content (e.g., clickbait) historically showing higher CTR".
7. [Reported] Indeed's LLM-as-filter: about 250 cross-reviewed matches gave 147 high-confidence labels. Llama2 and Mistral-7B "struggled with detailed prompts", while GPT-4 worked but cost too much. A fine-tuned GPT-3.5 matched GPT-4 at a quarter of the cost, but its 6.7 s latency was too slow, so labels trained a lightweight classifier (AUC 0.86). Results: batch matches −17.68%, unsubscribes −4.97%, applications +4.13%. Removing bad recommendations improved outcomes.
8. [Reported] Practical LLM deployment patterns: cache precomputed LLM outputs for head queries (Yelp covers 95% of traffic this way via batch calls), use RAG with catalog context to disambiguate intent, build "golden datasets" from a strong model, and fine-tune smaller models to serve them.
9. [Reported] Spotify's LLM-generated exploratory query suggestions led to a 9% increase in exploratory-intent queries and a 30% rise in maximum query length. Removing the ranker cut clicks on recommendations by 20%. Amazon used LLMs to write playlist descriptions from the first 15 tracks, as LLM labelers, and as LLM judges, for "double-digit recall improvements".
10. [Reported] Scaling laws for ID-based sequential recommenders (98.3K–0.8B parameters, MovieLens-20M and Amazon-2018) follow a power law. Larger models need less data: the 98.3K model needed 18.5M interactions against 9.2M for the 75.5M model to reach similar performance. Gains were largest for long-tail items and cold-start users.
11. [Reported] Distilling LLMs into small recommenders: DLLM2Rec (teacher BIGRec on Llama2-7B) gave student models a 47.97% average improvement on HR and NDCG across Amazon Video Games, MovieLens-10M and Amazon Toys. Inference time fell from 3–6 hours to 1.6–1.8 s. One named challenge is "unreliable teacher knowledge/labels", handled partly by checking the teacher's "content similarity between generated descriptions and actual items".
12. [Reported] Unified generative retrieval (Spotify, Flan-T5-base, one token per item ID) beat single-task versions by 16% recall@30 on average, and torso items gained 262% (recommendations) and 855% (search). Yet "generative models consistently lagged behind" SASRec and BERT4Rec, and BM25 for search. Yan's verdict: generative retrieval is "still far from fully replacing conventional methods."
13. [Reported] LinkedIn's 360Brew (150B parameters, built on Mixtral-8x22B, data from about 45M US monthly active users) replaces feature engineering with prompt engineering across 30+ ranking tasks. It matched or beat specialized models only after the data was tripled, and it outperformed traditional models by a wider margin for cold-start users.
14. [Reported] Netflix's UniCoRn serves search and recommendations with one contextual ranker. Personalization gave +10% for recommendations and +7% for search. Netflix's sliding-window training over long histories improved recall by 7.01% and MAP by 1.5%, while MRR fell 1.2%.
15. [Reported] Zalando's policy layer handles exploration (epsilon-greedy promotion of new products), lowers the visibility of items already bought, and enforces diversity: no two items from the same brand back to back. Overall, four A/B tests gave +15% engagement and +2.2% revenue.
16. [Reported] Yan's overall view: early 2023 work that applied LLMs to recommendations "often fell short". The 2024–25 industrial work is more promising because it is "backed by industry results". In most systems the LLM augments ID and behavioral models rather than replacing them.
17. [Inferred] For a prompt-based personal show/movie/anime recommender: copy the CALRec grounding step. Let the LLM generate candidate titles freely, then check each one against a real catalog or metadata source (title, year, type) before showing it. This addresses hallucinated items.
18. [Inferred] Use the LLM as a quality and fit judge and a filter, not only as a generator (Bing and Indeed showed gains from removing bad items). Add a simple "policy layer" in the prompt or code: drop already-watched titles, cap repeats from the same franchise or studio, and reserve a slot for something new or exploratory.

## Limitations or bias
- Secondary summary. The numbers are Yan's restatements of company papers and were not checked against the originals here. The papers are mostly self-reported industry results that may favor positive outcomes.
- Focuses on large-scale industrial systems (training, serving, latency). Little of it is about prompt-only, single-user recommenders, and almost nothing is about explanation, serendipity or user perception.
- The author's own judgments ("IMHO unfair" baselines, "Recommended read") are opinions, though they usefully flag weak comparisons.

## Summary-only items
- All per-paper results are second-hand via this article (the underlying papers were not opened). Treat them as "Reported", not verified.

## Best used for
- An overview of how industry combines LLMs with behavioral recommenders (as of early 2025). Concrete patterns for cold start, grounding generated items against a catalog, LLM-as-judge and filter, and policy-layer diversity rules.
