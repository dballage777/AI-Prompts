## Learned points
1. [Research] Scope: 123 papers were reviewed and coded. The library query ("popularity bias" AND recommender/recommendation*, Jan 2000–Jan 2024, last run 24 Jan 2024) returned 129 papers, and snowballing added more. The text also says "we ended up with 54 papers" after filtering. That count conflicts with the 123 used elsewhere and is not explained. Around 70% of the papers are from the last five years, and interest has risen sharply since about 2018.
2. [Research] There is no agreed definition. Popularity bias is variously framed as (a) recommending popular items "more frequently than their popularity would warrant", (b) emphasizing popular items over long-tail items per se, (c) a property of skewed training data, or (d) an artifact of offline metrics. Precision favors popularity-oriented algorithms, so non-personalized popularity baselines can look competitive.
3. [Research] The authors propose an impact-oriented definition: bias is a problem only when the focus on popular items limits value or causes harm for some stakeholder. The definition does not fix a popularity measure or a source, and it covers both short- and long-term effects.
4. [Research] Sources: a "natural bias" (some films simply appeal more broadly), data-collection bias, and algorithmic amplification. In the feedback loop, position bias gives top-ranked items more attention, consumption feeds retraining, and missing-not-at-random feedback (people rate what they like) makes it worse: the "rich get richer" or Matthew effect.
5. [Research] Field evidence (Lee & Hosanagar 2014/2019, a North American retailer): the recommender raised niche-item sales, but popular-item sales rose even more, so aggregate sales diversity fell.
6. [Research] Harms to users: obvious, non-novel recommendations that do not support discovery and are poorly personalized, which can erode trust. Harms to providers: missed long-tail sales and reduced sales diversity. In other settings, unfair exposure, amplified engagement-driven harmful content, and vulnerability to fake ratings or bots.
7. [Research] Popularity is not always bad. It is the standard cold-start strategy (users can rate popular items they know, though those ratings carry little information). Overly niche lists can seem obscure and lower satisfaction (Ekstrand et al. 2014). Netflix adds a "healthy dose of (unpersonalized) popularity" to its video ranker (Gomez-Uribe & Hunt 2015).
8. [Research] Novelty is usually defined as "not previously known to the user". Offline it is approximated by low popularity, so novelty metrics are inversely related to popularity. Serendipity is often unexpectedness plus relevance: a serendipitous item must be novel and must also differ from the user's usual taste. Diversity (items in a list differing from one another) relates to popularity less directly. Popular movies tend to be homogeneous in country, budget, and language. Aggregate diversity means catalog coverage.
9. [Research] Users differ in "mainstreamness" or personal popularity tendency. Calibration approaches (Oh et al. 2011; Steck 2018 = S3; Abdollahpouri et al. 2020b) fit each user's own popularity appetite, and several works ask whether niche-loving users get less utility.
10. [Research] Mitigation can happen pre-processing (resampling, dropping the short head), in-processing (the most common: regularized or multi-objective losses), or post-processing. Post-processing methods: re-scaling scores, re-ranking (most common; e.g., xQuAD adaptation, calibrated-popularity "CP"), rank aggregation with an inverse-popularity list, and post-filtering. Post-processing is cheap, versatile, and runs on top of any model.
11. [Research] Re-ranking to match each user's popularity preference does not necessarily reduce platform-wide popularity bias significantly (Klimashevskaia et al. 2022). A months-long A/B test of CP re-ranking on a real movie-streaming platform found no loss in CTR and more diverse consumption (Klimashevskaia et al. 2023a).
12. [Research] Utilization: some popularity reflects genuine quality ("true quality popularity", Zhao et al. 2022). Recent popularity matters for news and e-commerce accuracy.
13. [Research] Evaluation is almost entirely offline. Only four user studies were found (all published in 2015 or earlier) and one field test, plus later perception studies. In Steck (2011), 20 subjects judged even a small shift toward the long tail as quickly less useful, with a loss of trust. Yin et al. (2012) had 50 subjects rate movies: the long-tail method raised novelty and serendipity while keeping a good preference match. In Cremonesi et al. (2014), 382 subjects in a hotel scenario rated popular recommendations highest in low season and a hybrid best in high season. In Lee & Lee (2015), 44 subjects on last.fm saw novelty rise while mean relevance fell from 3.8 to 3.3 out of 5.
14. [Research] Perception studies (Ferwerda et al. 2023; Lesota et al. 2023) found that computational popularity-bias metrics often do not correlate well with what users perceive.
15. [Research] Methodological problems: head/tail thresholds are arbitrary (top 20% of items is common, but some use 10% or 1%) and change the results. Pre-processing is often undocumented. MovieLens dominates, and its pre-filtering makes popularity less skewed than in real catalogs. ARP (Average Recommendation Popularity) and the Gini index are the most-used bias metrics. APLT/ACLT count long-tail items. NDCG is the most-used accuracy metric.
16. [Research] The survey's critique: research falls into an "abstraction trap". It assumes popularity is bad per se, does not motivate why bias harms a given domain, and offers no principled answer to "what is the right amount of popularity bias". The authors call for application-specific reasoning, human-in-the-loop studies, and longitudinal simulations.
17. [Inferred] A personal show/movie/anime recommender has one user, so the relevant harm is user-side: obvious picks that bring no discovery. The prompt should set a target mix, e.g., a few well-known "safe" titles plus explicitly lesser-known ones, scaled to the user's own mainstreamness (from their history) rather than a fixed rule.
18. [Inferred] Steck (2011) and Lee & Lee (2015) show that pushing obscurity too hard quickly costs perceived usefulness. The recommender should label which picks are deep cuts and give a reason for each, so a miss does not damage trust in the whole list. Because metrics poorly match perception, success should be judged by asking the user whether picks felt new to them, not by computing popularity scores.

## Key quotes
- "A recommender system faces issues of popularity bias when the recommendations provided by the system focus on popular items to the extent that they limit the value of the system or create harm for some of the involved stakeholders." — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.01118

- "Clearly, a serendipitous item must also be novel." — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.01118

## Limitations or bias
- Focused on classic collaborative-filtering literature to January 2024. It does not cover popularity bias in LLM recommenders (see S4 for that).
- The user-study evidence is thin, old, and small (20–382 participants). The survey's own counts are inconsistent (54 vs 123 papers).
- The authors include their own work (Klimashevskaia et al. 2022, 2023a). The A/B result is summarized without effect sizes.

## Summary-only items
- The in-processing technique details (5.2.3) and the full metric definitions (Tables 3–4) were skimmed. Individual cited studies (e.g., Lee & Hosanagar, Ekstrand et al. 2014) are known only through the survey's summary.

## Best used for
- Definitions and relations of popularity bias, novelty, serendipity, diversity, and fairness; arguments for and against recommending popular titles; post-processing re-ranking options; evidence that users penalize excessive obscurity; the critique of offline-only metrics.
