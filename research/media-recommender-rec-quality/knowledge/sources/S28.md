# S28 — The Unfairness of Popularity Bias in Recommendation (Abdollahpouri, Mansoury, Burke & Mobasher)
- URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.13286 (full text: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.13286)
- Access status: OPENED · Date read: 2026-11-14
- Pages read: full PDF (sections 1–7 plus references)
- Published / last updated: arXiv v1 31 Jul 2019; latest v3 19 Sep 2019. Venue stated in the paper: RMSE workshop at the 13th ACM Conference on Recommender Systems (RecSys 2019), Copenhagen.
- Type: peer-reviewed workshop paper (short empirical study)

## Learned points
1. [Research] Definition: popularity bias means popular (frequently rated) items get a lot of exposure while less popular ones are under-represented in recommendations. Rating data is long-tailed, so algorithms inherit the bias and often amplify it: "the rich get richer and the poor get poorer."
2. [Research] The paper's new angle is the user's perspective rather than catalog coverage. Recommendations are unfair when their popularity mix departs from the mix in the user's own profile. This links to Steck's (2018) idea of calibration (a user who rated 70% action and 30% romance should get a similar mix). The authors suggest that genre distortion may itself be caused by different popularity levels across genres.
3. [Research] Data: MovieLens 1M, with 1,000,209 ratings of about 3,900 movies by 6,040 users. An item is "popular" if it is in the top 20% of item popularity. Item popularity = number of users who rated it divided by the total number of users.
4. [Research] Users are not uniformly mainstream. More than 4,000 of the 6,040 users have at least 20% non-popular items in their profiles. Even the most mainstream users mostly have about 10–20% long-tail items.
5. [Research] Profile size is negatively correlated with the share and average popularity of popular items. Heavy raters run out of hits and rate more niche titles, so the users most interested in the long tail are also the most active, valuable users.
6. [Research] Three user groups by share of popular items: Niche (bottom 20%; more than half the profile is long-tail), Blockbuster-focused (top 20%; on average more than 85% popular items), and Diverse (the rest; more than 3,500 users). Niche and Diverse together are about 75% of users. Niche users have the largest profiles and Blockbuster users the smallest.
7. [Research] Method: 80/20 train/test split, top-10 lists. Algorithms: User KNN, Item KNN, SVD++ and Biased MF, all tuned to similar precision (about 0.1), plus Most-popular and Random baselines.
8. [Research] In every algorithm except Random, many items are almost never recommended. The correlation between how often an item is rated and how often it is recommended is strongest for Most-popular, then User KNN and Item KNN. Biased MF shows no clear correlation but recommends a very narrow set of items.
9. [Research] Metric: Group Average Popularity, GAP(g), is the mean popularity of items in a group's profiles (GAPp) or in its recommendations (GAPr). ΔGAP = (GAPr − GAPp) / GAPp, and ΔGAP = 0 means fair. Most-popular has the highest ΔGAP, followed by Item KNN. Random has negative ΔGAP. Niche users have the largest ΔGAP under every algorithm.
10. [Research] For all non-random algorithms, the share of popular items in recommendations rises sharply for every group (often close to 100% popular). SVD++ was the most consistent with users' expected popularity ratio.
11. [Research] Answers to the research questions: many users want non-popular items, yet "almost all the recommendations they received were popular items". Users are affected unequally, and niche users are affected most.
12. [Research] Stated reasons why the long tail matters: it gives a fuller picture of preferences (long-tail items carry more information in active learning), it is a social good (it avoids a homogeneous market dominated by big brands), and ignoring niche users risks losing them.
13. [Inferred] For a prompt-based personal show/movie/anime recommender: LLMs trained on web text are likely to show a similar pull toward famous titles (this paper tests collaborative filtering only, not LLMs). Measure the user's own popularity mix (for example, the share of their watched titles that are mainstream hits versus obscure ones) and ask the model to match it. This is calibration, not maximum novelty.
14. [Inferred] Heavy watchers, such as anime fans with long lists, are probably the most "niche" users and the ones most hurt by blockbuster-heavy suggestions. A simple check: count how many recommended titles are top-chart staples and compare that with the user's history.

## Limitations or bias
- One dataset (MovieLens 1M, ratings from about 2000) and one split. The authors plan more datasets as future work.
- Uses classic collaborative filtering algorithms only, with no LLMs or content-based methods. Popularity is defined by rating counts within the dataset, not real-world fame.
- The fairness definition assumes users want recommendations that match the popularity ratio of their profile. It measures no user satisfaction, relevance or diversity (left for future work).
- Results are mostly shown in figures. Exact ΔGAP values are not given in the text.
