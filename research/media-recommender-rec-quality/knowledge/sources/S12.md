# S12 — Calibrated Recommendations: Survey and Future Directions (da Silva & Jannach)
- URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02643 (PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.02643)
- Access status: OPENED (full PDF) · Date read: 2026-11-14
- Pages read: full text, pp. 1–23 (methodology, architecture, evaluation, discussion, future work, summary) and the start of the references
- Published / last updated: arXiv v1 3 Jul 2025 (only version). Typeset in the ACM Transactions on Recommender Systems template with a placeholder DOI. Final publication status is not confirmed.
- Type: systematic literature survey (53 papers), University of Klagenfurt

## Learned points
1. [Research] Definition: calibration makes the item properties in a recommendation list match the distribution of the user's own past preferences, so the list is not limited to one subset of their interests. Steck coined the term in 2018.
2. [Research] Calibration is personalized diversification. It differs from generic diversity methods because the target mix is the individual user's. A user with diverse tastes gets a diverse list. A monothematic user may get less diversity after calibration.
3. [Research] Method: 57 ACM DL hits and 356 Google Scholar hits (query run May 2025, papers from 2018 on). 51 papers passed the criteria, plus two earlier precursors: Oh et al. 2011 (Personal Popularity Tendency Matching, using Earth Mover's Distance) and Jugovac et al. 2017. Total: 53. Over 80% were published in conference or workshop proceedings, with 12 at RecSys. Publications grew most in 2024.
4. [Research] Standard pipeline after Steck, implemented as a post-processing re-rank. Steps: baseline ranking, candidate selection (usually the top 100; range 10–1,000), aggregate relevance (sum of predicted scores), target distribution P (past genres weighted by rating, each genre counting 1/number of genres of the item), realized distribution Q, a calibration measure (KL divergence is most common, JS is second), a trade-off, and greedy item selection.
5. [Research] Trade-off objective: maximize (1−λ)·relevance − λ·miscalibration. λ = 0 means accuracy only, 1 means calibration only, and studies typically sweep λ from 0 to 1 in steps of 0.1. Steck smooths Q with α ≈ 0.01 so rare genres are not ignored. Finding the optimal list is NP-hard. A greedy surrogate-submodular algorithm gives a (1−1/e) guarantee.
6. [Research] Variants: recency weighting of the history, entropy- and time-aware target distributions, LSTM-predicted future distributions, and personalized λ (several studies show gains, depending on the algorithm). A benchmark of 57 distance measures found 4 (Vicis Emanon2, Kulczynski S, Kumar Johnson, Additive Chi) consistently beat KL and JS. Simply normalizing the distribution improved both precision and calibration over Steck's version.
7. [Research] Calibration targets: genre in most studies, and popularity (Abdollahpouri's Calibrated Popularity with Head/Middle/Tail groups) in many. Others include quality level, expert video tags, and user "subprofiles" (sets of items that capture one interest). Multi-objective versions balance accuracy, diversity, popularity, provider fairness, novelty, surprise, coverage, and redundancy. One two-stage approach first calibrates popularity, then genre.
8. [Research] Evidence base: almost every paper uses a movie dataset, mostly MovieLens. Music is a distant second. 88% of studies are offline only, 8% are user studies, and 2% (one paper) report an online test. 27.1% do not report their data split. Almost 90% use collaborative-filtering baselines (BPR, ItemKNN, and UserKNN are the most common), and about 10% use content-based ones.
9. [Research] User studies. Recipes, 32 participants: similarity-only lists were rated significantly worse than diversity-enhanced or calibrated lists. Movies, 500 participants: fairness calibration toward non-blockbuster, low-budget titles steered choices without lowering perceived quality, but users noticed the fairness improvement only when told by an explanation. Music, 56 participants: users did perceive popularity-bias differences when the bias was framed as popularity miscalibration. Spotify users, 40 participants: popularity level did not significantly affect satisfaction.
10. [Research] The single field A/B test (Klimashevskaia et al., Calibrated Popularity) promoted less-popular items without hurting click-through rate. This suggests the accuracy–calibration trade-off may not appear in practice. Setting the popular/unpopular threshold was hard, and a weak threshold can make the intervention too small to matter.
11. [Research] Open issues: tuning thresholds and λ, scalability, averages that hide users whose lists get worse, subtle effects users don't notice without labels, possible suppression of trending content (e.g., in news), and some users who would rather get popular or trending items.
12. [Research] Future directions: decide when and for whom to calibrate; dynamic or intent-aware calibration for shifting tastes (only Kaya & Bridge link calibration with intent-awareness); cold start (unclear what to calibrate to with little history); more domains; more field studies; and a unified benchmark (about 58% of papers propose new techniques, but none compares most of them).
13. [Inferred] For a prompt-based recommender, ask the LLM to state the user's taste mix (e.g., 60% dark thriller, 30% cozy slice-of-life anime, 10% documentary), then make each batch roughly match that mix. Weight recent watches more heavily and keep at least one slot for minor interests.
14. [Inferred] Popularity calibration works the same way: if the user's history is 30% niche, keep about 30% lesser-known titles in each batch. Label this ("including a few hidden gems") because users don't notice calibration unless told.
15. [Inferred] Let the user set the trade-off ("stick close to my favorites" vs. "mix it up"), since the right λ is personal. For new users with little history, ask about the mix of moods and genres they want instead of calibrating to sparse data.

## Key quotes
- "When a user has watched, say, 70 romance movies and 30 action movies, then it is reasonable to expect the personalized list of recommended movies to be comprised of about 70% romance and 30% action movies as well." — https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02643 (quoting Steck 2018)
- "we found that 88% of the analyzed studies employ an offline evaluation protocol, while only 8% conduct user studies" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02643
- "users only perceive a recommendation list as calibrated or fair when it is accompanied by a label or explanatory text indicating so" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02643

## Limitations or bias
- The survey excludes preprints and theses. The evidence is mostly offline, on MovieLens, with collaborative-filtering baselines. There are few human studies and they are small (32–500 participants).
- The second author is a co-author of several surveyed works (e.g., Alves et al.; Jugovac et al.).
- It does not cover LLM-based calibration.

## Summary-only items
- Results of individual surveyed papers (e.g., the 57-measure benchmark, MCF, DACSR) are known only through the survey's summaries.

## Best used for
- Definition and mechanics of calibration, matching the user's genre and popularity mix, the evidence that it rarely hurts accuracy, and the need to label calibrated lists.
