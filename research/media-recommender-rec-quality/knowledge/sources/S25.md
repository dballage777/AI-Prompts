## Learned points
1. [Reported] Core claim: LLMs "are not databases". They synthesize the most probable next token rather than retrieving facts. If the most linguistically plausible word is wrong, the model outputs it anyway.
2. [Reported] Hallucination risk is highest when training data is thin or conflicting. In media, this means recent releases, recent events (the latest Academy Awards) and "lesser-known or fringe titles", which is where personal recommenders often want to go.
3. [Reported] The article claims that as of 2026 no LLM is hallucination-free and this is unlikely to change soon. Grounding in verified external data is presented as "the only viable approach." (This is the vendor's commercial position.)
4. [Reported] It lists LLM advantages for entertainment over traditional database search: ranking and sorting, "hyper-personalized recommendations", catalog harmonization, and conversational search. Consumers see 10–20 metadata attributes per title, while services track hundreds to thousands.
5. [Reported] Risk varies by attribute. Its risk table:
   - General: identifiers (TMSID) Critical, Type Very low, Actors Low, Genre Low, Description Low, Images Critical, Duration Medium.
   - Movies: Year Medium, Director Medium.
   - TV: Year range Medium, Creator Medium, Season count High.
   - Episodes: Episode title, Season number, Episode number, Original air date and Episode director all High.
6. [Reported] Why the categorical attributes are low risk: models usually know movie vs. show, and genres form a finite set (for example, "The Batman" as action/crime). Sub-genres are often missed, and labels "will not match a standard taxonomy." LLMs can confuse shows and movies with the same title, especially when they share cast.
7. [Reported] Actors: high accuracy for leading names, but "association bias" can place an actor in a project because they often work with that director or genre. Directors of small films may be swapped for the producer or a more famous contemporary.
8. [Reported] Numbers: LLMs "regress to the mean" on runtimes (22, 44, 90 or 120 minutes). Release years are often off by one ("Gracenote research has shown...", with no data given). Numbers split into sub-tokens (154 becomes "15" and "4"), which causes off-by-one errors. Low season numbers (1–3) dominate training text, so the model defaults to them. Episode numbers are "often a coin toss" and can differ between runs of the same prompt.
9. [Reported] Knowledge cutoff causes stale facts. A show with five seasons today may be reported as having three. End years for shows cancelled or renewed after the cutoff are hallucinated.
10. [Reported] "Semantic gravity": the model fabricates plausible titles that fit a show's naming pattern, such as a Friends episode "The One With the Solar Eclipse" (or "The One with the Coffee"), which sound authentic but do not exist.
11. [Reported] Overconfidence: without specific tuning the model has no "I don't know" state. An illustrative probability map: "Titanic (1997) director" gives James at 99.2%, while a less-documented Breaking Bad episode ('The Fly') puts the showrunner Vince ahead of the actual director Rian. The figures are presented as an example, and their source is not given.
12. [Reported] Descriptions are generally plausible but "soft". They will not follow editorial rules (for example, no spoilers) unless the rules are requested. Image URLs are often invented paths with unknown usage rights.
13. [Inferred] For a prompt-based show/movie/anime recommender, the risky fields are not the picks' genres or vibes but hard facts: release year, season and episode counts, runtime, where to stream, and especially IDs and URLs. The model should either not state these or verify them against a database or API (for example an anime or movie metadata API) before showing them.
14. [Inferred] Every recommended title should be resolved to a real catalog entry with title, year and type to catch same-title confusion and invented titles, with extra care for new releases and obscure or niche picks. The prompt should explicitly allow the model to say "not sure" about facts, and should ask for spoiler-free descriptions.

## Limitations or bias
- Vendor source with a commercial interest: Gracenote sells the metadata used for "grounding", and the article concludes grounding is the only viable fix.
- No method, models, sample sizes or error rates are given. The risk ratings are qualitative. "Gracenote research" on off-by-one years is cited without data. The probability-map percentages are illustrative.
- The token-splitting explanation is simplified, and the article does not distinguish between models or tool-using or retrieval setups.
- It addresses metadata Q&A, not recommendation quality as such (relevance, diversity).
