## Learned points
1. [Research] The paper uses Klimashevskaia et al.'s definition: popularity bias is present when recommendations "focus on popular items to the extent that they limit the value of the system or create harm" for stakeholders (users, providers, content creators). Recommending popular items more often is acceptable if they really are more relevant. Bias starts when the predicted popularity exceeds the user's own base popularity "in expectation".
2. [Research] Bias is measured relative to each user. A recommender is positively or negatively biased if it recommends more popular or less popular items than is appropriate for that user, judged against the popularity of their own watch history. Both directions count as bias.
3. [Research] Item popularity follows a power law. On MovieLens 10M the fitted Pareto coefficient is α = 0.68, so mean and variance are undefined, and metrics built on raw averages (such as average popularity lift) are statistically ill-behaved: one blockbuster entering a set can make the value jump.
4. [Research] Five desiderata for a popularity-bias metric: well behaved (stable with more data), centered on 0, anti-symmetric, sensitive to the long tail, and monotonic. Popularity lift, Gini, popularity rank correlation and Herfindahl each fail at least one. The proposed log popularity difference, ΔM = mean(log φ) over recommended items − mean(log φ) over the user's history, satisfies all five.
5. [Research] The recommender tested, called WOK ("WOrld Knowledge"), is a simple LLM prompt: the user's watch history plus a request for 10 movies, with formatting rules (title plus year in parentheses), no already-watched movies, and nothing newer than 2008 (the end of MovieLens). It uses only parametric knowledge, with no catalog in the prompt. Models: gpt-3.5-turbo-0613, gpt-4-1106-preview, claude-instant-1.2, claude-2.1, all at temperature 0.
6. [Research] Setup: MovieLens 10M (10M ratings, 10k movies, 72k users), subsampled to 5 folds of 1,000 users each. Baselines are ItemKNN and UserKNN (K=30), TopPop and Random. Metrics are HR@5 and HR@10, popularity bias (log difference), and unmatched items per 10.
7. [Research] Accuracy: UserKNN is best (HR@10 0.411), then ItemKNN (0.282). The LLMs are far lower: HR@10 0.054 (claude-2.1) to 0.081 (gpt-4). The authors attribute the gap to collaborative filtering seeing every user's interactions while WOK sees only one user's history.
8. [Research] Popularity bias (lower is better): TopPop 1.455, ItemKNN 0.957, UserKNN 0.630, claude-instant 0.565, gpt-3.5 0.682, gpt-4 0.392, claude-2.1 0.377 (lowest). Random is −4.020, because MovieLens users' histories are already well above average popularity. LLM recommenders showed less popularity bias than CF "even without any explicit mitigation."
9. [Research] Invalid or unmatched items per 10 recommendations: 0.61 (gpt-3.5), 0.87 (gpt-4), 1.36 (both Claude models). All models returned valid recommendations at least 8.5 times out of 10. Invalid means wrong format, already watched, nonexistent title, or a real title missing from MovieLens.
10. [Research] A "mitigate" prompt (match the user's average popularity level, blockbusters for blockbuster watchers and niche for niche watchers) reduced bias only slightly for most models. The exception was claude-2.1, which went from 0.377 to 0.181. HR@10 also usually dropped (gpt-4 from 0.081 to 0.065).
11. [Research] A "minimize" prompt ("Recommend indie, niche, or less well-known movies, avoiding mainstream blockbusters.") pushed every model to negative bias (gpt-4 −0.972, gpt-3.5 −0.775), but accuracy collapsed (gpt-4 HR@10 0.005) and unmatched items rose (gpt-3.5 3.39 per 10). Some of that penalty may be artifact, because niche titles may simply be missing from the 10k-movie catalog.
12. [Research] Metric agreement (Kendall's τ over 16 settings): log popularity difference and popularity lift correlate at 0.97, while Gini and Herfindahl correlate negatively with them. These two metric families measure different things: average popularity versus popularity dispersion.
13. [Research] Stated improvements and open problems: fine-tuning, grounding in a catalog via RAG "to avoid hallucinations", chain-of-thought or automatic prompt optimization to find a middle-ground debiasing prompt, evaluation of intent-based recommendations, and temporal "rich-get-richer" dynamics, which were not modeled.
14. [Research] LLMs enable intent-based requests such as "I want to watch a New York gangster movie featuring a female lead role" and cross-content-type generalization. The authors note that movies are especially well covered in web training data.
15. [Inferred] For a personal show/movie/anime recommender, the user's own history is the baseline to calibrate against. A moderate instruction to match the popularity level of the history is safer than "avoid mainstream", which trades away accuracy and increases invalid titles.
16. [Inferred] Every title should be checked against a real catalog or database after generation. Even at temperature 0, about 6–14% of outputs were unusable, and the share rises when the model is pushed toward niche items, which is where an anime or deep-cut recommender will often be asked to go.

## Limitations or bias
- Single domain and dataset (MovieLens 10M, movies up to 2008). Hit rate against held-out ratings undercounts good niche recommendations that fall outside the catalog.
- Older models (2023-era). A deliberately naive prompt with no catalog, no RAG and no chain of thought. The LLMs' accuracy is far below CF, so their lower bias partly comes with weak relevance.
- Workshop paper. Authors are Amazon employees, but no product claims are made. The temporal feedback loop was not studied.
