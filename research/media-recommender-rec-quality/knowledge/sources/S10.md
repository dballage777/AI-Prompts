## Learned points
1. [Documented] Two common kinds of recommendation: home page recommendations, personalized so "Every user sees different recommendations", and related-item recommendations (items similar to the one being viewed).
2. [Documented] Why recommend instead of relying on search: recommendations surface items users "might not have thought to search for on their own." Google states that 40% of app installs on Google Play and 60% of watch time on YouTube come from recommendations. These are vendor claims with no date or method given.
3. [Documented] Terminology: items (also called documents) are what gets recommended. The query (also called context) is the information used to recommend: user ID, items previously interacted with, plus context such as time of day and device. An embedding maps queries and items into a shared vector space.
4. [Documented] Standard three-stage architecture: candidate generation (YouTube reduces billions of videos to hundreds or thousands, possibly with several generators), scoring (a more precise model ranks to about 10 items to display), and re-ranking (applies constraints such as removing explicitly disliked items, boosting fresher content, and ensuring diversity, freshness, and fairness).
5. [Documented] Content-based filtering "Uses similarity between items to recommend items similar to what the user likes". Example: two cute-cat videos lead to cute-animal videos. The user is represented in the same feature space through explicit choices (profile settings) and implicit signals (past installs). The worked example scores items by dot product, which counts shared features.
6. [Documented] Content-based pros: it needs no data on other users, scales easily, and "can recommend niche items that very few other users are interested in." Cons: it needs hand-engineered features and a lot of domain knowledge, and "can only make recommendations based on existing interests of the user", so it has limited ability to expand them.
7. [Documented] Collaborative filtering uses similarities between users and items at the same time ("If user A is similar to user B, and user B likes video 1..."). This allows serendipitous recommendations and learns embeddings automatically. Feedback can be explicit (ratings) or implicit (watching). The movie example learns a children-vs-adult axis and then a blockbuster-vs-arthouse axis as embedding dimensions.
8. [Documented] CF cons: it cannot handle fresh items (the cold-start problem). Remedies: WALS projection (solve for a new item's embedding from a few interactions without retraining) and heuristics such as averaging the embeddings of items from the same category or uploader. It is also hard to include side features (such as country or age) without a generalized WALS block matrix.
9. [Documented] Similarity measures and popularity: the dot product is sensitive to embedding norm, and frequent (popular) items tend to have large norms, so "the popular items may end up dominating the recommendations". Cosine ignores the norm. A tempered variant ‖q‖^α‖x‖^α cos(q,x) with α in (0,1) puts less emphasis on the norm. Rare items given a large initial norm can be wrongly over-recommended, so initialization and regularization need care.
10. [Documented] Scoring: candidates from several generators (matrix factorization, personalization features, local vs distant items, popular/trending, social graph) go into one pool scored by a single model, because generators' scores are not comparable and a smaller pool allows a richer model.
11. [Documented] The objective choice works like a "mischievous genie." Maximizing clicks leads to click-bait, and maximizing watch time leads to overly long videos. The course suggests increasing diversity and maximizing session watch time instead. Positional bias: lower items get fewer clicks, so create position-independent rankings and score "as if they are in the top position."
12. [Documented] Re-ranking techniques: filters (for example, a click-bait classifier that removes items) and manual score transforms (for example, by item age or length). Freshness: retrain often with warm starts, use an "average" user (or clusters of users) for new users, use DNN models that accept unseen items, and add document age as a feature.
13. [Documented] Diversity: always recommending the items closest to the query gives near-duplicates ("nothing but owl videos") and a "bad or boring user experience." Fixes: multiple candidate generators from different sources, multiple rankers with different objectives, and re-ranking by genre or other metadata. Fairness: diverse teams, comprehensive data, per-demographic metrics, and separate models for underserved groups.
14. [Inferred] A prompt-based personal recommender can copy this pipeline. Generate candidates from several "sources" (similar to recent favourites, a creator or studio you like, trending or new this season, a wildcard outside your usual genres). Then score them against the taste profile. Then re-rank: drop seen and disliked titles, enforce genre or format variety, and boost new releases.
15. [Inferred] Be explicit about what the recommender optimizes. Something like "titles I'm likely to finish and rate highly" avoids an LLM equivalent of click-bait (hyped or popular-but-mismatched picks). An LLM's popularity pull is analogous to the dot-product norm effect and needs a deliberate counterweight.

## Limitations or bias
- An introductory course aimed at large-scale ML systems (YouTube, Play), focused on embeddings and trained models. It says nothing about LLM recommenders or conversational recommendation.
- The 40% and 60% figures are undated vendor claims with no method given.
- Fairness guidance is brief and generic. Novelty and serendipity are mentioned only in passing (as CF advantages).

## Summary-only items
- Matrix factorization, softmax, DNN retrieval, and training pages were not read. Only their links and the summaries above were seen.

## Best used for
- Standard vocabulary (items, query/context, embeddings, candidate generation, scoring, re-ranking), the content-based vs collaborative trade-offs, cold-start remedies, and practical re-ranking levers for freshness, diversity, and fairness, which map onto the stages of a prompt pipeline.
