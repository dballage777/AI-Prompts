## Learned points
1. What the Index is: a searchable database of court opinions on fair use, organized by category and type of use (for example music, internet/digitization, parody). It was created by the Office of the Register to support the 2013 IPEC Joint Strategic Plan on Intellectual Property Enforcement. — Documented
2. Coverage: decisions from the US Supreme Court, circuit courts of appeal, and district courts. Each entry gives a brief summary of facts, questions presented, and whether the use was found fair. It usually reflects only the highest court decision in a case. — Documented
3. What it is not: it is not complete ("does not include all judicial opinions on fair use"), it does not contain the opinions themselves (only citations), and it is "not a substitute for legal advice". — Documented
4. The Copyright Office cannot give individuals specific legal advice on fair use (37 C.F.R. 201.2(a)(3)). For a specific use, it advises consulting an attorney. — Documented
5. Fair use is defined as a doctrine allowing unlicensed use of copyrighted works in certain circumstances. It is codified in Section 107 of the 1976 Copyright Act. Example purposes include criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, and research. — Documented
6. Factor 1, purpose and character: nonprofit educational and noncommercial uses are more likely fair, but not automatically. Commercial uses are not automatically unfair. "Transformative" uses (adding something new, with a further purpose, not substituting for the original) are more likely fair. — Documented
7. Factor 2, nature of the work: more creative works (novel, movie, song) are less likely to support fair use than factual works. Unpublished works are less likely fair. — Documented
8. Factor 3, amount and substantiality: both quantity and quality count. A large portion makes fair use less likely and a small amount more likely. Even a small "heart" of the work can weigh against fair use, while some courts have allowed entire works. — Documented
9. Factor 4, market effect: courts ask whether the use harms the existing or future market (for example by displacing sales) and whether it would cause substantial harm if it became widespread. — Documented
10. Courts may consider other factors, and decide case by case on the facts. There is no safe-harbor number of words, lines, pages, or copies. — Documented
11. Relevance to Bible quoting (inferred): publisher "fair-use" verse limits (for example a 500-verse rule) are the publisher's own permission policies, which grant a license. They are distinct from statutory fair use under Section 107, which has no numeric threshold. A bot prompt should rely on the publisher's stated terms or on public-domain texts, and should not claim that a quote is "fair use" as a legal conclusion. — Inferred
12. Relevance to AI (inferred): an app that reproduces modern translations at scale could raise factor 3 and 4 concerns (substitution for the Bible product). That favors short quotes or public-domain translations. This page itself says nothing about AI. — Inferred

## Limitations / bias
- This is general US law guidance. It says nothing about Bible translations, religious use, or AI.
- The searchable case database (fair-index.html) and the FAQ were not opened. Only the landing page and factor summary were read.
- It is not legal advice by its own terms. Any bot or product decisions on copyright should be confirmed by counsel.

## Best for
- An authoritative statement that statutory fair use has no fixed verse or word count, which is why a bot should follow publisher permission terms or default to public-domain translations rather than claim "fair use".
