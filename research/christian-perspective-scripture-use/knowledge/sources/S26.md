## Learned points
1. Infobox lists the KJV copyright status as "Perpetually copyrighted in UK" and "De facto public domain elsewhere"; note [b] says it "has publication restrictions in the United Kingdom". — Documented
2. The section opens by saying the Authorised Version is in the public domain "in most of the world". The page does NOT state US status in so many words. US public-domain status is consistent with "elsewhere" but would need a US primary source to confirm. — Documented / Inferred
3. In the UK, the right to print, publish and distribute the KJV is a royal prerogative. The Crown licenses publishers under letters patent. — Documented (cites ref 215: UK House of Commons Library, "The royal prerogative and ministerial advice", PDF)
4. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland the letters patent are held by the King's Printer, and in Scotland by the Scottish Bible Board. The earliest known reference to the King's Printer's Bible right is from 1577. — Documented (ref 216: Metzger & Coogan 1993, p. 618)
5. The King's Printer is now Cambridge University Press, which inherited the right when it took over Eyre & Spottiswoode in 1990. Separate royal charters let Cambridge and Oxford University Presses print the KJV independently. In Scotland, Collins publishes it under licence from the Scottish Bible Board. — Documented (ref 216)
6. The letters patent bar anyone other than the holders, or those they authorise, from printing, publishing or importing the KJV into the UK. Wikipedia calls this the last remnant of the Crown's printing monopoly; the Book of Common Prayer has the same protection. — Documented (ref 216)
7. Crown copyright usually expires 50 years after publication, but s.171(b) of the UK Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 preserves "any right or privilege of the Crown". Editors have flagged this sentence "[clarification needed]". — Documented (ref 217: legislation.gov.uk, CDPA 1988 s.171)
8. Within the UK, Cambridge University Press permits at most 500 words for "liturgical and non-commercial educational use". Its prescribed acknowledgement must be included, the quote must be no more than 25% of the quoting publication, and it must not be a complete Bible book. — Documented (ref 218: Cambridge University Press "Bibles" page, retrieved 9 Oct 2025)
9. Beyond that limit, the Press considers permission case by case. A 2011 spokesman said it generally does not charge a fee but tries to ensure a reputable source text. — Reported (refs 219 Daily Telegraph 2011, 220 CUP "The Queen's Printer's Patent")
10. The page reports that the KJV is still "the most used translation in the United States", citing a 2014 Lifeway Research article, and that it has been gradually overtaken by modern versions (NIV, NRSV, ESV). — Reported
11. Some American publishers use the 1769 text with updated American spelling, so KJV texts in circulation vary slightly. — Documented
12. Historical context: in the 18th century, American colonial printers (beginning with Samuel Kneeland in 1752) printed the KJV without Crown authorisation. — Reported
13. Implication for a US bot: quoting the KJV in a US-facing product needs no publisher permission or notice. A UK-facing product is subject to the Crown patent, and the CUP limits above apply to UK reproduction. — Inferred
