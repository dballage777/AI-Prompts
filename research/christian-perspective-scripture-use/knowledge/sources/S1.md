## Learned points
1. Faith leaders are described as public-health "gatekeepers" or "first responders" for mental health and substance use problems. People often turn to a faith leader first, so the role is to reduce stigma and help people reach treatment, not to replace it. — [Documented]
2. The guide's framework is Notice. Talk. Act.®: NOTICE early signs, TALK with individuals and the community about mental health, ACT to connect people with appropriate support services. — [Documented]
3. Assess three things: level of distress, possibility of danger (including thoughts of suicide or risky behavior), and level of functioning (can the person care for themselves, solve problems, make decisions). — [Documented]
4. Crisis rule: "Thoughts of suicide should always be taken seriously." A person who is actively suicidal is a psychiatric emergency, so call 988. For emergencies, call 988 or go to the nearest hospital. Suicidal thoughts, intentions, plans, or behaviors call for immediate psychiatric evaluation. The guide repeats "call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org" several times. — [Documented]
5. The 988 Lifeline is described as 24/7, free, and confidential (call, text, chat), with specialized routes: Veterans Crisis Lifeline, Spanish (voice/text/chat), LGBTQ+ (text "PRIDE" to 988 or press 3), and ASL by videophone. — [Documented]
6. Suicide warning signs listed: talking or writing about death; hopeless, helpless, or worthless comments; "no reason for living" ("It would be better if I wasn't here", "I want out"); more alcohol or drug use; withdrawal; reckless behavior; dramatic mood changes; giving away possessions or changing a will. Risk factors include losses (relationship, death, legal or financial trouble, bullying), a previous attempt, trauma, firearms in the home, chronic illness or pain, and family history. — [Documented]
7. Data cited (CDC/SAMHSA): 22.8% of U.S. adults have a mental illness in a year; 16.5% of people 12+ have a substance use disorder; suicide is the 11th leading cause of death, with about 48,000 deaths and 1.5 million attempts a year; men are four times more likely than women to die by suicide; the youth (10–24) suicide rate rose 62% from 2007 to 2021; about 7% of adults have major depression each year; nearly 30% will have an anxiety disorder in their lifetime; three-fourths of mental illnesses begin by age 24. — [Research]
8. Language guidance: convey that mental illness and substance use "are not spiritual weaknesses" but treatable conditions. Use language that frames mental illness as a medical condition, like high blood pressure. "The person may have an illness; the person is not the illness." Mental illness is not caused by personal weakness or lack of character and is not anyone's fault. — [Documented]
9. Distinguishing spiritual problems from mental illness: clinical needs and spiritual concerns are "often inextricably intertwined." Common spiritual distress includes "has God forsaken me? why doesn't God heal me? Is taking medication evidence of a lack of faith?" Mental health conditions can show up as religious concerns, for example an "unpardonable" sin, vocational indecision, or unanswered prayers. Religious-themed experiences (messages from God, punishment for sin, possession by evil spirits) may be symptoms such as delusions or hallucinations, a spiritual problem, or both. — [Documented]
10. The DSM-5-TR lists "Religious or Spiritual Problem" (loss or questioning of faith, conversion problems, questioning spiritual values) as a non-disorder condition that may be a focus of clinical attention. — [Documented]
11. C.A.R.E. model for faith leaders: Consult your faith group's pastoral-care policies and boundaries; Attend to the person's language; Resist assuming a complex situation is purely spiritual; Encourage professional help for urgent issues (suicidal intent, despondency, self-harm or harm-to-others impulses) with immediate referral, while assuring ongoing spiritual support. — [Documented]
12. Referral timing: if issues persist after 4 to 6 sessions of spiritual counsel with hopelessness, undiminished distress, or more dysfunction, refer to a clinician. Elsewhere the guide gives 6 to 8 sessions without meaningful improvement as a referral trigger. Other triggers include prolonged grief, substance misuse, family dysfunction, significant sleep changes, isolation or having no one to confide in, and eating disorders or self-harm (prompt or immediate referral). — [Documented]
13. How to refer: make it collaborative ("Let us think together about the helping resources that will be of most value to you"), be clear about the difference between spiritual support and clinical care, reassure the person you will "journey with them," explore barriers (fears, stigma, religious misunderstandings, cost, insurance), keep a list of professionals, help with appointments, and follow up. — [Documented]
14. How faith can harm or hinder: religious concepts can fuel resistance to treatment, such as "depend on God" for healing, seeing psychiatric care as a "lack of faith," or reading symptoms as a "curse" or "punishment from God." The suggested reframe is that God has given us the ability to develop medicines that help keep us well. — [Documented]
15. How faith can help: faith stories of people finding a way forward when there seems to be no way can build hope. A leader's own confidence that the person can take the next step helps, as does conveying that each person "is sacred… of extreme value… loved ultimately." Hopelessness itself can be a symptom. — [Documented]
16. Evidence claim: people involved in a religious or spiritual group have a lower risk of premature death or illness related to mental health. The reasons are "not well understood," and fellowship and emotional support may contribute. Pastoral counseling can add to therapy or medication but does not replace them. — [Research]
17. Faith-integrated care is described as beneficial when it integrates the community's spiritual practices and beliefs "if requested," which offers congruence between faith and treatment. This includes medication adherence. — [Documented]
18. Boundaries and confidentiality: pastoral care and professional counseling must be kept separate, privacy emphasized, and dual relationships watched for. Practical support ideas include visits, prayers at services, cards, listening, meals, transport, childcare, and linking to advocacy groups. — [Documented]
19. Lifestyle supports endorsed: physical activity, nutrition, mind-body and mindfulness or meditation practices, restorative sleep, and social connection. The advice is to build "small sustainable habits." CBT is named as a treatment for persistent sleep problems. — [Documented]
20. The guide is multi-faith: the resources include Jewish, Muslim, Buddhist (Kalyana Mitta), and interfaith mental health networks. It addresses faith leaders generally rather than one denomination. — [Documented]
21. Faith leader self-care: it covers compassion fatigue and burnout (numbness, irritability, poor sleep, detachment). Strategies include boundaries, peer support or supervision, and mindfulness. — [Documented]
22. Implication for an AI bot: the bot can play the "Notice-Talk-Act" supportive role. It should frame mental illness as medical, never as a spiritual failure. It should gently counter "medication = lack of faith" beliefs. It should treat any suicidal content as an emergency (988/911 or ER) and stay supportive while urging professional care. — [Inferred]

## Limitations / bias
- Written for human faith leaders in congregations, not for AI or apps. Session-count referral thresholds (4–6 and 6–8) are for in-person pastoral counseling and are inconsistent with each other within the guide.
- Produced by a psychiatric organization, so it leans toward medical-model and medication framing. Multi-faith rather than specifically Christian.
- Statistics are secondary citations (CDC, SAMHSA, NIDA). The religion-mortality claim gives no effect size.
- Two-column PDF extraction can garble ordering. Quotes were checked against linear text extraction.

## Best for
- Crisis rules (988/ER), referral triggers, the spiritual-vs-clinical boundary, and non-stigmatizing faith language for a Christian wellness bot.
