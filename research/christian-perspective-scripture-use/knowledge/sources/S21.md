## Learned points
1. FaithNet is described as an interfaith resource network of NAMI members, friends, clergy and congregations "of all faith traditions"; it explicitly says it "is not a religious network" but reaches out to all religious organizations. A Christian bot can cite NAMI as a neutral, multi-faith bridge resource. — [Documented]
2. NAMI frames faith as helpful for "many" (not all): FaithNet promotes "the vital role of spirituality in the recovery journeys of many ... for whom faith is a key component." Supports framing faith as an optional resource, not a requirement for recovery. — [Documented]
3. Every NAMI page carries crisis/help lines: NAMI HelpLine 800-950-6264 (M–F, 10 a.m.–10 p.m. ET), text "NAMI" to 62640, and "In a crisis, call or text 988 (24/7)." Useful as a non-crisis referral alongside 988. — [Documented]
4. "Things to Avoid Saying" list includes "Just pray about it," "You just need to change your attitude," "Everyone feels that way sometimes," "Yes, we all feel a little crazy now and then." A Christian bot should never reduce a mental-health concern to "just pray." — [Documented]
5. Things to avoid doing: criticizing/blaming, talking too much or too fast, hostility, assuming things about the person, sarcasm or jokes about their condition, patronizing. — [Documented]
6. Recommended approach: ease in gradually, calm and straightforward, one topic at a time, reflective listening (example given), "I" statements rather than "you" statements, avoid prying, don't press, respect how the person describes their own symptoms. — [Documented]
7. Prayer is offered only with consent and "if appropriate": suggested phrasing "How can I help?" and "Can I pray with you now?"; prayer-list additions only "With their permission." Maps to an opt-in prayer offer in a bot. — [Documented]
8. Congregational guidance: person-first language ("Never define them by their illness"), do not assume the person wants inclusion (privacy), ask what would help, "Instead of trying to fix their problems, just listen," don't belittle ("suck it up"), learn to recognize symptoms, invite mental-health leaders to speak to reduce stigma. — [Documented]
9. NAMI warns some churches "view mental illness as a moral failing for which prayer is the only treatment" while others run outreach programs; advises finding a community where you feel welcomed. Signals a harm to avoid: implying illness is sin or that prayer replaces treatment. — [Documented]
10. Faith & Spirituality page lists ways faith can help: contemplative prayer/meditation ("10-20 minutes of meditation" reported beneficial by many), togetherness/structured social activity, a framework for meaning and the "why me?" question, and helping others/volunteering to improve mood and reduce anxiety. Stated as anecdotal ("Many people have found"), no study numbers given. — [Reported]
11. Pairing Scripture with coping: NAMI names "meditating on Bible passages (Protestant Christianity)" and Catholic rosary as contemplative practices that can invoke a relaxation response; other faiths' practices listed side by side (Judaism, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism). — [Documented]
12. For people alienated from faith or uncomfortable with organized religion, NAMI suggests secular sources of meaning (nature, art, music, poetry, science, history) — a model for respecting users of no faith. Also: "Learning about a faith doesn't obligate you to follow it." — [Documented]
13. Suggests that a person with a condition meet privately with their pastor/priest/religious leader to share challenges, and that local NAMI Affiliates often have relationships with area faith communities (clergy as supportive partner, not replacement for care). — [Documented]
14. The "Quotes, Verses and Passages" page is a ready-made list of comfort/compassion verses (e.g., Romans 15:13, Joshua 1:9, Psalm 46:1-3, 1 Peter 5:7, Isaiah 41:10, Galatians 6:2, Matthew 25:35-36), mostly labeled NIV, plus one NRSV and a quote from psychiatrist Judith Lewis Herman on recovery requiring relationships. — [Documented]
15. That verse page quotes NIV text without any Biblica copyright notice visible in the retrieved text; at least two references appear mis-cited (the "Two are better than one" text is labeled Ecclesiastes 4:5; "plans to give you hope and a future" is labeled Jeremiah 29:27). Do not copy references from this page unverified. — [Inferred]
16. The prayers page offers Christian liturgical-style prayers "For the Sick Person," "For Recovery," "For Strength and Confidence," and for mental-health care providers, that pair asking God for healing with honoring clinicians' work. — [Documented]
17. FaithNet work is mostly done at affiliate level; NAMI offers leaders mini grants, presentations, scripts and guides, plus a printable Awareness Bulletin (PDFs not read). — [Documented]

## Limitations / bias
- Guidance is practical advice for congregations and peers, not a research review; benefit claims are anecdotal with no study citations or numbers.
- Pages are undated in visible text; Awareness Bulletin PDFs, Advisory Group, Pathways to Hope and Sample Sermons pages were not read.
- Verse list uses a copyrighted translation (NIV) without a visible notice and contains apparent citation errors, so it is not a model for quotation practice.
- Written for faith communities and families, not for AI or digital tools.
## Best for
- Concrete do/don't phrasing for faith-sensitive support ("never 'just pray about it'", consent before prayer, listen first) plus NAMI HelpLine and 988 referral details.
