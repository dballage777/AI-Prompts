## Learned points
1. "Deaths of despair" (term from Case and Deaton, 2015) means deaths from suicide, drug use, and alcohol poisoning. These deaths often share a context of hopelessness. — Documented
2. Context: these deaths rose sharply, especially among white adults without a college degree. US life expectancy fell three years in a row (2015, 2016, 2017), the longest consecutive decline since World War I. — Reported
3. Study design: a prospective observational cohort analysis that tested whether religious service attendance was associated with later death from suicide, drug overdose, or alcohol. It is published as Chen, Koh, Kawachi, Botticelli and VanderWeele in JAMA Psychiatry (listed "in press", DOI 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2020.0175). — Research
4. Sample: 66,492 women from the Nurses' Health Study II and 43,141 men from the Health Professionals Follow-Up Study, all US health professionals. — Research
5. Women: attendance was measured in 2001 and follow-up ran through 2017 (16 years). Those attending at least weekly had 68 percent lower likelihood of these deaths than those who never attended. — Research
6. Men: followed 1988–2014 (26 years). Weekly attenders had 33 percent lower likelihood than those who never attended. — Research
7. Controls included age, race, region, income, health status, health behaviors, smoking, mental health, other social support, and other variables. Results were "quite consistent" across sensitivity analyses. — Research
8. Causation caveat: the study "was not able to examine the mechanisms". The post reports associations. Proposed mechanisms (social support, purpose, teachings on the worth of life and against suicide, care for the body, hope in God, meaning in suffering) are labelled plausible, not tested. — Documented
9. Generalizability caveat: the sample was health professionals. The author says whether the pattern holds for working-class or economically stressed groups needs further work. The page reports percentage reductions only; no hazard ratios or confidence intervals are given. — Documented
10. The author argues religious community may beat other social support because it adds purpose, hope, and meaning, and says this is "for those with religious commitments". He also says other forms of community participation are valuable. — Reported
11. He frames religious community as one resource among several, alongside economic responses and "potential therapeutic approaches", not as a replacement for them. — Documented
12. Clinicians themselves show higher suicide rates than the general public (cited from other studies, not this one), with burnout as a risk context. — Reported
13. Implication for a bot: it may say that research links regular communal worship with lower rates of these deaths. It should frame this as association, encourage (not pressure) connection to a faith community or other community, and never present faith as a cure or as a reason to skip treatment. It should never imply that people who do not attend are at fault. — Inferred

## Limitations / bias
- This is a blog summary by the lead investigator, so the tone is advocacy-leaning (it uses "seemed to prevent" while the design is observational). The JAMA Psychiatry paper itself was not read.
- The sample is mostly health professionals and largely white. It may not generalize to all US adults.
- The measure is service attendance (communal practice), not private belief or Scripture reading. It does not show that quoting Scripture to users helps.
- Possible reverse causation or healthy-attender effects cannot be fully excluded by observational data.

## Best for
- Accurately worded, caveated evidence that communal religious participation is associated with fewer suicide, overdose, and alcohol deaths, so the bot can encourage community without overclaiming.
