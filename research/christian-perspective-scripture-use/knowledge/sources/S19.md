## Learned points
1. Self-description: a Christian mental health organization offering free, "evidence-informed" recovery and support groups, resources, and church-based mental health ministry training, so churches become "mental health-ready". Its mission line is "blending science and Scripture". — Documented
2. Framing device: mental health support is described as "a discipleship growth experience". It avoids the term "support group" because of stigma and calls groups growth-oriented. — Documented
3. Clear professional-care boundary: groups are peer- and lay-led, are not therapy, and are "designed to complement professional mental health treatment". Resources "are not intended to replace or discontinue" professional therapy and treatment. — Documented
4. The disclaimer says resources must not be used to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disorder, or to replace treatment by mental health or medical professionals. It calls them "biblical and educational resources" giving lay-level support. — Documented
5. MHGA does not offer personal coaching or consultations and does not give a referral list of professionals. — Documented
6. Programs: Living Grace (adults 18+), Redefine Grace (mid-to-late teens), Family Grace (family members and friends), Thrive (a deeper recovery course, also used in a coaching program), Living Hope (trauma groups), and clergy training "Healthy Minds 101". Also free workbooks, devotionals, help guides, a free online course, and leader training in English and Spanish. Curricula are available in English, Spanish, and Chinese. — Documented
7. Integration model: it describes a whole-person approach (physical, mental/emotional, relational, spiritual) and an "empowerment model" (discovery learning, "learned hopefulness", resiliency skills). It explicitly says "This is not a professional therapeutic intervention program". — Documented
8. Recovery is defined as "not a cure" but learning resilience to "thrive and flourish". Integrating Christ-centered Scripture "with proven mental health tools" is said to help manage symptoms and build resilience. This is a pairing model (Scripture plus skills), not Scripture alone. — Documented
9. It recognizes a severity spectrum, from situational stress to diagnosable conditions (MDD, GAD, bipolar). It says all levels "deserve community and professional support". A working definition of difficulties: prolonged adverse feelings or thoughts disrupting daily life "for two weeks or more". — Documented
10. Ecumenical stance: curricula are used across many denominations and it "value[s] an ecumenical approach". Doctrinal basis: Trinitarian, aligned with the Nicene and Apostles' Creeds. — Documented
11. Outreach to others: groups "often attract non-believers and other faith groups". Grace "is for all of God's creation", including the "unbelieving". The About page notes that some faith groups see mental illness as "a punishment, curse, etc.", which is a stigma to counter. — Documented
12. "Task-sharing" model: groups act as bridges between congregants and mental health professionals. Church leaders do not need to be psychology experts. — Documented
13. Reach claims (homepage): "since 2011", "30,000+ participants", "58+ countries". — Reported
14. Outcome claims: studies (said to be published in "four journals") report reduced symptoms, resilience, faith renewal, and clergy confidence. Thrive is reported to improve depression, anxiety, stress, and spiritual well-being. A Hong Kong partner (BOKSS) reported 94% of surveyed participants with improved well-being. Refugee trauma symptoms reportedly fell "from seven or more to less than two" in 10 weeks. Effect sizes, sample sizes, and citations are not given on the pages read. — Reported
15. The FAQ asserts that peer groups lead to "up to 90% growth" without a citation on the page. Treat it as unverified. — Reported
16. No crisis line (988 or 911) appeared on the pages read. A bot must supply its own crisis protocol rather than model this site. — Documented
17. Implication for a bot: copy MHGA's boundary language (complements care, is not therapy, does not diagnose) and its Scripture-plus-skills pairing. Do not copy its outcome claims as facts. — Inferred

## Limitations / bias
- The source promotes its own programs and is donor-funded. Outcome claims are self-reported, and study citations or effect sizes were not visible on the pages read.
- It is doctrinally Christian (creedal and Trinitarian). "Ecumenical" here means across Christian denominations, not interfaith.
- It is a group or curriculum model, not an AI or chat context.
- Some site text could not be verified (for example the "90% growth" claim).

## Best for
- A concrete, faith-friendly model of the "complements, never replaces professional care" boundary, and of pairing Scripture with practical resilience skills, in respectful, stigma-reducing language.
