## Learned points
1. Mission (home page): conduct research on religion, spirituality and health; train researchers; "Interpret the research for clinical and societal applications"; explore its meaning for pastors and theologians; and discuss how theological input can advance research. Founded 1998. — Documented (summary-only)
2. Clinician and faith resources: workshops on "Integrating Spirituality into Patient Care" (in person and on Zoom; sessions listed for Nov–Dec 2026); an annual research course (23rd, Aug 9–13, 2027); CME videos; the Crossroads newsletter; a military chaplain handbook on moral injury (2023); and book series for Christians, LGBTQ Christians, Jews, Muslims, Hindus, Buddhists, and non-believers. — Documented (summary-only)
3. Religiously integrated CBT (RCBT): a 10-session, 50-minute manualized CBT for depression in people with chronic medical illness. It is a "religious overlay on top of Beck's cognitive restructuring and behavior modification" and matches conventional CBT step by step. Free PDF manuals and therapist and participant workbooks are posted in Christian, Jewish, Muslim (Shia and Sunni, plus Urdu), Hindu, and Buddhist versions. The page does not state license terms. — Documented
4. Trial design (Research Summary): 70 religious adults aged 18–85 with major depression (BDI 10–40) and at least one chronic illness, randomized to RCBT or conventional CBT (target 30 completers per arm). Ten 50-minute sessions over 12 weeks by master's-level therapists, delivered by phone, Skype, or online. BDI measured at baseline and at weeks 4, 8, 12, and 24. — Documented (March 2014 design; results not in this document)
5. Rationale figures in the Summary (secondary, as cited there): 65% of Americans said religion is important in daily life (Gallup, January 2009); 56% said religion was "very important" (Pew survey of 35,000); nearly 90% of Duke Hospital medical patients used religion to cope, and 45% of those said it was the most important factor that keeps them going; 76% of depressed medical patients over 50 prayed at least daily; 77%–83% of depressed older adults with chronic illness would prefer religion included in therapy; online CBT dropout was 28% in a cited Lancet trial. — Research (cited, not re-verified)
6. Why integrate faith: many religious people "may shy away from secular psychotherapy because they perceive it as unsympathetic to their religious beliefs." Some feel that seeking therapy means "they have abandoned their faith," or feel guilty for being depressed. Religious CBT may "normalize" the need for therapy. The Summary also notes clergy are often reluctant to refer, and that therapy gains may not last if the faith community counteracts them. — Documented
7. Tailoring rule: "Faith issues will be considered only in terms of patients' beliefs and religious language." Before therapy, the therapist identifies the client's "religious language, symbols, and particular faith tradition," and that information guides integration. The Christian manual was adapted for other faiths with an interfaith council. — Documented
8. Pairing Scripture with CBT (Christian manual): session titles join a CBT skill to a faith theme. Session 2 is "Behavioral Activation: Walking by Faith"; Session 3 is "Identifying Unhelpful Thoughts: The Battlefield of the Mind"; Session 4 is "Challenging Unhelpful Thoughts: Bringing All Thoughts Captive"; then sessions on loss, spiritual struggles, gratitude, altruism and generosity, growth, and hope and relapse prevention. — Documented
9. Scripture method: (a) a weekly memory verse tied to the session topic, recited at the start of the next session; (b) about 20 minutes a day of contemplative prayer using the verse: read slowly, read again and ask where it touches your life, read a third time and listen, journal, then rest in silence; (c) use "religious beliefs and practices, as well as scriptures" to challenge unhelpful thoughts in the ABCDE thought log. Example memory verse: Isaiah 26:3 ("perfect peace… whose thoughts are fixed on You"). — Documented
10. Translation labeling: a search of the extracted Christian manual text found no named Bible translation (NIV, ESV, KJV, NRSV) attached to the quoted verses. — Documented (absence)
11. Session 6 assesses "spiritual struggles," for example whether the client feels God is punishing or abandoning them, and addresses them inside the CBT frame. — Documented (summary of manual text)
12. Safety in the manual: "Patients should be given emergency contact numbers before they begin treatment," and in an emergency the therapist must stabilize the patient and ensure safety. RCBT is delivered by trained therapists and is not a self-help tool. — Documented
13. Comparison arm: conventional-CBT therapists "gently redirect" religious topics toward secular approaches. This shows that the faith overlay is a choice made per client. — Documented
14. Publications: the site lists 18 RCBT papers (2012–2016), for example Koenig et al. 2015, *J Nerv Ment Dis* 203(4):243–251 (religious vs conventional CBT in chronic illness), and Pearce et al. 2015, *Psychotherapy* 52(1):56–66. The page gives citations only, no findings, so I record no efficacy numbers. — Documented (no outcome data retrieved)
15. Bot implications: (a) faith integration should mirror the user's own beliefs and language, not impose them; (b) Scripture works best as an add-on to a proven skill (thought records, behavioral activation, gratitude), not a replacement; (c) brief verse-plus-reflection exercises are a documented pattern; (d) offer a secular version when the user prefers it; (e) the structured version was clinician-delivered, so a bot should present such exercises as self-help encouragement and refer for depression treatment. — Inferred

## Limitations / bias
- I read no RCBT outcome results. Efficacy is unverified from this source.
- The Center and its funder (Templeton) promote religion-and-health research, which is a possible pro-integration bias.
- The trial population is narrow (chronically ill, depressed, religious, therapist-delivered), so results may not generalize to a self-help bot or a career audience.
- The Summary's statistics are dated (2009 Gallup and similar) and secondary.
- HTML pages were read via condensed extraction.

## Best for
- A documented model for pairing Scripture with CBT skills (memory verse, contemplative reading, thought challenging) while tailoring to the user's own faith language.
