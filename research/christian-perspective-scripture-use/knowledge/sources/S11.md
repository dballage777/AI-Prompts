## Learned points
1. Poll data on the program page (2024 APA Healthy Minds Poll): 60% say faith is an important factor in supporting their mental wellness; 57% would seek help from a faith leader for mental health concerns; 3 in 5 would seek mental health care if a faith leader recommended it; only 52% of adults in a religious community say their community discusses mental health "openly and without stigma." — Documented (summary-only)
2. Framing: the program says psychiatry and spirituality "often work hand in hand to form a support system." It runs the Mental Health and Faith Community Partnership, which brings psychiatrists and faith leaders together. — Documented (summary-only)
3. The guide calls faith leaders public-health "gatekeepers" or "first responders," because people "often turn first to a faith leader." Their role is to dispel misunderstandings, reduce stigma, and "facilitate access to treatment." The role is a bridge to care, not a substitute for it. — Documented
4. Notice. Talk. Act.® adapted for faith communities: NOTICE early signs of mental health concerns; TALK with individuals and the faith community about mental health; ACT to connect people with the support services they need. — Documented
5. Assessing a person (Notice step): level of distress, possibility of danger (including "thoughts of suicide or risky behavior"), and level of functioning. The guide prints a 988 call-out ("call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org") on several pages. — Documented
6. Crisis rule: "A person who is actively suicidal is a psychiatric emergency—call 988." Suicidal thoughts, intentions, plans, or behaviors "should be treated as emergency situations requiring immediate psychiatric evaluation/consultation." "For emergencies, call 988 or go to the nearest hospital." — Documented
7. Spiritual and clinical needs overlap. Clinical needs and spiritual concerns are "often inextricably intertwined." Typical faith-framed distress includes: "has God forsaken me? why doesn't God heal me? Is taking medication evidence of a lack of faith?" Faith leaders "should be clear about the difference between religious/spiritual support and professional clinical treatment." — Documented
8. C.A.R.E. model: Consult the pastoral-care policies of your denomination or faith group; Attend to the person's language, since mental health conditions are sometimes expressed as spiritual concerns (an "unpardonable" sin, vocational indecision, unanswered prayer); Resist assuming a complex situation is entirely spiritual; Encourage professional help when care is urgent. — Documented
9. Referral timing (numbers): if spiritual counsel is given and issues persist "after 4 to 6 sessions" with hopelessness, undiminished distress, or added dysfunction, refer to a clinician "with ongoing support from you." Elsewhere the guide lists "6-8 sessions without meaningful improvement" as a referral trigger. Immediate referral is needed for danger to self or others (severe aggression, out-of-control eating disorder, self-harm such as cutting) and for suicidal thoughts. — Documented
10. Other referral triggers: developmental problems in youth; prolonged grief; family dysfunction; substance misuse; significant sleep changes; a person who feels they have no one to confide in or has recently withdrawn from supports. — Documented
11. How to refer: make it collaborative ("Let us think together about the helping resources that will be of most value to you"); reassure the person you will "journey with them"; ask about barriers (fears, stigma, "religious misunderstandings," cost, insurance); compile a list of professionals; follow up and offer spiritual encouragement to stay the course. If the person is not ready, keep walking with them. — Documented
12. Faith-based resistance to treatment: people may "depend on God" for healing, see psychiatric care as a "lack of faith," or read symptoms as a "curse" or "punishment from God." The guide suggests affirming that "God has given us the ability to develop medicines that are helpful in keeping us well." — Documented
13. Hope: hopelessness can itself be a symptom. Faith stories "that illustrate how people have found a way forward when there seems to be no way can facilitate hope." Leaders can convey that each person "is sacred" and "loved ultimately." The person "is not the illness," and mental illness is not anyone's fault. — Documented
14. Language: convey that mental illness and substance use "are not spiritual weaknesses but conditions for which treatment is available." Use language that frames mental illness as a medical condition, "like high blood pressure." — Documented
15. Religious content can be a symptom. Experiences such as a message from "God," punishment for "sin," a "great holy cause," or possession by "evil spirits" may be symptoms (delusions, hallucinations, paranoia), a religious or spiritual problem, or both. The guide notes DSM-5-TR's "Religious or Spiritual Problem" category (loss or questioning of faith, problems with conversion), which is not a mental disorder. — Documented
16. Evidence claim: "Studies show" that people involved in a religious or spiritual group have a lower risk of premature death or mental-health-related illness, but "the reasons for this apparent benefit are not well understood." Pastoral counseling can be "an additional support to therapy and/or medication." — Research (no specific studies cited at that point)
17. Statistics in the guide: more than 1 in 5 (22.8%) US adults experience mental illness in a given year; 1 in 6 people aged 12 and older (16.5%) have a substance use disorder; the suicide rate for ages 10–24 rose 62% from 2007 to 2021; about 48,000 suicide deaths and 1.5 million attempts a year (CDC, data for 2020–2022); suicide is the 11th leading cause of death; 68% of adults with mental disorders also have medical conditions, and 29% of adults with medical conditions also have mental disorders. — Documented (secondary citations)
18. 988 details in the guide: 24/7, free, confidential support by call, text, and chat; LGBTQ+ youth can text "PRIDE" to 988 or press "3"; ASL users can use videophones. — Documented
19. Inclusion: the guide's examples are multi-faith (for example, it names a Buddhist "Kalyana Mitta" alongside clergy, deacons, and chaplains). The Faith Resources PDF lists Christian, Catholic, Jewish, Muslim, interfaith, LGBTQ (Group for the Advancement of Psychiatry toolkit), and BIPOC resources. It also lists NAMI FaithNet, Pathways to Promise, the Action Alliance's Faith.Hope.Life., SAMHSA's faith-based engagement pages, and the NAMI HelpLine 800-950-NAMI. — Documented
20. Privacy and helper care: the guide stresses individual and family privacy when congregants help, and includes a section on compassion fatigue and burnout for faith leaders. — Documented
21. Bot implications: a Christian wellness bot should act like the guide's ideal faith helper. It notices distress, talks openly without stigma, and acts by connecting to care. It says plainly that it offers spiritual encouragement, not clinical treatment. It treats medication and therapy as compatible with faith. It never frames symptoms as sin, curse, or weak faith. It escalates suicidal content to 988 immediately, and it keeps offering encouragement after a referral. — Inferred

## Limitations / bias
- Written for human faith leaders, not for AI. The application to a bot is my inference.
- The authors are a psychiatry body, so the guide leans toward clinical referral. Its referral session counts (4–6 vs 6–8) are not consistent and have no cited evidence.
- The web pages were read through a condensed extraction; poll figures come from that extraction. The underlying poll methodology was not read.
- The guide is multi-faith, not specifically Christian or Protestant.

## Best for
- Concrete faith-leader rules a bot can adopt: separating spiritual support from clinical care, referral triggers, collaborative referral wording, and how to answer "is medication a lack of faith?"
