## Learned points
1. Scope and method: a review of quantitative studies summarized in the two editions of the Handbook of Religion and Health: over 1,200 studies from 1872–2000 and 2,100 from 2000–2010; estimated to capture about 75% of published research. Quality rated 0–10 by the author alone; agreement with an independent rater on 75 studies was moderate (r = 0.57; kappa 0.49; agreement in 56/75). Religion and spirituality are used interchangeably ("R/S"). — Research
2. Coping: 454 studies (110 before 2000, 344 from 2000–2010) on R/S and coping across illness, bereavement, disasters, war and other stress; "In the overwhelming majority of studies, people reported that R/S was helpful." — Research
3. Depression: of at least 444 studies, 272 (61%) found less depression with more R/S and 28 (6%) found more depression. Of the 178 most rigorous, 119 (67%) inverse and 13 (7%) positive. Of 70 prospective cohort studies, 39 (56%) found R/S predicted lower depression or faster remission; 7 (10%) predicted worse depression. Of 30 clinical trials, 19 (63%) found R/S interventions beat standard treatment or control; 2 (7%) found standard treatment superior. — Research
4. Effect size: a 2003 independent review (147 studies, 98,975 subjects) found an average R/S–depression correlation of −0.10, small but similar to the effect of gender on depression, and 50% stronger in stressed than nonstressed populations. — Research
5. Suicide: of at least 141 studies, 106 (75%) found inverse relationships and 4 (3%) positive. Of 49 most rigorous, 39 (80%) found less suicide, fewer attempts or more negative attitudes toward suicide among the more R/S; 2 (4%) found positive relationships, one in college students "distressed over R/S concerns." — Research
6. Anxiety: of at least 299 studies, 147 (49%) inverse and 33 (11%) found more anxiety in the more R/S (31 of those 33 cross-sectional, so direction unclear). Of 67 high-quality studies, 38 (55%) inverse and 7 (10%) positive. Of 32 randomized trials, 22 (69%) found R/S interventions reduced anxiety more than standard care or control; 1 (3%) found increased anxiety (people with severe alcohol dependence). — Research
7. Positive outcomes: well-being 256/326 (79%) positive, 3 (<1%) inverse; hope 29/40 (73%) positive, none inverse; optimism 26/32 (81%); meaning and purpose 42/45 (93%) positive; self-esteem 42/69 (61%) higher, 2 (3%) lower; social support 61/74 (82%) positive, none inverse. — Research
8. Psychosis is mixed: of 43 studies, 14 (33%) inverse and 10 (23%) positive (for example, religion linked with religious delusions); bipolar disorder participants described struggles to separate genuine spiritual experience from hyperreligiosity; a case of mania after Eastern meditation is noted. — Research
9. Mechanisms: religious beliefs give meaning, purpose, an optimistic worldview, a sense of control (including via prayer), answers to existential questions, and role models of suffering "often illustrated in religious scriptures"; doctrines reduce stressful life events; emphasis on love, compassion, altruism, forgiveness and gratitude builds social support and positive emotions. — Reported (author's model)
10. Harms the paper names: religion may justify hatred and exclusion; enable control of vulnerable people (cults); foster rigid thinking and obsessive practices; produce anxiety, fear and excessive guilt; create strain from failing high standards; enable escape from family problems; and "delay diagnosis and effective mental health care." The anxiety section notes R/S can raise fear through threats of punishment and damnation. "R/S is not a panacea." — Reported
11. Spiritual struggle: unmet spiritual needs, "especially if they involve R/S struggles, can adversely affect health and may increase mortality." The paper does not use the term "negative religious coping" and gives no separate numbers on it. — Documented (term absent)
12. Prevalence of use: in some US areas, 90% of hospitalized patients use religion to cope and over 40% say it is their primary coping behavior. A national survey found 56% of US psychiatrists never, rarely or only sometimes ask about R/S in depression or anxiety; about 10% of US physicians "often or always" take a spiritual history. — Research
13. Clinical step 1, spiritual history: a brief (two-minute) screen covering (1) religious background, (2) role of beliefs in coping (or causing distress), (3) beliefs that may conflict with care, (4) participation in a supportive spiritual community, (5) spiritual needs; if needs are found, refer to pastoral care/chaplain. Recording denomination alone is "NOT taking a spiritual history." — Documented
14. Step 2, respect beliefs: do not challenge beliefs, even if they conflict with treatment or seem pathological, at least initially; take a neutral posture and ask questions; consult or refer to a chaplain. Support beliefs that appear healthy. — Documented
15. Step 3, know your limits: most health professionals lack training to address spiritual needs; chaplains "are the true experts"; for "any but the most simple spiritual needs," refer to chaplains. — Documented
16. Step 4, patient-centered and never coercive: the patient decides whether to share or engage; professionals generally should not offer prayer themselves but may say they are open to it and let the patient initiate; if requested, ask what the patient wants prayer for, keep it short and private. — Documented
17. Step 5–7: the clinician's own belief or unbelief should not drive whether spirituality is addressed; learn the faith traditions of the people you serve; follow up after a chaplain referral and, with written consent, link to the patient's faith community. — Documented
18. Implications for a Christian-perspective bot: ask about faith rather than assume it; let the user lead on prayer or Scripture; never argue with or pressure beliefs; treat guilt, fear of punishment or spiritual distress as a reason for gentle care and referral to a pastor/chaplain and, where symptoms are present, a licensed clinician; do not let faith content delay professional help. — Inferred

## Limitations / bias
- Dated (2012; literature through mid-2010); newer meta-analyses may revise the numbers.
- Vote-counting review (counts of significant studies), mostly cross-sectional data; quality ratings by one rater with only moderate inter-rater agreement; author is a prominent advocate in the field.
- Written for clinicians in hospitals; the steps must be adapted for a non-clinical AI that cannot assess, document or follow up.
- Explicitly frames its mechanism model on Western monotheistic religions.
- Does not use or quantify "negative religious coping," and gives no explicit rule against "prescribing religion"; the closest are the non-coercion and patient-initiative rules.

## Best for
- Evidence numbers on faith and depression, anxiety and suicide, plus clinician rules (spiritual history, respect, no coercion, user-led prayer, chaplain referral) to adapt for the bot.
