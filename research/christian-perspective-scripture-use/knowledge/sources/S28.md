## Learned points
1. The author's limits table:
   - NIV, ESV, NLT, NKJV and NRSV: up to 500 verses, Scripture no more than 25% of the work, whole books may not be shared.
   - CSB, NASB and LSB: up to 1,000 verses, Scripture no more than 50% of the work, whole books may not be shared.
   - Verify against each publisher's own page. S27 says the ESV allows 1,000 verses. — Reported (secondary; image table)
2. The author's point: because Scripture can be only 25–50% of your work, plain Scripture can never be shared alone and must come with commentary. Even a whole short book (2 John, 13 verses) is excluded. — Reported / opinion
3. Quotes the ESV permission terms: no quoting in any work released under a Creative Commons license, and no translating into other languages. The author claims fair use or fair dealing still applies anyway. — Reported (quote of Crossway terms; legal claim is opinion)
4. Most countries have fair use or fair dealing exceptions. Per the author, publisher allowances do not go much beyond fair use and are sometimes stricter. — Reported / opinion (not legal advice)
5. Getting permission is hard:
   - The NIV non-commercial permission form has 49 fields, including one for a "distribution and marketing strategy".
   - 3 of 7 publishers the author contacted never replied.
   - Some publishers license only apps they judge "truly unique and innovative".
   — Reported (first-person)
6. Crossway let open-source Bible apps use the ESV for years and then withdrew permission to collect licensing fees, forcing apps to remove it. App-specific terms can change, which is a risk for a bot that embeds a copyrighted translation. — Reported
7. Online free availability (e.g., Bible Gateway) is partly a strategy to drive print sales. Bible Gateway printing maxes out at 10 chapters, and sharing printouts may be infringing. — Reported / opinion
8. The author was offered one translation for free distribution at $1,000 USD a year. Free app access usually involves royalties or ads, per the author. — Reported (first-person, unverified)
9. When the author asked for permission for an app, one publisher floated planting a deliberate error in the text to trace commercial misuse. — Reported (anecdote, publisher unnamed)
10. Ownership claims:
   - The NKJV is owned by Thomas Nelson, part of HarperCollins/News Corp.
   - Zondervan (News Corp) holds exclusive US commercial print rights to the NIV via Biblica.
   - Almost all publishers distribute through the Digital Bible Library.
   — Reported
11. Downloading translations for offline use in YouVersion collects users' name and country. The author raises a privacy concern for persecuted Christians. — Reported
12. The KJV is public domain in most countries, and malicious modifications have not been a widespread problem because many trustworthy copies exist. — Reported / opinion
13. Recommends the Berean Standard Bible (BSB) as a modern translation from the original languages, overseen by scholars and dedicated to the public domain in 2023. — Reported (check the BSB's own site)
14. Implication for an AI bot: default to public-domain texts (KJV; BSB if its public-domain dedication is confirmed). If a copyrighted modern translation is used, quote short passages, name the translation, and follow that publisher's current digital/app terms, because the 25%/50% "of the work" rules are hard to apply to chat output. — Inferred
