## Learned points
1. Origin: Saddleback's commitment grew after Rick and Kay Warren's son Matthew died by suicide on April 5, 2013, following a lifelong struggle with mental illness. The ministry aims to "extend the radical friendship of Jesus". — Documented
2. Scriptural basis: John 15:15, Galatians 5:13b and Romans 15:13. Their "Hope Circle" lists five truths: you are loved, you have a purpose, you belong, you have a choice, and you are needed. These are non-clinical, identity-affirming messages a bot could echo. — Documented
3. The kit states plainly that the faith community is not a substitute for professional mental health care, and recommends professional help to diagnose and manage illness. This is the core safety framing. — Documented
4. The kit treats mental illness as an illness that "often requires medication and treatment by mental health professionals". It calls it a misperception that faith communities therefore have nothing to offer, since the church cares for "body, mind, and soul". — Documented
5. Partnership model across five phases: managing life well, an intensifying crisis, an acute crisis, treatment/recovery, and back to baseline. Professional care and faith support both have roles at every phase.
   - Acute crisis: in-patient or residential treatment, with church support through visits, meals, childcare, prayer and cards.
   - Intensifying crisis: crisis stabilization, PHP/IOP, and more therapist and psychiatrist visits, plus support groups.
   — Documented
6. C.H.U.R.C.H. strategy: Care; Help with basic needs; Utilize volunteers; Remove stigma; Collaborate with the community (build a referral list of professionals, treatment programs, hospitals, food banks, housing, job training for crisis or emergency); offer Hope. Steps are graded as crawl, walk or run by cost and training. — Documented
7. The kit acknowledges that "the church is a necessary part of holistic care, but it can't do it all." — Documented
8. Anti-stigma theology:
   - "We believe it is not a sin to be sick."
   - In the testimony guidelines, lack of healing "is in no way a reflection of our spiritual life." — Documented
   - So a bot should never imply that illness or non-healing reflects weak faith. — Inferred
9. Safe-messaging rules for speakers (citing the American Association of Suicidology):
   - Share no details of suicide methods.
   - Say "died by suicide" or "took their own life", not "committed suicide" or "successful suicide".
   - Avoid "they/them/those" for people with mental illness.
   - Don't use "suicide" out of context.
   — Documented
10. Model: a community gathering for social connection and "accurate psychoeducation". The current site says it is quarterly and led by "non-professionals and volunteers with lived experience". The 2019 kit describes a monthly gathering (started April 2018) led by staff, licensed professionals, people with lived experience and volunteers. — Documented (discrepancy noted)
11. Statistics cited in the kit:
   - More than 50% of Americans will be diagnosed with a mental illness at some point.
   - 50% of people with a mental illness will not receive treatment.
   - People often turn first to clergy.
   - Annual depression screening is recommended for ages 12 and up.
   — Reported (kit cites NAMI, USPSTF/AAP and other footnotes)
12. Crisis guidance is outdated: the 2019 kit lists the "National Suicide Prevention Lifeline: 1-800-273-TALK (8255)". The current site pages show no 988 or crisis-line information, only the ministry phone and email. A bot must use 988 and 911 and should not copy crisis numbers from older faith materials. — Documented / Inferred
13. Recommended reading pairs clinical and faith works, e.g., *Grace for the Afflicted: A Clinical and Biblical Perspective on Mental Illness*, *The Body Keeps the Score*, *Try Softer*. — Documented
14. Support groups are framed as "Christ-centered communities", e.g., helping members "learn to trust in God's control and power". Celebrate Recovery is listed as a faith-based support option alongside professional care. — Documented

## Limitations / bias
- An evangelical megachurch ministry writing for church audiences, not a non-denominational or clinical body. Its language is explicitly Christian and not adapted for users of other faiths or none.
- The kit dates from 2019, and its crisis line is outdated (pre-988). Statistics are second-hand and the "first place they call is the church" claim is not sourced on the site.
- The site is thin. Much content links out to KayWarren.com, which was not read.
## Best for
- A model for pairing faith support with professional care (not a substitute; a role at every phase), plus anti-stigma wording and safe-messaging language for suicide.
