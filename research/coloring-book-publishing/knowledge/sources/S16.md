## Learned points
1. Headline conclusions: existing law is adequate; AI used to "assist rather than stand in for human creativity" does not affect protection; purely AI-generated material, or material with "insufficient human control over the expressive elements," is not protected; analysis is case-by-case. Documented
2. "Based on the functioning of current generally available technology, prompts do not alone provide sufficient control." Even highly detailed prompts do not control how the system expresses them. Documented
3. Re-prompting does not help: revising prompts is "re-rolling" the dice; the effort, time, or expense of many revisions is irrelevant ("sweat of the brow"). Documented
4. Choosing one output from many is not authorship: "selection of a single output is not itself a creative act" (quoting the Kernochan Center, adopted by the Office). Documented
5. Identical prompts can yield different outputs, which the Office treats as evidence of lack of human control; systems that rewrite prompts internally make this clearer. Documented
6. Assistive vs. generative use: assistive uses (e.g., de-blurring, object removal, brainstorming, outlining) do not limit protection; "uses where an AI system makes expressive choices require further analysis." The distinction depends on how the tool is used. Documented
7. Using AI for brainstorming while "referencing, but not incorporating, the output" in one's own work does not affect copyrightability of the human work. Documented
8. EXPRESSIVE INPUTS (key for coloring books): when a human inputs their own copyrightable work (e.g., a hand-drawn illustration) and it is "perceptible in the output," the human is author of at least that portion, with scope like a derivative work. Documented
9. Rose Enigma example: hand-drawn sketch plus prompt; registered with annotation limiting it to "unaltered human pictorial authorship that is clearly perceptible in the deposit"; AI-added realism, lighting and shadows were excluded. Documented
10. Contrast SURYAST (refused, Dec. 11, 2023): a human photograph run through an AI style-transfer app (Starry Night style) did not show the input clearly enough; insufficient creative control. Documented
11. Selection, coordination and arrangement of AI material can be protected as a compilation (Zarya of the Dawn), but not the AI elements standing alone; "multiple similar registrations have been made since then." Documented
12. Arranging only "two or three elements" is generally not enough (Compendium § 312.2). Documented
13. Human modification of AI output (editing, adapting, enhancing) can be protected for the material the human contributed; region-editing tools such as Midjourney's "Vary Region + Remix" "can enable the user to control the selection and placement of individual creative elements," but whether that meets the originality minimum is case-by-case. Documented
14. AI elements inside a larger human-authored work do not defeat protection of the whole (e.g., a film with AI background artwork). Documented
15. AI cannot be a joint author or create a work made for hire (not an author; cannot intend or contract). Documented
16. The Office may revisit conclusions if tools give users more control; more automated prompt optimization could reduce control further. Documented
17. Creators with disabilities: AI used as a tool to realize human expression is protectable (Randy Travis sound recording registered). Documented
18. Coloring-book application: a page made by text prompt alone is unprotected; protection is strongest where the creator draws or sketches the line art (including tracing or substantially redrawing), uses their own drawing as an input that stays visible in the result, or makes substantial perceptible edits, plus creative selection and sequencing of many pages. Inferred
19. For an AI-only coloring book, others could likely copy individual pages without infringing, so the seller's competitive moat is weak. Inferred
