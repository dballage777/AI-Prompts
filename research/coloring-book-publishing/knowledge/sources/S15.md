## Learned points
1. Copyright protects only "material that is the product of human creativity"; the term "author" excludes non-humans (citing Burrow-Giles, Urantia, Naruto). Documented
2. Test: is the work "basically one of human authorship, with the computer … merely being an assisting instrument," or were the traditional elements of authorship "conceived and executed" by a machine? This is "necessarily a case-by-case inquiry." Documented
3. When AI "receives solely a prompt" and produces complex visual output, the traditional elements of authorship are determined by the technology, so the output is not protected and "must be disclaimed in a registration application." Documented
4. Prompts are like "instructions to a commissioned artist": they say what to depict, but the machine decides how. Iterative feedback prompts do not change this (endnote 30). A prompt itself may be creative, but that does not make the generated output copyrightable (endnote 27). Documented
5. Two routes to protection for works containing AI material: (a) human selection or arrangement creative enough that the work "as a whole constitutes an original work of authorship"; (b) human modification of AI material "to such a degree" that the changes meet the copyright standard. Only the human-authored aspects are protected. Documented
6. Ordinary tools are fine: a Photoshop user remains the author of the edited image; but Photoshop's AI-powered features are subject to the same AI analysis (endnote 36). Documented
7. DISCLOSURE DUTY: applicants "have a duty to disclose the inclusion of AI-generated content" and give a brief explanation of the human author's contributions. Documented
8. How to apply: use the Standard Application (not the Single Application, which requires all content be created by the same individual and has no disclaimer field). Describe human authorship in "Author Created"; exclude AI content in "Limitation of the Claim" > "Material Excluded" > "Other," e.g., "[description of content] generated by artificial intelligence." Extra notes can go in "Note to CO." Documented
9. Example claim wording for arrangement: "Selection, coordination, and arrangement of [describe human-authored content] created by the author and [describe AI content] generated by artificial intelligence." Documented
10. Do not list an AI tool or its company as author or co-author "simply because they used it." Documented
11. Only AI content that is "more than de minimis" must be excluded; brief quotes, short phrases and de minimis uses need not be disclaimed (endnote 40). Applicants who are unsure may just state that the work contains AI material; the Office will follow up. Documented
12. Fixing past filings: for pending applications, contact the Public Information Office ((202) 707-3000 or (877) 476-0778); for issued registrations, file a supplementary registration that describes human authorship, disclaims AI material, and completes "New Material Added/Other." Documented
13. Consequences of non-disclosure: the Office may cancel the registration; a court may disregard it in an infringement suit under 17 U.S.C. 411(b) if the applicant knowingly gave inaccurate information that would have caused refusal. Documented
14. For a coloring book: pure prompt-to-image line art pages would have to be disclaimed; protectable material could be the human-drawn pages, substantive human redrawing of AI line art, original text, and the creative selection/sequence of pages. Inferred
15. Registration disclosure (USCO) is a separate duty from any marketplace disclosure (e.g., KDP's AI questions); this document covers only USCO. Inferred

## Exact quotes

- "When an AI technology determines the expressive elements of its output, the generated material is not the product of human authorship." — https://www.copyright.gov/ai/ai_policy_guidance.pdf
