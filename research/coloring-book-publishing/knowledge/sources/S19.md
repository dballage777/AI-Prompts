## Learned points
1. Free to Use and Reuse is a curated showcase: "More than 100 sets of rights-free images", with "25 to 50 historical photographs, posters, maps, and movies in each set." Documented
2. Sets relevant to coloring books include Classic Children's Books (illustrated around 1900: Denslow's Mother Goose, Peter Rabbit, The Wonderful Wizard of Oz, Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, The Jungle Book, Jack and the Beanstalk, and others), Dragons, Dinosaurs & Fossils, Birds, Cats, Dogs, Horses, Gardens, Japanese Fine Prints, Art of the Book, Holidays, Autumn and Halloween, Winter, Travel Posters, WPA Posters, and World War I Posters. Documented
3. Most sets come from the Prints & Photographs Division ("unless otherwise credited"). The set descriptions use phrases such as "rights-free" and "copyright free pictures." The main page itself contains no per-item rights statement or caveat text. Documented
4. LOC's general position (Legal page): "we generally do not own the rights to materials in our collections." Users must determine for themselves whether an item is protected or in the public domain, and must satisfy any restrictions when publishing or distributing. Documented
5. Users are also responsible for privacy and publicity rights in collection items. The guide also flags licensing and trademarks as other possible rights. Documented
6. Catalog-record rights notes and what each means: "No known restrictions on publication" (the Library knows of no restrictions; this does not mean the item is in the public domain); "Publication may be restricted" (see the rights statement); "May be restricted: … P&P Restrictions Notebook"; "Rights status not evaluated" (do your own evaluation); and no note at all (also do your own evaluation). Documented
7. "No known restrictions" is used when a copyright expired or was not renewed, or for late-19th/early-20th-century collections with no copyright markings, no Copyright Office registration, no restrictions in the acquisition papers, and extensive past use with no claimant. Documented
8. Collection rights statements say "considered to be in the public domain" for expired copyrights, or note that the creator dedicated the work to the public domain (example: Carol M. Highsmith), or list donor restrictions (examples: LOOK Magazine, Brigitte Stelzer). Some collections contain exceptions to the general analysis (examples: FSA/OWI, Horydczak). Documented
9. A download being available is a clue, not a clearance: jpeg and tiff files display off-site for no-known-restrictions, expired, or released items, and also for some collections where the creator kept publication rights. "you will still need to make your own determination." Documented
10. US government works: works by federal officers or employees are not eligible for copyright in the US (17 U.S.C. § 105). Documented
11. US duration rules as stated on the guide (US use only): works published or registered in the US more than 95 years ago are public domain. The rule of thumb is to add 100 to the publication or registration year and subtract 4; the work enters the public domain on January 1 of that year. Example: 1927, renewed, entered the PD on January 1, 2023. Works published or registered through Dec. 31, 1963 are PD unless the copyright was renewed (28-year first term). Works from January 1, 1964 to December 31, 1977 are generally protected for 95 years. Documented
12. Applying that rule on the date read (2026): works published in the US in 1930 or earlier are PD as of January 1, 2026 (1930 + 100 − 4 = 2026). Inferred (arithmetic from the LOC rule)
13. Researching copyright status: search US Copyright Office records (online for 1978 to the present), hire a searcher, or pay the Copyright Office. See Circular 22. "Searches cannot be considered conclusive but will show a good faith effort." Documented
14. Permissions: LOC charges no permission fees and does not sign permission forms. P&P administers permissions only for the Seagram County Court House Archives. Any permission needed must come from the copyright owner or donor. Documented
15. Credit: LOC "requests the courtesy of a credit line." This is a request, not a licence condition. Preferred format: collection, division, Library of Congress, reproduction number (e.g., "Wright Brothers collection, Prints & Photographs Division, Library of Congress, LC-ppmsca-04598"). Short forms are acceptable (e.g., "Courtesy of the Library of Congress, LC-USZ62-13459"). Documented
16. The guidance applies to US use only. Use in other countries "is governed by the laws of the country in which the material is being used." This matters for KDP or Etsy sales in the EU or UK. Documented
17. Coloring-book workflow: pick an image from a set, open its item record, read the rights note or collection rights statement, confirm the US publication date (and the author's life dates for non-US markets), save the reproduction number, and put a credit line on the copyright page. Inferred
