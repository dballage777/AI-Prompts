# S22 — USCO: How Long Does Copyright Protection Last? (FAQ)
- URL (given): https://www.copyright.gov/help/faq/faq-duration.html | Final URL: same
- Access: OPENED | Date read: 2026-11-09 | Published/updated: no date on the FAQ page. Linked Circular 15A footer: "REVIEWED: 08/2011 REVISED: 04/2026."
- Pages read: FAQ page (two Q&As); linked Circular 15A, Duration of Copyright (https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ15a.pdf, full text).
- Source type & possible bias: Primary federal agency guidance. General rules only; the Office says to consult chapter 3 of the Copyright Act for a specific work.

## Learned points
1. Works created on or after January 1, 1978: copyright lasts the life of the author plus 70 years. Documented
2. Anonymous works, pseudonymous works, and works made for hire: 95 years from first publication or 120 years from creation, whichever expires first. Documented
3. If an anonymous/pseudonymous author's identity is later revealed in Copyright Office records, the term becomes life plus 70 years (Circ. 15A). Relevant to publishing a coloring book under a pen name. Documented
4. Joint works (not for hire): 70 years after the last surviving author's death (Circ. 15A). Documented
5. Works first published before 1978 have terms that "vary depending on several factors." Documented
6. 1909 Act works: first term of 28 years; renewal term now 67 years; maximum total 95 years. Documented
7. Circ. 15A (revised 04/2026): "all works published in the United States before January 1, 1931, are in the public domain." Key test for public-domain coloring-book source art. Documented
8. Works first copyrighted January 1, 1950 to December 31, 1963 had to be renewed; if not renewed in time, copyright expired December 31 of the 28th year and "protection was lost permanently." Works from 1964–1977 were renewed automatically (1992 amendment). Documented
9. All copyright terms run through the end of the calendar year in which they expire, so works enter the public domain on January 1. Documented
10. Some foreign works that lost U.S. protection for failure to follow formalities (including failure to renew) were restored as of January 1, 1996 under the URAA (Circular 38B). So a non-renewed foreign work from 1931–1963 may still be protected. Documented (rule) / Inferred (practical caution)
11. Renewal registration is not needed for works created on or after January 1, 1978. Documented
12. Practical rule: using pre-1931 U.S.-published art is the safe public-domain zone; for 1931–1963 works, renewal status must be checked; a new tracing or modern restoration may carry its own copyright, which these pages do not address. Inferred

## Exact quotes
- "As a general rule, for works created after January 1, 1978, copyright protection lasts for the life of the author plus an additional 70 years." — https://www.copyright.gov/help/faq/faq-duration.html
- "Applying these standards, all works published in the United States before January 1, 1931, are in the public domain." — https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ15a.pdf
- "copyright endures for a term of 95 years from the year of its first publication or a term of 120 years from the year of its creation, whichever expires first." — https://www.copyright.gov/help/faq/faq-duration.html

## Limitations
- FAQ page is very short and undated. Circular 15A's "before January 1, 1931" cutoff reflects its April 2026 revision; the cutoff advances one year each January 1.
- Did not read Circulars 1, 6A, 30, 32, or 38B.
- U.S. terms only; other countries' terms differ (e.g., for sales on non-U.S. Amazon stores).
