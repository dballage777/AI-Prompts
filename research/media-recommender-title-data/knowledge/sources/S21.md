## Learned points
1. [Research] Setup: four zero-shot, ungrounded LLMs (Mistral Large, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, GPT-OSS-120B, Claude Sonnet 4.6) were each given a user's 30 most recent interactions and asked for a top-10 list at temperature 0. Three catalogs were used: MovieLens-25M (62,423 movies), Amazon Reviews 2023 Toys & Games (890,874 products), and Yelp Open Dataset (150,346 businesses keyed by name + city). Each catalog had 300 audit users with at least 30 interactions, plus 100 held-out users, giving about 3,000 recommendations per cell.
2. [Research] OOD@10 (recommendations not in the catalog) on MovieLens: Mistral 2.0%, Llama 0.6%, GPT-OSS 2.7%, Claude 1.2%. On Yelp: 11.6 / 12.9 / 38.7 / 14.4%. On Amazon Toys: 49.3 / 53.6 / 61.0 / 50.6%. Anchored to human labels, the intervals are MovieLens 1.2% [0.6, 7.2], Yelp 11.0% [8.4, 17.0], and Amazon 51.1% [41.5, 60.7].
3. [Research] The cause the author proposes is "catalog canonicity" (memorization). MovieLens films are "canonical film entries seen many times in any web-scale corpus". Long-tail product strings are not. The gradient did not follow model size: the largest model, GPT-OSS-120B, was worst on both long-tail catalogs.
4. [Research] Matching strategy changes the result. On identical outputs, plausible off-the-shelf matchers disagree on the Amazon OOD rate by more than 10x. Exact match after Unicode folding recovers only 49.6% of human-confirmed items, because of alternate titles, moved articles, and accents. Token-set ≥90 (the most common rule) treats a truncated catalog entry as matching any longer title that contains its words. Run over the full catalog, it put 93.8% of Amazon items in catalog where the human put 48.9%. Token-sort ≥90 misses alternate titles, for example "Se7en" against the MovieLens entry "Seven (a.k.a. Se7en) (1995)".
5. [Research] Validating the matcher: 201 human-labeled items, each shown with 8 candidate catalog entries. F1 barely separates matchers (0.656–0.904, and "always in catalog" scores 0.782), so the author selects on net bias instead. The adopted judge (claude-sonnet-4-6, temperature 0, picks a candidate index or 0) has net bias -0.040 overall vs +0.144 for token-set ≥90, and Cohen's κ 0.747 vs 0.513. Its precision is 0.934 and recall 0.876. Its error rate is 0.023 on MovieLens, 0.075 on Yelp, and 0.218 on Amazon, with most errors on numbered or sized product variants.
6. [Research] Confidence calibration: verbalized confidence (0–100, "Just Ask" template) sits in a narrow band, with cell means of 67–86, while the catalog-hit rate ranges from 39.0% to 99.4%. Confidence is mostly a property of the model (Claude about 72.9, Mistral about 84.8), not of the catalog. On MovieLens all four models are under-confident: they state 77.8–86.2 while being right 97.3–99.4% of the time. On Amazon all four are over-confident, with gaps of -24.4 to -42.7 points. Across the 12 cells, 7 are under-confident and 5 over-confident.
7. [Research] Miscalibration is worse on the long tail. On Amazon, the hit rate for Mistral falls from 52.1% (head) to 28.2% (tail) with no change in confidence, and tail ECE reaches 0.588. MovieLens tail strata were too small to report.
8. [Research] Filtering on confidence does not work. Split-conformal abstention on verbalized confidence (α = 0.05–0.20) reduced hallucination by at most 1.65 pp (median 0.02 pp). In 18 of 48 entries the filtered set was more hallucinated than the unfiltered one. The interpretation: "Just Ask" elicits a generic quality rating, not a catalog-membership probability.
9. [Research] Catalog-anchored prompting moved results more but inconsistently. On Llama × Yelp, OOD fell from 12.9% to 1.5% (the prompt also changed the list). On Claude × Yelp, confidence fell from 67.2 to 53.0 while the hit rate rose to 91.1%, which made ECE worse (0.380). Effects depend on the template and the vendor.
10. [Research] Recommended mitigations: report calibration alongside OOD, validate the matcher that produces the OOD number, and use catalog-anchored elicitation. Training-side methods (RecLM, LCFT) are most valuable for long-tail catalogs. For canonical catalogs at frontier scale, OOD is already low and calibration becomes the main concern.
11. [Research] Operational detail: the judge ran in batches of 10, and 1.0% of items went unanswered on the first pass and were counted as non-matches (about 190 of 18,833 verdicts). Judge cost was about $12 for 1,884 calls.
12. [Inferred] For the recommender: well-known films are rarely invented outright (about 1–3% per list), but anime, regional TV, and niche titles behave more like the long-tail catalogs. Every title still needs an existence lookup. Do not use the model's stated confidence to decide which titles to check. Check them all.
13. [Inferred] Title matching for verification should not be one fuzzy threshold. Normalize (Unicode folding, article moves such as "Seven (a.k.a. Se7en)"), match on title plus year and medium, compare alternate and original titles, and review candidates (top-N from search, then a pick-or-none judgment) instead of accepting a token-set ≥90 hit.

## Limitations or bias
- A recent single-author paper. Validation rests on 201 labels from one annotator, with only 3 out-of-catalog labels for MovieLens.
- The judge model shares a vendor with one generator (the author tested this and reports no favoring).
- MovieLens is a pre-2020-heavy, canonical film catalog. It does not cover TV episodes, anime, or new releases.
- "OOD" here means not in the target catalog. A real title missing from MovieLens counts as a hallucination, which slightly conflates "nonexistent" with "not in this catalog".
- Zero-shot ungrounded models only. Tool-using or grounded setups are out of scope.
