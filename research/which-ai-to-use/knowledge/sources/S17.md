- Source type & possible bias: An academic paper by Microsoft Research authors analyzing Microsoft's own product (Bing Copilot), accepted at NeurIPS 2026 (peer-reviewed conference). Data came from about 200k U.S. conversations. Microsoft has an interest in how its product is portrayed. The data comes from one free consumer chatbot.

## Learned points
1. Data: about 200k anonymized, privacy-scrubbed U.S. Bing Copilot conversations from 1 Jan to 30 Sep 2024. Copilot-Uniform is a uniform sample of about 100k conversations. Copilot-Thumbs is about 100k conversations that received thumbs-up or thumbs-down feedback. The study was approved by Microsoft's IRB. [Research]
2. Method: an LLM pipeline maps each conversation to O*NET "intermediate work activities" (IWAs; 332 in total). The user goal (what the person is trying to do) is classified separately from the AI action (what the AI does). Success is measured three ways: thumbs feedback, a GPT-4o-mini task-completion classifier, and "scope" (how much of the activity the AI covered). [Research]
3. The most common user goals were learning, communicating, teaching/explaining and writing, with learning and communicating the most prominent. The AI's own actions were mostly service verbs: provide, explain, teach, assist, respond. [Research]
4. User goals and AI actions often differ. In 40% of conversations the user-goal and AI-action activity sets don't overlap at all, and for each user-goal activity the AI performs about two activities on average. [Research]
5. Copilot did best at communicating, teaching/explaining and writing, and worse at image generation and data analysis. Thumbs feedback confirmed this pattern. [Research]
6. The IWAs with the highest positive-feedback share included writing and editing, evaluating or buying goods and services, and researching health, culture, law, policy and society. The lowest included five visual-design IWAs and five data-analysis IWAs. [Research]
7. Completion rate correlates strongly with thumbs feedback (weighted r > 0.75), which supports using the completion classifier. Scope correlates with how much people use an activity (r = 0.64), which suggests users have found the tasks where AI helps most broadly. [Research]
8. On the AI-action side, completion × scope is consistently lower than on the user-goal side. In other words, AI can help users with more of their work than it can do on its own. [Research]
9. Occupations with the highest AI applicability are information-work jobs: interpreters and translators, writers and authors, mathematicians, and sales and customer service representatives. The lowest are physical and manual jobs. Most occupations have some applicability because most work involves some information processing. [Research]
10. Teacher categories appear among the occupations scored (for example "Prim., Second., and Special Ed. Teachers"), but their numeric scores were only in figures and could not be read. [Documented; values not visible]
11. Usage became more varied over time. The number of user goals covering 75% of activity rose from 20 in the first month to 28 in the last month, and applicability estimates rose with it. [Research]
12. AI applicability correlates with earlier predicted exposure from Eloundou et al. (employment-weighted r = 0.73). Those predictions underestimated occupations such as proofreaders and software developers. Applicability correlates only weakly with wage (r = 0.13), which challenges the idea that AI mainly affects high-wage jobs. [Research]
13. Usage differs by platform, according to the paper's limitations section: ChatGPT usage has more writing tasks, Claude usage skews toward coding and math (it cites the Anthropic Economic Index), and Copilot data covers only one part of the market. [Research (citing other studies)]
14. The authors warn against simple conclusions about automation versus augmentation. A high AI-action score does not mean the job will be lost. Their example is ATMs, after which the number of bank-teller jobs grew. [Research]
15. Biggest stated limitation: "our data on AI usage are from 2024; models have improved dramatically since then." For newer data the authors point to follow-up work on M365 Copilot data from early 2026 (Counts et al.). They also note that agent harnesses have advanced. [Research]
16. Related work in the paper: the Anthropic Economic Index (Claude) and Google's AI & Economy ATLAS (Gemini) map usage to O*NET tasks. Later Anthropic Economic Index reports added success measures and occupation-level exposure scores. [Research]
17. For teacher-facing advice: general chatbots are strongest at explaining, teaching, writing and editing, and gathering information, and weakest at data analysis and image generation. Plan more checking for those weaker tasks. [Inferred]

## Limitations
- The data covers one product (free Bing Copilot), U.S. only, in 2024, so it is now old for capability questions. It is useful for patterns of use, not for ranking today's models.
- "Success" is measured by a proxy: a GPT-4o-mini classifier plus thumbs feedback that has selection bias. It does not measure productivity impact.
- Values in the figures and tables (scores per occupation, the full top and bottom IWA lists) were images and not readable, so I did not record them.
- The authors work at Microsoft and studied a Microsoft product.
- The title the orchestrator gave differs from the v7 title. The arXiv ID is the same.
