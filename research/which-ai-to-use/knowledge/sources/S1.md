## Learned points
1. Mollick splits his advice in two. For low-stakes chat (a recipe, a quick question, a letter), "tons of options" are good enough, the free default models included, so pick the one you like. For high-stakes questions (a medical or legal second opinion), use the most advanced models available. (Advice dated July 2026.) [Reported]
2. The high-stakes models he names: Claude's most powerful models (Opus and Fable) or ChatGPT's GPT-5.6 Sol, set to at least the "High" thinking level. His reasons: lower error rates and much higher scores on ability tests in complex fields. These cost money. [Reported]
3. You choose two things, a model and a thinking level. The article includes a chart on how to select them; the chart is an image and was not readable. [Reported]
4. For "real work" he says most people have two choices, ChatGPT or Claude, starting at $20/month. Both are "easy and powerful" but "badly documented and confusingly named." [Reported]
5. His framing: AI use has shifted from back-and-forth chat to agentic systems, a model plus tools that plan and act, where "an agentic system gives an AI a computer to use." [Reported]
6. There are two ways to give an agent a computer. (a) A virtual computer from the vendor: "ChatGPT Work" in ChatGPT, "Cowork" in Claude. (b) Your own computer, through the desktop apps: ChatGPT's Work/Codex modes and Claude's Cowork/Code modes. Work/Cowork return a finished result. Codex/Claude Code show the work itself: file changes, commands, tests, and a change record. [Reported]
7. Starting settings he recommends inside agent modes: Sol on High for ChatGPT; Fable or Opus on High for Claude. [Reported]
8. Permissions matter. In his example, ChatGPT actually sent an email to colleagues because he had earlier given it send permission, while Claude only drafted. His advice: leave approval-before-acting on (the default) until you trust the system and understand its mistakes. [Reported]
9. Prompt-injection risk: agents that read email or browse can meet text planted to trick them. He calls the problem "not solved" and advises limiting what the agent can touch and keeping approval on for anything that sends, spends, or deletes. [Reported]
10. Anecdote on accuracy: he gave GPT-5.6 Sol in Codex his book PDF. It worked for 30 minutes, checked 195 references, and every note was accurate, with no hallucinated page numbers that he could spot. It was also "incredibly nitpicky," and he used his own judgment to reject some notes. (This is a single anecdote.) [Reported]
11. Working with agents is "more like managing than it is chatting": delegate, review, ask for changes. Prompting skill matters less now than asking clearly for what you want and correcting the AI. [Reported]
12. Microsoft Copilot (often the only option in Microsoft workplaces) "uses a mix of AI models." He calls it okay for office documents but says it "lags badly" on agentic abilities. [Reported]
13. Chinese open-weights models (Kimi K3, DeepSeek, Qwen) are "surprisingly capable" but take expertise to use as agents. [Reported]
14. On Google (July 2026): he says it has "no leading frontier model" and nothing close to Codex or Claude Code, so he doesn't suggest Gemini as a primary system right now, "though this could change quickly." Exceptions he names: Gemini Notebook (formerly NotebookLM) as the most useful interface for research across many sources, and Gemini Omni for editing video directly. [Reported]
15. Multimedia gaps: Google and ChatGPT both have strong built-in image generators; Claude has none and "draws" images with code instead. ChatGPT's GPT-Live voice mode listens and speaks natively; Claude's voice is text read aloud. [Reported]
16. Cost note: the $20 tiers include "real but limited agent usage" that agents use up quickly. Pricier plans mostly buy more hours of AI labor, "not smarter AI." [Reported]
17. His bottom line: pick Claude or ChatGPT, pay the $20, give an agent a real task from your own life, review the output carefully, and ask for changes as you would with a person. [Reported]
18. Inferred for educators: free defaults are fine for low-stakes classroom drafting. For anything consequential (grading guidance, student-welfare issues), Mollick's framework points to top-tier models with high thinking levels, approvals left on, and human review. [Inferred]

## Limitations
- This is one expert's opinion from personal use, with no systematic or controlled testing. Accuracy claims rest on single anecdotes.
- It is dated July 2026. S4 (Artificial Analysis) shows the model landscape moved by Oct–Nov 2026; for example, Gemini 4 Carbon was #1 on its index on Oct 28, 2026. The Google assessment may be out of date.
- The model/thinking-level selection chart is an image and could not be read.
- The guide is aimed at knowledge workers using paid plans. It says nothing specific about schools, students, or student-data privacy.
