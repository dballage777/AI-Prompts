- Source type & possible bias: a vendor (Anthropic) studying its own product, Claude. The data are privacy-preserving, classifier-labelled samples of Claude conversations. Claude classifies Claude transcripts, and "success" is Claude's own assessment. The survey sample is Claude users and is self-described as not representative. The vendor has an interest in framing AI as augmenting rather than displacing workers. The data show nothing about other chatbots.

## Learned points
1. Usage mix, June 2026 report (chat and Cowork samples from Apr 10 to Jun 10, 2026): personal-use share rises from about 35% on weekdays to just under 50% on weekends. Weekend conversations shift toward emotional support, medical questions and investment advice. [Documented]
2. Artifacts: 93% of Claude conversations produce an identifiable output. The most common are explanations (17%), documents and reports (15%) and guidance (11%). Conversational outputs and written deliverables are each about one third of conversations; code and technical work is about one sixth. [Documented]
3. Use by purpose: coursework conversations most often produce documents and reports (21%), explanations (20%), educational materials (11%) and academic papers (6%). Personal conversations mostly produce explanations (25%) and recommendations (22%). [Documented]
4. The product matters more than the model for delegation. Claude Code shows higher AI autonomy than chat or Cowork for 26 of 31 output types, with an average gap of 0.37 on a 1–5 scale. Among Sonnet-served conversations the gap is still 0.26, "suggesting that the product used is likely more important than the underlying model." A median chat blog post takes 13 rounds of back-and-forth; a median Claude Code blog session has a single prompt. [Documented]
5. Model mix by surface: 54% of Claude Code sessions are served by Opus, compared with 10% of chat and Cowork conversations (June 2026). [Documented]
6. Reading level: Claude's responses sit about one year of education above the user's prompt on average. The gap is widest for images, games and apps (+1.7 to +2.6 years) and near zero for audience-facing writing such as blogs, academic papers and email. For teachers this means output may need explicit reading-level targeting. [Documented; the implication is Inferred]
7. Compute tracks value: tokens per conversation rise with the wage of the mapped occupation. Building apps uses more than 3 times the median conversation's tokens; an explanation uses about one fifth. Extended thinking is on in 34% of top-tercile conversations versus 31% of bottom-tercile ones. [Documented]
8. Model selection, March 2026 report (Feb 5–12, 2026 sample, 1M conversations): among paid Claude.ai users, 51% of usage is Opus, 55% for computer and math tasks, and 45% for educational tasks. 34% of software-developer tasks use Opus compared with 12% of tutor tasks. Each extra $10 of task wage adds 1.5 percentage points to the Opus share (2.8 pp on the API). Users roughly match the model to task difficulty. [Documented]
9. Learning curve: users with 6 or more months of tenure have about a 10% higher conversation success rate. The gap is about 5 pp raw and about 3 pp within the same task. Their prompts sit at a higher education level and they make fewer personal requests. Anthropic notes the result may reflect selection or survivorship rather than learning-by-doing. Possible lesson for educators: experience with prompting likely pays off. [Documented; the lesson is Inferred]
10. Usage is diversifying. The top 10 tasks fell from 24% of Claude.ai traffic (Nov 2025) to 19% (Feb 2026). Coursework fell from 19% to 12% and personal use rose from 35% to 42%, partly because of academic breaks and new casual sign-ups. Coding is still the largest category, at 35% of Claude.ai conversations. [Documented]
11. Success versus task length, January 2026 report (Nov 2025 data): Claude.ai success is 67% compared with 49% on the API. On the API, success falls from about 60% for sub-hour tasks to about 45% for tasks of 5 or more human-hours, with 50% reached at about 3.5 hours. Claude.ai's extrapolated 50% point is about 19 hours, likely because multi-turn chat lets users correct course. Personal tasks succeed 78% of the time versus 61% for software development. [Documented]
12. Augmentation versus automation on Claude.ai (Nov 2025): 52% augmented and 45% automated. Anthropic suggests product features (file creation, memory, Skills) shifted use toward human-in-the-loop work. The interaction modes are directive, feedback loop, task iteration, learning and validation. [Documented]
13. Survey of about 9,700 linked respondents (April–June 2026; skewed toward computer, math and management occupations; women only 12%): 86% report speed gains, 82% scope gains and 69% quality gains. 68% say they learn more with AI. Heavier delegators report learning at the same rate as others, but these are self-reports and do "not rule out skill erosion." [Documented]
14. Gender differences: in the linked sample, women use Claude more iteratively and collaboratively. Their automation share is 7.3 pp lower and their Claude Code share 6.3 pp lower than men's, controlling for occupation. [Documented]
15. Expectations gap (Oct 16, 2026; August 2026 survey of US adults, N=10,980, weighted, plus Anthropic staff): 26% of US adults who use AI at work say a task takes a quarter of the time or less, compared with 59% of staff. Among regular workplace AI users, 56% expect AI to do at least 7 of 8 named tasks by 2030, compared with 28% of people who never use AI. Frequency of use shapes beliefs about capability. [Documented]
16. US adults' top expected obstacles to workplace AI by 2030 are customers preferring a person, data privacy and security, "AI not being reliable enough," and laws, regulation and liability. [Documented]
17. Cross-cutting point: these data describe only how Claude is used and say nothing about Claude versus other chatbots. They are useful for "what tasks people bring to AI" and "how interaction style affects success," not for ranking platforms. [Inferred]

## Limitations
- The hub dashboard is JS-only, so none of its data was captured.
- The March report was read only partly (through the first experience-effects specification). The January report was read selectively. Appendices were not read.
- All metrics come from Claude-run classifiers, including "success" and "autonomy." The vendor is reporting on its own product.
- The survey samples skew heavily toward technical, management and male users. The Expectations-gap staff sample is Anthropic employees.
