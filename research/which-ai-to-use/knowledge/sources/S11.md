## Learned points
1. Scale of the hub as of Nov 7, 2026: 425 models tracked, 97 benchmarks tracked. Results are a mix of evaluations Epoch runs itself and data collected from external leaderboards and primary sources. [Documented]
2. The Epoch Capabilities Index (ECI) combines scores from many benchmarks (more than 50 for the general ECI) into one "general capability" scale. Epoch compares it to an IQ test: it is meant to capture broad underlying capability rather than specific skills. Domain ECIs exist for software engineering, math and cyber. [Documented]
3. ECI values mean nothing in absolute terms; only comparisons between models are meaningful. The scale is anchored so Claude 3.5 Sonnet = 130 and GPT-5 = 150, and there is no maximum. At launch, a 5-point ECI gain roughly matched a doubling of METR Time Horizon. [Documented]
4. Top general ECI scores in the CSV updated Nov 7, 2026, with 90% credible intervals: GPT-6 Astra 169.1 (166.2–172.3, released 2026-09-03); GPT-6.1 Sol 168.9; Claude Opus 5.5 168.4 (165.5–171.5); GPT-6.1 Terra 167.8; Claude Fable 5.1 166.9; Gemini 4 Argon 166.5 (limited access); Claude Sonnet 5.5 166.2; Claude Opus 5 164.5; Claude Fable 5 164.2; GPT-6 Sol 164.1. The best open-weights entry in the top 20 was DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 at 159.3. Meta's Muse Wonder scored 162.0. [Documented]
5. The top models sit inside each other's uncertainty intervals. In Epoch's Nov 4 report, GPT-6 Astra had a 72% probability of being more capable than Claude Opus 5.5, and the 90% interval for the gap between them ran from −0.9 to +2.1 ECI points. A small lead in the ranking is not a reliable difference. [Documented]
6. Methodology change on Nov 4, 2026: ECI moved from a frequentist least-squares fit with bootstrapped intervals to a Bayesian Item Response Theory model with a beta likelihood and per-benchmark noise. Uncertainty estimates are now better justified, and two models can be compared as a probability. Frontier models moved about one point on average, while very weak older models dropped substantially. [Documented]
7. ECI is based on Item Response Theory, a method from educational testing that compares students who took different exams. Each benchmark gets a fitted difficulty and slope, and doing better on harder benchmarks raises a model's ECI more. [Documented]
8. Specialized models can get low ECI scores even when they are very capable in their own domain. A general index is therefore not the right tool for choosing a model for one narrow task. [Documented]
9. Epoch warns about contamination: models may have seen benchmark questions, or similar ones, during training, which inflates scores. It singles out MATH Level 5. [Documented]
10. Small changes in prompts or evaluation settings can change results significantly. Epoch says its results "may not always generalize to other contexts or use cases." [Documented]
11. Self-reported lab scores can differ from independent runs. Example: Claude 3.5 Sonnet (2024-10-22) claimed 65% on GPQA Diamond, while Epoch's 16 runs gave a mean of 55% ± 3%. Epoch attributes the gap to undisclosed prompt and temperature settings. [Documented]
12. Model developers may publish only their best scores (cherry-picking) and may optimize for benchmarks. Epoch says there is "some evidence" that open-weight models optimize on benchmarks more aggressively, which could mean the open-versus-proprietary gap is understated. [Documented]
13. Epoch's own setup: chat/instruct variants only, the API-default temperature, zero-shot chain-of-thought, an empty system prompt, and multiple runs per model (16 for GPQA Diamond and Mock AIME, 8 for MATH Level 5) with ±1 standard-error bars. [Documented]
14. Strict answer formatting can push weak models below chance. On GPQA Diamond, Yi-34B-Chat scored 15% and open-mistral-7b scored 13%, against a 25% random baseline, because answers not given as "Answer: {LETTER}" score zero. Output format affects measured performance. [Documented]
15. Benchmarks near 0% or 100% cannot separate models well; saturation means older benchmarks stop being informative. "Elo-style" arena leaderboards (e.g., WebDev Arena) are excluded from ECI because scores must lie between 0 and 1. [Documented]
16. FrontierMath Erdős (Sep 2026) consists of 68 open Erdős problems that must be proved or disproved in Lean, with $300 and 72 hours per attempt. Results: GPT-6 Astra (pre-release) 3% (2 of 68); GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-5.5, Claude Fable 5.1 and Claude Fable 5 all 0%. In additional non-benchmark attempts, Astra solved 5 of 68 at least once, using over $220,000 of compute. [Documented]
17. The FrontierMath Erdős harness matters. Results depend on budget, scaffold and number of attempts, and Epoch reports separately the attempts that did not follow the fixed protocol. "Best-of-many" results from labs are not comparable to single-attempt benchmark scores. [Documented]
18. In October 2026, Epoch published four benchmark reviews: one rated Verified and three rated Flawed, with four more lacking enough information for a verdict. Many published benchmarks have quality problems. [Documented] (headline only; the reviews themselves were not read)
19. For educators: as of Nov 2026, capability benchmarks show OpenAI, Anthropic and Google flagships in a statistically tight cluster at the top. Benchmark rank alone is a weak basis for picking a classroom chatbot, and task fit, cost, limits and reliability matter more. [Inferred]

## Limitations
- The interactive leaderboard did not render as text. Rankings come from the public CSV, which was downloaded on Nov 7, 2026 and changes as models are added. The CSV interval for Opus 5.5 differs slightly from the figure in the Nov 4 report.
- The ECI measures API models under benchmark conditions. It says nothing directly about consumer app UX, usage limits, citation accuracy or sycophancy.
- Individual benchmark pages and the benchmark reviews were not read in detail.
