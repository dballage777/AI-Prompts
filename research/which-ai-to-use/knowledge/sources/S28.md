## Learned points
1. Design: 4 chatbots via their public web interfaces with default settings: GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini 1.5 Flash, and Copilot (powered by GPT-4). Each answered 120 text-only USMLE-style pediatric MCQs (6 topics × 20), three times each in fresh sessions, for 1,440 responses. One fixed prompt: "Generate the list of correct answers for the following MCQs:". [Research]
2. Benchmarks: Year-4 MBBS students at Alfaisal who had finished a 9-week pediatrics clerkship (cohort size not stated in the text), plus three random-answer sets as a baseline. Questions were validated by 3 experts. [Research]
3. Headline: the LLM average was 80.4 ± 10.9%, compared with students at 72.1 ± 13.1% (+8.3 points) and random answers at 20.5 ± 6.2%. [Research]
4. Per model: Copilot 84.5 ± 8.3%, Claude 84.2 ± 9.9%, GPT-4o 80.9 ± 9.1%, Gemini 72.0 ± 18.9% (slightly below students). [Research]
5. Statistical significance was limited. Only GPT-4o vs students was significant (χ² = 4.277, p = 0.039). Copilot (p = 0.683) and Claude (p = 0.711) were not significantly different from students despite higher means. Between models, Claude > GPT-4o (p = 0.015) and GPT-4o > Gemini (p = 0.039). No other pairs were significant. The authors note Type I error risk from multiple comparisons. [Research]
6. Consistency across 3 runs differed by model. Copilot's answers matched earlier attempts 99.2–100% of the time, with 83.3% of items correct on all three runs. Claude matched 96.7–98.3% (81.7% correct on all runs). GPT-4o matched 79.2–94.2% (68.3%). Gemini matched 73.3–99.2% (52%; Gemini's attempt scores were 75.8, 75, and 65%). Run-to-run variability is a reliability dimension separate from accuracy. [Research]
7. Topic matters more than platform. Averages across LLMs: pulmonology 96.3%, development 86.3%, infectious diseases 85%, neonatology 69.7%, nephrology 67.5%. Gemini fell to 46.7% in nephrology and 53.3% in neonatology. [Research]
8. Only 50% (60/120) of questions were answered correctly by all models on all attempts. 6.7% (8/120) were never answered correctly; 6 of those were high-level critical-thinking items, and 2 were neonatology recall items that most students got right. [Research]
9. Practical tool constraint: Copilot accepted only 4,000 characters per prompt, so only about 20 MCQs fit at a time. This may have changed how questions were delivered to Copilot compared with the other models. [Research]
10. Authors' recommendation: LLMs can be "valuable supplementary tools" in medical education with human oversight, especially for advanced reasoning and specialized domains. Careful model selection should consider topic-specific strengths. [Research]
11. Self-stated limitations: a single fixed prompt (prompt sensitivity was not tested), hallucination not assessed, explanation quality not assessed, a cross-sectional design that doesn't reflect newer models, and only 120 items. [Research]
12. Context cited from prior studies: GPT-4 at 81% in a meta-analysis across licensing exams (GPT-3.5 at 58%), and in pathology ChatGPT showed 80–85% consistency compared with Bard's 54–61%. [Research] (secondary citations inside the paper)
13. Lag problem: models tested in January 2025 (Gemini 1.5 Flash, a fast/light tier, compared with mid or flagship tiers of the others) and published in May 2026. By publication, all four were superseded. The Gemini result partly reflects tier choice and not only vendor. [Inferred]
14. For educators: running the same question set 2–3 times in fresh sessions is a cheap way to check consistency before relying on any chatbot for answer keys. Expect weak spots in niche subtopics. [Inferred]

## Limitations
- Figures 1–2 and Table 1 are images and were not read. All numbers come from the body text.
- The student cohort size is not stated in the text I read.
- Unequal model tiers (Gemini 1.5 Flash vs Claude 3.5 Sonnet and GPT-4o). The Copilot version was unspecified beyond "powered by GPT-4."
- The narrow domain (pediatrics MCQs, recognition-type items) may not generalize to open-ended educational tasks.
- A typo in the conclusions ("thAlme highest") shows light copyediting; this does not affect the data.
