Prepared 2026-11-08. All findings are "as of November 2026."
**This is research to discuss with your vet, not veterinary advice.**

## 2. Short answer, by dog

### Dog 2: the 6-month-old puppy
1. **The biggest finding is that the puppy is eating adult-only food.** Both current recipes say they meet AAFCO standards "for adult maintenance" only [S16, S17] (Commercial, label text verified). AAFCO is the group that sets the nutrient standards printed on US pet food labels. Veterinary nutritionists say puppies should eat a food made for growth or for "all life stages" until at least 12 months old [S11, S12, S13, S15] (Expert body, Strong). Tufts says: "PLEASE don't change to an adult food before 12 months" [S12].
2. **Why it matters:** adult foods may not have enough of the nutrients growing dogs need. For larger puppies, the balance of calories and calcium matters for bone development [S13, S14, S15] (Expert body).
3. **An easy option within the same brand:** the Nutrish puppy recipe (Chicken & Brown Rice) is labeled "All Life Stages, including growth of large size dogs (70 lbs…)", at 390 calories per cup [S18] (Commercial, label verified). This isn't an endorsement. Any food labeled for growth or all life stages qualifies.
4. **Ask your vet about expected adult size.** If your vet thinks the puppy will be over about 50 lb as an adult, Tufts suggests a large-breed puppy food. Tufts calls 50 lb "more conservative" than AAFCO's 70 lb cutoff [S11] (Expert body).
5. **Homemade isn't recommended for growing puppies** unless a board-certified veterinary nutritionist designs the recipe [S6, S8, S15] (Expert body, Strong). "They should not be fed to growing kittens or puppies" [S6].
6. **Portions:**
   - Judge portions by body condition: aim for a 4–5 score on a 9-point scale, with regular weight checks. Don't go by the bag alone [S12, S23] (Expert body).
   - The brand's puppy chart suggests 2½–3 cups a day for 20–29 lb puppies aged 4–8 months [S18] (Commercial). That's more than he eats now. But label charts can be off [S23], so ask your vet to check his growth and body score rather than just adding food.
   - Spaying or neutering can lower calorie needs by up to 30% [S11].

### Dog 1: the 9-year-old
1. **Losing weight is worth it.** In a study of 48 Labradors, dogs fed 25% less from puppyhood lived longer (median) and showed signs of chronic disease later [S22] (Research). That was a lifelong study, not weight loss in old age (Inferred). Labs often "live to eat," and the ideal body score is 4–5 out of 9 [S23] (Expert body).
2. **Don't just cut back his current adult food.** He already eats about 680–730 calories a day, which is below the bag's chart for 51–100 lb dogs (3–5½ cups) [S16, S17], and he's still above his goal weight. Merck says feeding a maintenance food in restricted amounts reduces all of its nutrients, and "maintenance diets are not formulated to be fed in that way." Merck suggests a weight-management or veterinary weight-loss food instead [S24] (Expert body, verified).
3. **Ask your vet to check his health first.** If a dog seems to need unusually few calories, Merck suggests checking for conditions such as hypothyroidism [S24] (Expert body).
4. **Use calories, not cups.** Pick one recipe instead of alternating, or count calories, so his intake stays steady (Inferred). Keep treats under 10% of daily calories [S12] (Expert body).
5. **Weight loss can be done safely under supervision.** In a study of 24 obese middle-aged dogs, controlled loss of about 1.5% of body weight per week reduced fat and kept muscle [S26] (Research). Let your vet set his pace and calorie target.
6. **Homemade food isn't a good fit during weight loss.** "It's very difficult to meet all of a pet's nutritional needs during the sometimes long process of weight loss" [S8] (Expert body, verified).

### For both dogs: meal prep vs. bagged
- **Most homemade diets are incomplete.**
  - 95% of 200 published recipes lacked at least one essential nutrient [S1, S2] (Research).
  - Only about 6% of 1,726 diets that owners actually feed were potentially complete [S4] (Research).
  - Recipes written by board-certified veterinary nutritionists were the exception [S1].
- **There's no evidence homemade food is healthier:** "there is no evidence to support claims that home-prepared diets are healthier than commercial diets" [S6] (Expert body).
- **Owners drift from recipes.** Only 13% of Tufts clients were still following the original recipe [S7], and more than 80% had changed it [S6] (Expert body). Leaving out the prescribed calcium supplement can cause serious bone and kidney problems [S5] (Expert body).
- **Fresh-cooked food:** studies show better digestibility and firmer stools than kibble, but the companies selling fresh food funded those studies, and none showed long-term health benefits [S32] (Research, industry-funded).

## 4. What the evidence says, by question
**Q1. What does "complete and balanced" mean?**
- An AAFCO statement on the label says which life stage a food meets: adult maintenance, growth, or all life stages. It also says whether the food was "formulated to meet" the standard or tested in a feeding trial [S11, S21].
- AAFCO "does not regulate, test, approve or certify pet food" [S21] (Expert body, verified).
- A "veterinarian recommended" claim means little [S9].
- Ingredient lists "can be very misleading" [S19].

**Q2. Safety**
- **Raw food:** FDA testing found *Salmonella* in 15 and *Listeria* in 32 of 196 raw samples, and none in 120 dry dog food samples [S27] (Expert body). Bacteria grew from 42% of raw cat foods and none of the cooked ones. Freeze-dried counts as raw unless it's labeled cooked [S28].
- **Cooking temperatures:** at least 165°F (74°C) kills bacteria and parasites [S24].
- **Toxic foods:** the ASPCA list covers foods to keep away from dogs [S31]. Grapes and whole bones are flagged in [S5].

**Q3. Health outcomes and the heart-disease (DCM) question**
- DCM is a type of heart disease that weakens the heart muscle. Diet-associated DCM appears linked to diets "high in pulses (peas, lentils, chickpeas, beans)," whether grain-free or not [S30] (Expert body, June 2026).
- Both of your current recipes list dried peas (and the beef recipe also lists pea starch), behind meat, soybean meal, and grains [S16, S17]. No source gives a threshold for how much is too much, so ask your vet whether this matters (Inferred).
- FDA's public updates are older. The 2019 update [S29] included home-cooked diets among the cases.

**Q4. Life-stage and size needs**
- Puppies: see section 2 [S11–S15].
- Seniors: AAFCO and the NRC have no separate "senior" nutrient standard [S24] (Expert body).
- Dry foods range from about 300 to over 700 calories per cup, so "cups" alone mean little [S23].

**Q5. Doing meal prep safely**
- Use a recipe from a board-certified veterinary nutritionist.
- Follow it exactly, including the supplements, and lab-test the food for long-term feeding [S1, S5, S7].
- Homemade food has about 200 calories per cup, versus 300–400 for dry food, so you feed about twice the volume [S7].

**Q6. Judging a bagged food**
- **Label checks:** the life-stage statement, calories per cup, and who formulates the food [S20].
- **Questions WSAVA suggests asking the maker:** Do you employ a qualified nutritionist? Who formulates the food? What quality control do you use? What research have you done? [S20]
- **Nutrish's answers:** its pages don't name a nutritionist [S16]. The brand is now owned by Post (bought from Smucker in 2023) [S16] (Commercial).

**Q7. Cost and time**
- Compare foods by cost per calorie, not cost per bag [S10] (Expert body).
- Home cooking costs more and always takes more time [S8].

**Q8. Switching foods**
- No veterinary source in this set gave a specific schedule. A gradual change over about a week is common advice from brand sources, which weren't included as evidence here. Ask your vet (Inferred, and a gap).

## 5. Where experts disagree, and why
- **Large-breed cutoff:** Tufts uses 50 lb as a conservative cutoff, while AAFCO uses 70 lb [S11, S18]. The difference matters for your puppy only if he's expected to be big.
- **How long to feed puppy food:** Tufts says at least 12 months in one place, and 18 months for large or giant breeds in another [S11, S12]. The source isn't consistent on this.
- **Fresh food benefits:** company-funded studies show better digestion [S32]. Veterinary nutritionists say there's no evidence homemade food is healthier [S6]. The studies measured stool and lab markers, not long-term health.
- **DCM:** Tufts reports ongoing research in 2026 [S30], and FDA has issued no newer public update [S29]. The evidence is still developing.

## 6. About your current food
- **Chicken & Veggies:** "adult maintenance"; 340 calories per cup; the chart for 51–100 lb dogs is 3¼–5½ cups [S16].
- **Beef, Pea & Brown Rice:** "adult maintenance"; 365 calories per cup; the chart for 51–100 lb dogs is 3–5 cups [S17].
- Both are "formulated to meet" the standards, rather than tested in a feeding trial. Both have taurine added [S16, S17] (Commercial).
- Neither is labeled for growth, which matters for the puppy.
- Questions to ask the maker [S20]:
  - Who formulates the diets, and what are their qualifications?
  - What quality-control steps do you use?
  - Have you done any feeding trials or published research?

## 7. If you choose meal prep later (rules from the sources)
1. Use a recipe from a board-certified veterinary nutritionist, never an internet recipe [S1, S5, S6]. Some offer telehealth.
2. Follow it exactly. Don't swap ingredients or skip supplements [S5, S7].
3. Cook all meat. Don't feed raw or freeze-dried raw food [S24, S27, S28].
4. Keep foods on the ASPCA toxic list out of meals and treats [S31].
5. Re-check the recipe at least once a year, or when your dog's health changes [S7].
6. Wait until the puppy is about 12 months old, and until the senior reaches his goal weight [S6, S8].

## 8. Questions to ask your vet
1. What body condition score are each of my dogs right now (out of 9)?
2. What daily calorie target should Dog 1 have for safe weight loss, and how fast should he lose?
3. Should Dog 1 switch to a weight-management or prescription weight-loss food instead of eating less of his current food?
4. Should we check Dog 1 for thyroid or other problems before the diet?
5. What adult size do you expect for Dog 2? Should he eat a large-breed puppy food or a regular all-life-stages food, and until what age?
6. How many calories should Dog 2 eat at his age, and how will we track his growth?
7. Do the peas in our current food matter for heart health (DCM)?
8. If we want home-cooked meals someday, can you refer us to a board-certified veterinary nutritionist?

## 9. Confidence and gaps
- **High confidence:**
  - The puppy should be on a growth or all-life-stages food.
  - Homemade diets without professional formulation are usually incomplete.
  - Cutting back an adult maintenance food isn't the ideal way to slim a dog.
- **Moderate confidence:**
  - How much the peas matter for DCM.
  - The benefits of fresh food.
- **Gaps:**
  - No veterinary source on how fast to switch foods.
  - The AAHA guidelines and Purina's study page were blocked.
  - The Dog Aging Project page (S3) was blocked; its study was covered through S4.
  - S25 (senior nutrient review) yielded only its title and keywords.
  - No breed-specific studies for Heeler or Beagle mixes.
  - No direct test of Nutrish products for health outcomes.
