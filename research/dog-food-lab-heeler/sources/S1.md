# S1 — UC Davis News: "Homemade dog food recipes can be risky business, study finds"

- Access: OPENED (HTTP 200; full article text read, ~940 words incl. navigation) | Date read: 2026-11-08 | Published/updated: July 15, 2013 (by Andy Fell)
- Source type & bias: University press release (Expert body) summarizing the authors' own peer-reviewed study (S2). Promotional framing of in-house research. ## Findings
1. 200 recipes from 34 sources (veterinary textbooks, pet care books, websites) were analyzed with a computer-based nutrient program; described as "thought to be the largest ever nutritional evaluation" of such recipes. [Q1] [Expert body, reporting Research]
2. Only 9 of 200 recipes (8 of them written by veterinarians) met AAFCO minimums for adult dogs for all essential nutrients; only 5 (all by veterinarians) met National Research Council Minimum Requirements for adult dogs. [Q1] [Expert body, reporting Research]
3. 95 percent of the 200 recipes were lacking at least one essential nutrient; more than 83 percent had multiple deficiencies. [Q1][Q2] [Expert body, reporting Research]
4. Recipes written by veterinarians had fewer and less severe deficiencies, but most still had at least one. Only 4 of 200 were written by board-certified veterinary nutritionists, and all 4 had acceptable profiles for adult dogs. [Q1][Q5] [Expert body, reporting Research]
5. Deficiencies in choline, vitamin D, zinc and vitamin E were singled out as able to cause "immune dysfunction, accumulation of fat in the liver and musculoskeletal abnormalities" (author statement, not measured outcomes). [Q2][Q3] [Expert body]
6. Because many recipes shared the same deficiencies, rotating recipes ("balance over time") is "not likely to correct these problems." [Q1][Q5] [Expert body]
7. 92 percent of recipes had vague or incomplete instructions requiring at least one owner assumption (ingredients, preparation, or supplement use) — a source of recipe drift. [Q5] [Expert body, reporting Research]
8. 85 percent of recipes gave no calorie information and did not say what size of pet the recipe was for. [Q4][Q5] [Expert body, reporting Research]
9. Lab analysis of 15 prepared recipes broadly agreed with the computer analysis on deficiencies and excesses, "with only a few discrepancies." [Q1] [Expert body, reporting Research]
10. Recommendation: avoid general recipes from books and the Internet; consult a board-certified veterinary nutritionist for customized recipes. [Q5] [Expert body]
11. Standards used were for ADULT dogs only (AAFCO adult minimums; NRC adult minimums); no puppy/growth evaluation is described. [Q4] [Expert body]

## Relevance to these two dogs (Inferred)
- A generic online/book recipe is very likely to be incomplete even for the adult Lab × Heeler; the study only tested adult standards, so a growing 6-month-old puppy would likely face a stricter bar that these recipes were not even checked against.
- 85% of recipes lacked calorie info, which matters directly for the senior dog's planned ~5 lb weight loss and for portioning a growing puppy.
- Supports "if home-prepared, have a board-certified veterinary nutritionist formulate it and follow it exactly" over rotating free recipes.

## Limitations
- Press release, 2013; recipe landscape may have changed. Reports the study's numbers secondhand (primary abstract unavailable via Europe PMC — see S2).
- Nutrient adequacy judged against standards, not health outcomes in dogs; health effects quoted are the author's statement.
- Author conflict of interest (analysis-software ownership) disclosed.
- Says nothing about commercial diets' quality, cost, or safety beyond implied contrast.
