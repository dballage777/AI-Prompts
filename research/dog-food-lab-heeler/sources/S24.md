Veterinary reference written for professionals by Sherry Lynn Sanderson, DVM, PhD, DACVIM-SAIM, DACVIM-Nutrition (Univ. of Georgia) and peer reviewed by Elizabeth Prouty. Merck is a pharma publisher, but the page has no product promotion. Its references for the senior life-stage point are the Purina Institute (pet-food industry) and Cat Care For Life.

## Findings
1. AAFCO nutrient profiles cover two life stages, Adult Maintenance and Growth and Reproduction. NRC (2006) lists growth after weaning, adult maintenance, late gestation, and peak lactation. [Q1][Q4] [Expert body]
2. Neither AAFCO nor NRC recognizes that requirements may change in healthy older dogs. The adult stage "must be divided" into young adult, mature adult, and geriatric/super senior stages. [Q4] [Expert body]
3. Nutritional diseases are rare when owners feed "a good-quality, commercial, complete, and balanced diet or a homemade diet formulated by a Board Certified Veterinary Nutritionist." Problems occur most often with unbalanced homemade diets. [Q1][Q5] [Expert body]
4. Limited-ingredient homemade diets are often inadequate. For example, a hamburger-and-rice diet can cause calcium deficiency and secondary nutritional hyperparathyroidism, and an all-liver diet can cause vitamin A toxicity. [Q2][Q5] [Expert body]
5. Raw vs cooked: raw eggs (avidin, which destroys biotin), raw fish (thiaminases), and raw soybeans (trypsin inhibitors) contain antinutritional factors destroyed by cooking. Cooking can also reduce some nutrients. 74°C (165°F) minimum kills bacteria, worms, and protozoa; 100°C (212°F) is needed for bacterial spores. [Q2][Q5] [Expert body]
6. Energy needs are "not a linear function of body weight." They depend on activity, life stage, lean body mass, age, environment, and breed. Household dogs often need fewer calories than kennel dogs. [Q4] [Expert body]
7. The page gives RER/MER formulas. Its MER table lists separate multipliers for neutered, intact, "Obesity prone" adults and for puppies. Any formula "should be considered a starting point," and an individual animal may need "as much as 30% more or less" than calculated. [Q4] [Expert body]
8. If a dog needs 10%–30% less food than the label recommends to stay at ideal weight, consider a less energy-dense diet. Feeding a maintenance diet in a restricted way also reduces intake of all nutrients. [Q4][Q6] [Expert body]
9. Over-the-counter weight-management diets are less energy dense and are adjusted for nutrients. Even so, severe restriction on them may cause deficiencies. Therapeutic weight-loss diets are formulated to supply all nutrients at low calories. [Q4][Q6] [Expert body]
10. If a dog's calorie needs seem unusually low, check for underlying conditions such as hypothyroidism. [Q4] [Expert body]
11. Protein minimums on a dry-matter basis are AAFCO 22.5% (56.3 g/1,000 kcal ME) for growth and reproduction and 18% (45 g/1,000 kcal ME) for adult dogs. Signs of protein deficiency include weight loss and skeletal muscle atrophy. [Q1][Q4] [Expert body]
12. Dry foods contain 3%–11% water and canned foods 60% to >87%. Water should not be restricted in healthy animals. [Q7] [Expert body]
13. Most manufacturers that meet WSAVA guidelines add extra protein to allow for small cooking losses. [Q6] [Expert body]

## Exact quotes (final URL above)
- "If maintenance diets are fed in a calorically restricted fashion, there is a reduction in the intake of all nutrients in the diet, and maintenance diets are not formulated to be fed in that way."
- "Any given animal may require as much as 30% more or less of the calories calculated for its body weight than another animal with the same body weight."
- "neither AAFCO nor NRC recognizes that nutritional requirements may change in healthy, older adult dogs and cats"

## Relevance to these two dogs (Inferred)
- For the 9-year-old, cutting the adult Nutrish portion to lose weight also cuts every nutrient. A vet may suggest a weight-management or therapeutic diet instead of a large cut, and may screen for conditions such as hypothyroidism.
- The puppy needs a Growth (or All Life Stages) food. The adult-maintenance profile is a different AAFCO category, with a lower protein minimum, for example.
- There is no official AAFCO "senior" profile. A "senior" label is not a regulated nutrient standard (per this page's statement on AAFCO and NRC).

## Limitations
- Professional reference with no dedicated obesity or weight-loss protocol on this page. It may be in a separate Merck chapter that was not read.
- The senior life-stage claim cites the Purina Institute and Cat Care For Life, not primary studies.
- I did not compute calorie targets for these dogs (outside scope; no doses).
