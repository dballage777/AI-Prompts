# S14 — IVC Journal: Feeding large breed puppies
- URL: https://ivcjournal.com/feeding-large-breed-puppies/ | Final URL: same (HTTP 200)
- Access: OPENED (full article text read) | Date read: 2026-11-08 | Published/updated: March 1, 2013 (no update shown)
- Source type & bias: Reported (trade magazine for integrative and holistic vets, Redstone Media). By Susan Wynn, DVM, former president of the American Academy of Veterinary Nutrition. Her board-certification status is not stated on the page. The article is old (2013), has few references, and focuses on LARGE-breed puppies, partly from a raw/"paleolithic" diet angle.

## Findings
1. Some breeders have recommended adult foods for puppies to avoid growth problems. The author calls one such case (a grainless adult diet) "a big feeding mistake." [Q4] [Reported]
2. The most important factors for preventing developmental orthopedic disease (DOD) are growth rate (proportional to calorie intake) and dietary calcium level. [Q4][Q2] [Reported]
3. The idea that high protein causes skeletal problems in large-breed puppies "was disproved" (Nap, 1991). Protein of 23% to 31% (dry matter) showed no harmful effect on growth. [Q4][Q3] [Reported, citing Research]
4. Adult maintenance foods are often less calorie-dense than puppy foods. They may also have more calcium and phosphorus relative to calories than a large-breed puppy diet. A fast-growing puppy eating more adult food to meet its calorie needs can get a calcium overdose. A "calorie-calcium mismatch is probably common." [Q4][Q2] [Reported]
5. Worked example: a major over-the-counter adult maintenance dry food contained 22% protein, 1.1% calcium and 0.8% phosphorus as fed. Converted, that is 69 grams/1,000 kcal protein and 3.5 grams/1,000 kcal calcium. The calcium was correct, and the protein was "adequate but a little marginal for growth." [Q4][Q6] [Reported]
6. A popular all-life-stages raw diet met AAFCO growth figures as fed (0.56% calcium, 0.38% phosphorus), but on an energy basis it supplied 7.5 grams/1,000 kcal calcium, "too much calcium for large breed puppies." Label adequacy does not guarantee suitability for large breeds. [Q1][Q2][Q6] [Reported]
7. Guidelines for large, fast-growing puppies (dry matter): about 30% protein, 9% fat, 1.5% calcium (around 3 grams/1,000 kcal), 0.8% to 1% phosphorus, and a calcium-to-phosphorus ratio of 1:1 to 1.3:1. [Q4] [Reported]
8. Check BCS weekly in puppies. Keep large, rapidly growing puppies at 4/9. "Never free feed." [Q4] [Reported]
9. Treats add calories, should be small, and should not contain added calcium. Avoid calcium-containing supplements and treats. There is no study evidence either way on glucosamine for large-breed puppies. [Q7][Q2][Q5] [Reported]
10. The switch from puppy to adult food should happen when the dog is near its adult frame and its needs fall, which "does not happen at an arbitrary age." Calorie needs drop after spay or neuter, "sometimes pretty dramatically." [Q4][Q8] [Reported]
11. Puppy or all-life-stages foods fed to adults make an ideal BCS harder to maintain. In obese-prone adults fed only 50% to 60% of expected calories, cutting a regular diet can cause nutrient deficiencies. Such dogs need a weight-management diet or a supplemented homemade diet. [Q4][Q2] [Reported]
12. Homemade diets for large-breed puppies should be analyzed or designed by a veterinary nutritionist. Feeding-trial-tested foods are preferred over foods "simply been designed on a computer." [Q5][Q1] [Reported]

## Exact quotes

- "since manufacturers formulate their diets so that nutrients are provided in proportion to the calories, some of these dogs may actually experience nutrient deficiencies." — same URL

## Relevance to these two dogs (Inferred)
- The puppy is probably not large-breed, so the calcium cutoffs here may be less critical for him. The general point still applies: an adult food's nutrients are set for adult needs per calorie, and a puppy eating more of it is not the same as eating a growth food.
- For the 9-year-old's ~5 lb weight loss, Finding 11 says that simply cutting cups of a regular food too deeply can short him on nutrients. That supports involving the vet or using a weight-management formula.

## Limitations
- From 2013, so it predates later AAFCO and DCM developments. It is a trade magazine with an integrative-medicine slant and only 4 references. The "major OTC" product is unnamed. It is focused on large and giant breeds. Some advice (for example "ignore the feeding quantities... on commercial bags") is aimed at fresh or raw feeding.
