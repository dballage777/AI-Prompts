# S15 — dvm360: Feeding growing puppies (Proceedings)
- URL: https://www.dvm360.com/view/feeding-growing-puppies-proceedings | Final URL: same (HTTP 200)
- Access: OPENED (full text read; one minor page-markup artifact near a heading, with no content loss apparent) | Date read: 2026-11-08 | Published/updated: August 1, 2011
- Source type & bias: Expert body (board-certified veterinary nutritionist, published as conference proceedings in a veterinary trade outlet). By Cailin R. Heinze, VMD, MS, DACVN. Written for vets. The page is old (2011), and Tufts Petfoodology discloses that Dr. Heinze has industry ties.

## Findings
1. Traditional advice "such as switching growing large breed dogs to adult food or not using growth diets at all, are no longer appropriate and may be harmful." [Q4] [Expert body]
2. "All life stages" foods are essentially puppy diets that can also be fed to adults. Some are marketed only as adult foods but have been tested for growth. They may have too many calories for adults at maintenance. [Q6][Q4] [Expert body]
3. There are no AAFCO or legal definitions for large-breed growth diets, and no rule that they be tested in large-breed puppies. In general they are lower in calcium and energy density. [Q1][Q6] [Expert body]
4. Per the author, the majority, and the most severe cases, of nutrition-related disease in growing puppies come from home-prepared diets. "Nearly all" online and book recipes are deficient, often in nutrients essential for growth. [Q2][Q5][Q3] [Expert body, author experience]
5. Growing puppies should not be fed home-prepared diets. Owners should wait until at least 1 year (small breeds) or 18 months (giant breeds) and consult a board-certified veterinary nutritionist. [Q5][Q4] [Expert body]
6. Raw diets, home-made or commercial, should not be fed to growing puppies. The reasons given are nutrient deficiencies and a greater risk of food-borne illness for developing immune systems. [Q2] [Expert body]
7. The two major nutritional risk factors for DOD are rapid growth (excess calories) and excess calcium. "Even a slightly fat puppy is likely growing at an unsafe rate." [Q4][Q2] [Expert body]
8. In a cited Labrador study, free-fed puppies had "over two times the rate of hip dysplasia" compared with littermates fed 75% of that intake. The restricted dogs had less osteoarthritis at 8 years and a longer lifespan (18-24 months). Caloric restriction did not change final size. [Q3][Q4] [Expert body, citing Research: Kealy 1992, Runge 2008, Kealy 2002]
9. Puppies absorb calcium with "minimal regulation" for the first 6 months. NRC figures: safe upper limit 4.5 g/Mcal, recommended allowance 3 g/Mcal. The author looks for 3-3.6 g/Mcal in diets for large and giant breeds. [Q4][Q2] [Expert body]
10. Adult maintenance diets are "likely to be deficient in essential nutrients (including calcium) needed for growth." Feeding more of a lower-calorie adult diet can also overshoot calcium. [Q4][Q2] [Expert body]
11. Toy, small and medium breeds (< 50 lb adult): feed a growth or all-life-stages food that has passed AAFCO feeding trials until adult size (or 1 year), then transition. Keep BCS at 5/9, meal-feed, and weigh and review intake every 2 weeks during active growth. [Q4][Q8] [Expert body]
12. Large and giant breeds (> 50 lb adult): feed a large-breed growth diet until 12-24 months and keep BCS at 4/9. Mixes of unknown parentage, or mixes prone to orthopedic disease, are safest on a large-breed food. [Q4] [Expert body]
13. Energy needs fall after spay or neuter, and many puppies need "up to 20% less." Growing puppies should get no vitamin or mineral supplements on a balanced commercial diet. [Q4][Q5] [Expert body]

## Relevance to these two dogs (Inferred)
- The 6-month-old Heeler/Beagle mix eating an adult food is in the situation this source calls potentially harmful. If his expected adult weight is under 50 lb, the source's plan is a growth or all-life-stages food (feeding-trial tested) until about 1 year. The vet should confirm the expected adult size.
- This is the strongest source against home meal prep for the puppy right now. Any home-prepared plan would wait until at least 12 months and need a board-certified veterinary nutritionist.
- The two figures on post-surgery calorie drops differ: up to 20% here, up to 30% in S11. Both point toward reducing food at spay or neuter.

## Limitations
- From 2011, so it predates the FDA DCM investigation and current products. It is written for vets. Point 4 rests on the author's clinical experience, not a cited study. The Kealy study was in Labradors (a large breed), so it may apply less directly to a medium mix.
