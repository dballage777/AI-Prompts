# S6 — Should you make your own pet food at home? (Tufts Petfoodology)
- URL: https://sites.tufts.edu/petfoodology/2016/07/14/should-you-make-your-own-pet-food-at-home/ | Final URL: same (no redirect)
- Access: OPENED (full article text read, about 990 words; no block page) | Date read: 2026-11-08 | Published/updated: July 14, 2016 (no update date shown)
- Source type & bias: Expert body. Author Cailin R. Heinze, VMD, MS, DACVIM (Nutrition), Tufts Cummings School. The Tufts Nutrition Service sells home-cooked recipe consultations (a possible interest), but the post mostly advises caution. The post is 10 years old.

## Findings
1. "There is no evidence to support claims that home-prepared diets are healthier than commercial diets." [Q3] [Expert body]
2. Very few pets need a home-cooked diet for health reasons, and an improperly prepared one "can seriously harm your pet's health, especially for a growing kitten or puppy." [Q2][Q4] [Expert body]
3. Commercial foods must legally meet or exceed set nutrient amounts to be marketed as "complete and balanced". Studies (cited: JAVMA 2012, 2012, 2013) found that most owner-designed recipes, and recipes from magazines, books or the internet, are deficient in one or more essential nutrients. [Q1] [Expert body citing Research]
4. Deficiencies may not show for weeks or even years in adult animals, until a serious health problem appears that may not be easily reversed. [Q2][Q3] [Expert body]
5. The author reports seeing puppies brought to the emergency room "with broken bones and seizures due to inadequate nutrients," and cats with taurine-deficiency heart disease and blindness. [Q2][Q4] [Expert body, clinical anecdote]
6. Occasional home-cooked meals for healthy adult pets on special occasions are unlikely to cause problems beyond a possible upset stomach, as long as foods toxic to pets are avoided. [Q2][Q7] [Expert body]
7. Good recipes give precise gram amounts of specific ingredients and add vitamin and mineral sources (calcium, B vitamins, iron, zinc, essential fatty acids). A "whole foods only" approach can almost never meet all needs. [Q5] [Expert body]
8. Most pet vitamin and mineral supplements are not enough. Specific veterinary supplements, or multiple human supplements ("potentially as many as 7-9 different products"), are typically needed. [Q5] [Expert body]
9. The advice is to get a recipe from a board-certified veterinary nutritionist (acvn.org) or a PhD animal nutritionist who uses formulation software. [Q5] [Expert body]
10. Recipe drift: in a Tufts client survey, "Greater than 80% of pet owners had made changes to their recipes," and many changes could cause too little or too much of a nutrient. Even swapping one meat for another alters nutrients and calories. [Q5] [Expert body citing Research (Johnson et al., J Small Anim Pract 2016)]
11. Home-cooked diets "should not be fed to growing kittens or puppies or pregnant or nursing animals." [Q4] [Expert body]
12. Home-cooked diets can be healthy but are time-consuming and "should not be undertaken lightly." [Q7] [Expert body]

## Relevance to these two dogs (Inferred)
- Dog 2 (6-month-old puppy): this source says plainly that a home-cooked diet should not be fed to a growing puppy, so an all-homemade diet for Dog 2 conflicts with expert advice.
- Dog 1 (senior, weight loss): the source allows home-cooking for adults only with a nutritionist-formulated recipe that is followed exactly. Weight loss is addressed more directly in S8.
- The occasional special-occasion meal is described as low risk, which supports small, non-toxic extras rather than full meal prep.

## Limitations
- Published 2016 with no update date. Cited studies are from 2012–2016.
- Short general blog post. Gives no specific nutrient levels and does not discuss raw diets or food handling in detail.
- The clinical cases are the author's observations, not systematic data.
