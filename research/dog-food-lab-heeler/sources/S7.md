## Findings
1. Nutritionists formulate by calories, not volume. "Most commercial dry foods are between 300-400 calories (kcal) per cup." Home-cooked foods are much lower per cup because they hold more moisture. [Q4][Q6][Q7] [Expert body]
2. Example: chicken breast and rice are "just over 200 kcal per cup each." Going from a 400 kcal/cup dog food to a chicken-and-rice homemade diet means feeding "about twice as much volume." [Q4][Q7] [Expert body]
3. Lower-fat, grain-free, lower-starch, or vegetable-heavy homemade diets end up with even larger volumes, because vegetables such as green beans, broccoli and zucchini are low in calories per volume. [Q4][Q7] [Expert body]
4. Dog foods for healthy dogs are "typically 20-28% of calories from protein." Label percentages are by weight, not by calories. A typical dog recipe can look like far more carbohydrate than protein. [Q1][Q6] [Expert body]
5. It is a misconception that a balanced homemade diet can be made without concentrated supplements. Supplements are needed to supply consistent amounts of all essential nutrients at a manageable time and cost. [Q1][Q5] [Expert body]
6. In a Tufts client survey, "only 13% of clients were following the original recipes that were prescribed 6-12 months later." Adjusting or leaving out supplements was a common change and "can result in a very unbalanced, unhealthy diet." [Q5] [Expert body citing Research]
7. Cooking method changes nutrients. For example, baking potatoes instead of boiling gives "~20% higher potassium concentrations." Cooking all raw ingredients together in one pot instead of separately can change calories and nutrients. [Q5] [Expert body]
8. Ingredient swaps change the diet. Using sweet potatoes in place of the same volume or weight of white potatoes gives "a ~60% increase in calories." [Q5] [Expert body]
9. "Never change a recipe without talking to the nutritionist who formulated it!" [Q5] [Expert body]
10. Owners who change calories by cutting fat or carbohydrate alone, or by adding only protein or fat, can "dramatically change the nutrient profile." If no nutritionist is available, scale every ingredient, oils and supplements included, by the same percentage, preferably measured in grams. [Q4][Q5] [Expert body]
11. Individual metabolic rates vary, so even a professionally formulated recipe may cause weight gain or loss. Calories need monitoring and adjustment. [Q4] [Expert body]

## Relevance to these two dogs (Inferred)
- The owner measures the current food in cups (about 2 cups/day each). A homemade diet would need to be measured in calories and grams. Equal cups of homemade food would not supply equal calories, which matters for Dog 1's weight-loss goal.
- For Dog 1's weight loss, this source warns against simply cutting the fat or carbohydrate in a homemade recipe. Calorie changes should be proportional across all ingredients or guided by a professional.
- Recipe drift (only 13% adherence) is a real risk for a busy household. It argues for a simple, consistent plan whatever the owner chooses.

## Limitations
- Aimed at owners who already have nutritionist-made recipes. It does not compare home-cooked and commercial outcomes.
- The ~20% potassium and ~60% calorie figures are single examples, not general rules.
- The 13% adherence figure is stated as "6-12 months later" here and as "a median of 1 year later" in S8. These are small wording differences about the same Tufts study.
