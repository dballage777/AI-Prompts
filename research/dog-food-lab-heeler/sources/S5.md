# S5 — Texas A&M Stories: "Most homemade dog diets lack nutrients, Texas A&M study finds"
- URL: https://stories.tamu.edu/news/2025/11/21/most-homemade-dog-diets-lack-nutrients-texas-am-study-finds/ | Final URL: same (no redirect)
- Access: OPENED (HTTP 200; full article text read, ~900 words incl. navigation) | Date read: 2026-11-08 | Published/updated: Nov. 21, 2025 (by Courtney Price, Texas A&M CVMBS)
- Source type & bias: University news story (Expert body) about its own consortium's study (S4); quotes study authors including a board-certified veterinary nutritionist. Promotional of in-house research; names a specific commercial tool (Balance It) without discussing alternatives.

## Findings
1. Study (S4, Am J Vet Res) assessed 1,726 homemade diets from owner-provided ingredients and preparation methods; only 6% "had the potential to be nutritionally complete." [Q1] [Expert body, reporting Research]
2. Because exact ingredient amounts weren't collected, "it's possible that a smaller percentage than 6% were nutritionally complete" (Dr. Janice O'Brien). [Q1] [Expert body]
3. Diets were entered into Balance It, an online tool for creating homemade diets, which the author describes as compliant with FDA and AAFCO recommendations for canine nutrition. [Q1][Q5] [Expert body; tool is Commercial]
4. Dog Aging Project has more than 50,000 enrolled dogs; diet data came from open-ended owner surveys. [Q1] [Expert body]
5. Board-certified nutritionist Dr. Katie Tolbert: if formulating at home, work with a board-certified veterinary nutritionist and "stick to the diet exactly as prescribed"; nothing is a "filler" to leave out. [Q5] [Expert body]
6. Substituting a different oil can change nutritional completeness; omitting a calcium supplement can "cause serious problems." [Q2][Q5] [Expert body]
7. Calcium–phosphate imbalance can cause bone problems including "rubber jaw" (bone softening) and can also cause kidney problems (Tolbert). [Q2] [Expert body]
8. Dogs not already in good health can have exacerbated symptoms without a balanced diet formulated for their disease. [Q2][Q4] [Expert body]
9. Avoid unsafe additions such as grapes ("can be toxic to dogs") and whole bones (risk of sharp bone pieces in the stomach). [Q2] [Expert body]
10. Recommended steps: first talk with your local veterinarian about the pet's needs, then work with a board-certified veterinary nutritionist; for long-term homemade feeding, send a sample to a food testing laboratory to confirm the diet matches the recipe. [Q5] [Expert body]
11. Homemade diets have risen in popularity over the last two decades (stated without data). [Q7] [Reported]

## Relevance to these two dogs (Inferred)
- Calcium/phosphorus balance is highlighted as a bone-health risk; this is especially relevant to a growing 6-month-old puppy, though the article does not discuss puppies specifically.
- For the senior dog, "go off script" drift (swapping oils, skipping supplements) is a practical risk of home meal prep; a vet visit first is the recommended starting point for both dogs, including the weight-loss goal.
- Grapes and whole bones should be kept out of any home-prepared meals or toppers.

## Limitations
- Institutional news story; secondary account of S4. No health-outcome data. No information on cost, time, commercial food selection, puppies, or weight loss.
- Promotes one commercial formulation tool by name; its FDA/AAFCO "compliance" is the author's characterization, not independently verified here.
