## Findings (5–15; each: text [Q#] [label])
1. Labels mix required info with marketing; unregulated terms "such as 'holistic' or 'premium', is of little practical value for nutritional assessment." [Q6] [Expert body]
2. Brand question 1 — Do they employ a Nutritionist? Appropriate qualifications: "a PhD in Animal Nutrition or Board Certification by the American College of Veterinary Nutrition (ACVN) or the European College of Veterinary Comparative Nutrition (ECVCN)." Ask name, qualifications, employment status; "Consultants may have limited influence compared to a staff Nutritionist." [Q6][Q5] [Expert body]
3. Brand question 2 — Who formulates the diet? Experienced formulator (MS or PhD in Animal Nutrition), a veterinarian, or a pet owner/breeder/trainer? "Recipe development is a complex process requiring knowledge of nutrition, raw materials, and processing not taught in veterinary school programs." [Q5][Q6][Q1] [Expert body]
4. Brand question 3 — Quality control: does the diet meet AAFCO/FEDIAF profile "based on analysis using a nutrient database or on chemical analysis of the finished product?" QC should include ingredient validation, final diet nutrient analysis, toxicology, bacteriology, packaging/shelf-life screenings. [Q1][Q2][Q6] [Expert body]
5. Brand question 4 — What research/nutrition studies; published in peer-reviewed journals? Companies "are not required to conduct or sponsor nutritional research," but doing so "indicates a commitment to animal health and wellness." [Q3][Q6] [Expert body]
6. Label check 1 — Nutrition Adequacy Statement: is it complete; label may specify feeding trials vs formulation. "Short-term", "intermittent", or "complementary" foods "should only be fed as a small portion of the diet (10% or less)" or under vet supervision. [Q1][Q6][Q7] [Expert body]
7. Life stage match: AAFCO and FEDIAF give nutrient levels for reproduction, growth, adult; "Diets labeled 'for all life-stages' are formulated for reproduction and growth." [Q4][Q6] [Expert body]
8. Label check 2 — Calories per gram or serving: obesity increasing; accurate calorie content "can help prevent unintended overfeeding." Calorie info "is only required on pet food labels in the US." [Q4][Q6] [Expert body]
9. Label check 3 — Company contact (phone/email); companies "should be able to provide an 'average' or 'typical' analysis for all essential nutrients." [Q6] [Expert body]
10. Label check 4 — Who makes the food: "Made by" (own plant) vs "Made for"/"Distributed by" (third-party manufacturer). [Q6] [Expert body]
11. Bottom line: "If the manufacturer cannot or will not provide any of this information, veterinarians and owners should be cautious about feeding that brand." [Q6] [Expert body]

## Relevance to these two dogs (Inferred, 1–3 bullets)
- The Nutrish pages read (S16–S18) answer some WSAVA checks (adequacy statement, kcal/cup, owner company) but not others (named nutritionist and credentials, nutrient-database vs chemical analysis, published research); the owner would need to contact the company for those.
- For Dog 2, the life-stage check fails for the current adult-maintenance foods; an all-life-stages/growth food matches. The 10%-or-less rule for complementary items applies to toppers/treats for both dogs.
- The formulator point also bears on home meal prep (Q5): recipe design needs nutrition expertise.
