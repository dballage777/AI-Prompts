## Learned points
1. [Documented] NoveList formed a team in spring 2009 to add appeal factors to book records, creating a controlled appeal vocabulary for catalogs, led by a cataloger (Caplinger). The goal (Duncan Smith) was a vocabulary that "will resonate with readers, even if they might not have thought of it themselves."
2. [Documented] The source models compared were Saricks (2nd ed. *Genre Fiction*): six factors (Pacing, Characterization, Storyline, Tone/Mood, Frame/Setting, Style/Language). Wyatt (*RA Guide to Nonfiction*): eight factors (Pacing, Characterization, Storyline, Details, Learning/Experiencing, Language, Setting, Tone). Nancy Pearl's four "doorways": Story, Character, Setting, Language.
3. [Documented] There was a design trade-off: too many separate categories overwhelm users in the interface, while collapsing too many distinct appeal concepts loses meaning.
4. [Documented] The original 2013 breakouts were four: Storyline (including character- vs plot-driven and other structure terms), Pace, Tone, and Writing Style (including language and details). They display alongside Subject, Location and Genre headings. Character appeal terms are not listed in these four; S4 shows a Character category later.
5. [Documented] Pace is "the rate at which the story unfolds." Only clearly fast or slow books are tagged, because per Wyatt pace is only active at the extremes.
6. [Documented] Tone got the most effort because it "reaches the emotional core of the book." Setting terms were folded into Tone, following Saricks's view that setting as appeal means background and tone, not geographic place.
7. [Documented] Richly Detailed (Wyatt's "Details") sits in Writing Style. A future goal was to tie it to subject headings (what the detail is about, e.g., wine-making, antiques, forensics).
8. [Documented] Core difficulty: appeal "depends not only on the book itself, but upon a reader's response to it," unlike yes/no subject headings. The same word means different things to different people (heartwarming vs saccharine), and different words mean the same thing (chilling, eerie, creepy), so similar terms were grouped.
9. [Documented] Saricks warned against reader-dependent "weasel words" like "satisfying," which could mean heartwarming or creepy depending on mood. Wyatt advised choosing the "bluntest instrument," a single clear term per concept in a catalog record, unlike nuanced reviews.
10. [Documented] Method: start with top-priority authors and look at the whole corpus of an author's work; read professional reviews in depth; also include reader reviews, because professional critics contradict each other and can be harsher than the public (e.g., Stephen King). The aim is a "shared view" that is accurate for the largest number of readers.
11. [Documented] Negative appeal (e.g., cardboard characters, hackneyed prose) is deliberately not tagged, for the most part.
12. [Documented] Wyatt urged actively soliciting user feedback to correct mistaken terms: "we would not get it right 100 percent of the time."
13. [Reported] RA voices quoted (Trott, Naik, Stover) argue that appeal vocabulary should absorb how readers talk on Goodreads and in tags. David Wright proposed collaborative tagging that combines the flexibility of tags with controlled-vocabulary standardization.
14. [Documented] Worked example: subject headings for Atwood's *The Blind Assassin* give Canada, the 1940s, family secrets and sisters. Appeal adds character-driven, bleak, haunting, lyrically complex. Searching "bleak, lyrical" surfaces options "a subject heading search alone would never have uncovered."
15. [Documented] NoveList was then building two format-based appeal vocabularies, one for illustration (picture books and graphic novels) and one for audiobooks. This followed Wyatt's point that story is increasingly "format-dependent."
16. [Documented] Conclusion: cataloging traditionally describes content, and appeal describes the "experience." Users are best served by both, the "descriptive and the experiential."
17. [Inferred] For a cross-media recommender: tag positive appeals only, use single blunt terms with synonym grouping, triangulate critic and audience reviews, and add format-specific facets.
