## Learned points
1. [Reported] Appeal is defined as the elements of books readers respond to. Barry Trott calls appeal terms tools to connect books readers may not have thought of, "to cross genres and to go between fiction and nonfiction."
2. [Reported] There are four traditional appeal concepts: pacing, character, story line, and frame. Pacing is how the reader moves through the story arc (Matthew Reilly is rapid and high-octane; Alice Munro is measured and reflective). Character is how individuals behave and relate to each other and to the reader. Story line and frame are "less easily codified."
3. [Reported] Appeal is "not about subject." Duncan Smith (NoveList creator) says appeal shifted focus "away from material to its experience." Ike Pulver calls appeal a "feeling taxonomy" that classifies adjectivally (big, fast, exciting, intricate, thought-provoking) rather than nominally (horses, ships, cheese).
4. [Reported] Origin: appeal was introduced in 1989 in Saricks and Brown's Readers' Advisory Service in the Public Library and stayed stable until this rethink. New ideas come from listening to readers, including web tagging and blogs.
5. [Reported] There are four areas of change: (a) reading-experience terms (description, language, learning/experiencing, settings, style); (b) feeling terms (affect, mood, tone); (c) story terms (content, genre, subject, theme, type); and (d) design and format (physical layout, format).
6. [Reported] Character and pacing were not challenged, which the article reads as consensus on their usefulness. Frame is called "too reductive" and is being split into description, language, settings, style, affect, mood, and tone. Frame is recast as details and description that build the story's world.
7. [Reported] Story line is "a conglomeration of multiple subappeals" (Pulver). Subject is being re-introduced as intertwined with appeal, which the article calls "new and boggy ground" (open debate).
8. [Reported] Format and design are a new appeal dimension: audio, graphic, large print, hypertext, or "as a game." Audio narration can remove part of the experience (illustrations lost in an audio edition of a Picoult novel) or add to it (Simon Prebble's narration of Jonathan Strange & Mr. Norrell weaving in the footnotes). Readers "often talk about what they want in terms of films and television shows" (Teresa Jacobsen). Story is "increasingly format-dependent."
9. [Reported] Saricks's appeal meta-groups ("bigger boxes"), which allow "genre slides" (Olson):
   - Adrenaline (thrillers, adventure, suspense, romantic suspense): pacing is key, and readers talk about plot, scenes, and action.
   - Intellect (sf, literary fiction, mysteries, psychological suspense): language, the character's inner life, and "What if?" ("books of the mind").
   - Emotion (romance, horror, gentle reads, chick lit, women's lives and relationships): feeling, tone, mood, character reactions, setting, and participating in characters' lives.
   - Landscape (Westerns, fantasy, historical): a driving sense of place and detailed world building.
10. [Reported] Pearl's four doorways (story, setting, character, language) work as a sliding scale. Every book has all four, but their sizes differ. "Sure bets" with many readers (Lord of the Rings, Harry Potter) have all doorways large, a balanced profile.
11. [Reported] Pearl reads doorways from behavior. Language readers read slowly to savor words; story readers describe their reading as fast; setting readers say they know the place like their hometown; character readers talk about characters. These are self-reports a recommender can listen for.
12. [Reported] Worked example ("I just finished the last Lee Child novel"). Pearl reads it as the story doorway and suggests fast thrillers with appealing heroes (Hunter, Burdett), then crosses genre to Shane (slower but a strong hero in a tough situation) and Altered Carbon (sf, "impossible to put down"). Saricks reads it as adrenaline (pacing, short timeframe, cinematic, a loner hero) and suggests Coben, Deutermann, and Eisler, then crosses to Westerns (Hondo) and Western-set mysteries because of the "rides into town, solves the problem" hero pattern.
13. [Inferred] Both experts move across genres by keeping one or two dominant appeals (hero type, pace) and letting others vary, which is the same move needed for cross-media matching.
14. [Reported] Smith calls the work "learning to speak reader." The article states that there is no agreed vocabulary yet ("we have yet to agree on a vocabulary that really codifies RA work").
15. [Reported] Expert panel: Mary K. Chelton, Neil Hollands, Teresa Jacobsen, Georgine Olson, Nancy Pearl, Ike Pulver, Joyce Saricks, Duncan Smith, Barry Trott, and Neal Wyatt.
