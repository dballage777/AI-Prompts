## Learned points
1. [Reported] Williamson proposes one umbrella term, "materials matchmaking," instead of separate "viewers' advisory," "listeners' advisory," and "readers' advisory," so the result set stays broad and "unlimited by format."
2. [Reported] First step in any interaction: find out whether the person wants a specific suggestion, an answer to a question, or "a barrage of possibilities." For a recommender, this maps to: single pick vs. factual lookup vs. browse list.
3. [Reported] Saricks's appeal elements (pacing, characterization, story line, frame (physical setting and atmosphere), tone and mood, style and language) are said to transfer to viewers and listeners, but "some translation is required" and extra questions are needed.
4. [Reported] Film has "more contributing entities" than a book. Listen for whether the person is drawn to the overall film (director), the dialogue (writer), the characters (actors), or something else. Each points to a different set of suggestions.
5. [Reported] Genre terms and definitions are "directly transferable from print to film."
6. [Reported] Setting may matter even more to viewers than to readers. In many films setting is "almost a character unto itself," carrying theme music, moods, and intent. Films are said to create a stronger immediate emotional reaction because they engage more senses.
7. [Reported] Translation rule: cinematography in film "can be loosely equated" to a book's language and tone, acting as "the silent narrator." So a request for "dark and ominous" films may refer to pace and cinematography, not only plot or setting.
8. [Reported] Content tolerance (cursing, sexual content, violence) can differ by medium. Seeing and hearing affects some people more; imagining it while reading affects others more. Don't assume a reader's tolerance carries over to the screen. Ratings, warnings, and content notes help.
9. [Reported] Music: listeners often name a genre or a few artists but cannot say what attracts them. Use genre plus searchable criteria (label/imprint, release date or era), tempo, lyrical content, and the setting where they'll listen (workout vs. relaxation). Era matters for music in a way it usually doesn't for books.
10. [Reported] Quoting Wyatt, Pandora's dimensions (tempo, lyric, key) are framed as "appeal cousins" of RA's pace, character, language, setting, tone, and detail. This cross-media mapping supports treating appeal as one vocabulary used across formats.
11. [Reported] Audiobooks use standard appeal terms, since the content matches print (unless abridged), plus added facets: narration/narrative voice, reading style, recording length, where the person will listen, and format.
12. [Reported] Narrator claims: male voices are "generally more popular" and easier for older adults to hear (cited to MedlinePlus on hearing loss). Multicast performances suit old-radio fans and listeners who struggle to tell characters apart. Author-narrators lend authenticity and intimacy. For a new listener, send home female, male, and multicast samples so they can find their preference.
13. [Reported] The Audie Awards (30+ categories) are suggested as a source of appeal vocabulary for audio.
14. [Reported] "Becoming a recommendation engine": until a library product exists, staff must act as recommendation engines based on a customer's tastes and usage. The article quotes Iskold that good recommendations retain users and drive return business.
15. [Reported] Tools surveyed (2011): IMDb (filmographies; Parents Guide with content advisories on sex/nudity, violence/gore, profanity, alcohol/drugs/smoking, and frightening/intense scenes, rated 1–10 with reasons); Rotten Tomatoes (search by critic; with Flixster, a database of 250,000+ movies, 2.3 billion user reviews, and 500,000 critic reviews); Filmsite; TuneGlue (Last.fm/Amazon user-driven similarity map); Musicovery (mood compass: Energetic, Positive, Calm, Dark, plus decade and genre); AMG/AllMusic/AllMovie/AllGame; SongLyrics; Pandora (Music Genome Project, songs rated by trained experts on how they sound). Print film guides are judged "too static" for live matchmaking.
16. [Reported] Cross-promotion: "No material exists in a vacuum." TV alludes to books, soundtracks promote bands, and books are based on films and TV. Suggests themed multiformat bundles (book + DVD + CD), for example "Lost: The Multimedia Experience," "Date Night," "Frank Lloyd Wright," and "Chicago Blues."
17. [Reported] Offer matchmaking proactively and without jargon. For example, when someone places a hold on a popular title, ask if they want a movie "for tonight/the weekend."
18. [Reported] Staff training points: approach customers wearing concert or film apparel, write recommendations and staff picks, roundtable books, film, and music at meetings, mix formats in displays, and challenge the stereotype that libraries ignore pop culture.

## Limitations or bias
- Practitioner opinion; no data on how well cross-format suggestions work. The narrator-gender claim rests on a general hearing-loss reference, not on listener-preference research.
- Dated (2011): tools listed (TuneGlue, Musicovery, physical DVD/CD bundles, Flixster) are partly defunct; the streaming context is absent. Covers film, music, and audiobooks; no TV-specific, anime, or game guidance.
- Public-library service framing; the claims about library relevance are advocacy.

## Summary-only items
- None (full text read). The editor's introduction (Trott) frames format-based advisory as a coming challenge.
