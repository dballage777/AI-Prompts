## Learned points
1. [Reported] The column frames Saricks' appeal elements as "pacing, characterization, storyline, frame"; the editor's intro lists the elements as character, language, mood, setting, and story, and says appeal helps advisors connect works the reader "may not have considered."
2. [Reported] Taylor adds two reader-side considerations that advisors weigh almost by habit: the patron's mood and desired reading experience, and their tolerance for the cost in time and money a book demands (credited to Catherine Ross's research).
3. [Reported] Core concept: "non-appeal" factors, meaning what a book lacks, or includes when it could have been left out, and what people dislike. A Fiction_L contributor called this the "dark underbelly" of readers' advisory.
4. [Reported] Taylor avoids the term "negative appeal" on purpose: disliking qualities is not a negative trait in the reader, and "negative appeal" is a marketing term for fear-of-loss messaging. She also notes many practitioners already fold dislikes into appeal without saying so.
5. [Reported] Dislikes carry as much information as likes. The same books named in a Fiction_L "Books you didn't like" thread showed up again in the follow-up "Books I loved" thread, so one person's non-appeal is another's appeal.
6. [Reported] Method, the "disinterested" approach: set aside whether you liked a book and name the elements behind the feeling. Example: pages of description speak to pacing and mood. Some readers love deep description of a place, so a dislike becomes a usable descriptor.
7. [Reported] Principle: separate what patrons want from what the advisor thinks they should want. "The former drives us while the latter derails our mission."
8. [Reported] Method for titles you have not read or will not read: use tools such as NoveList for read-alikes, plus professional and newspaper reviews (Mary K. Chelton's advice).
9. [Reported] Method: bring in colleagues who read the genre you avoid. Patrons see this as taking the request seriously, not as a weakness.
10. [Reported] Method: give your opinion only when asked, and qualify it as one taste among many. Example quip: "more of a Homicide: Life on the Streets girl, and not a Murder She Wrote girl." Notice the TV references used to place a taste on the cozy-to-gritty spectrum.
11. [Reported] Method: invite patrons to explain a book's appeal back to you ("I just didn't get it"), framed as a challenge or a debate. Many readers like doing a service beyond reading for pleasure.
12. [Reported] When asked for a "good" book, the advisor should ask "Good for what?" or "In what way?" Readers are not always looking for books they will simply "like."
13. [Research] From six years of participant observation in six book groups: the "best" books are not necessarily the best for discussion. Book groups sometimes want books that split opinion; best discussions come from books some members liked and others disliked.
14. [Reported] A book group asked Fiction_L for books they would likely dislike, so the books would spark disagreement. That is a use case where the recommendation goal is debate, not enjoyment.
15. [Reported] Genre bias in book groups: Elizabeth Long found "mainstream" women's groups rarely treat genre books as part of the relevant literary universe. Patricia Gregory's interviews with ten St. Louis women's book clubs found romance, mysteries, and SF bestsellers "at the top of the list of forbidden books."
16. [Reported] Seattle Public Library's guide said good discussion books have "multidimensional characters" making hard choices, and that heavily plot-driven books "leave little to discuss," naming most mysteries, Westerns, romances, and SF/fantasy.
17. [Research] Taylor disputes that genre line: long-lasting groups in her study read "forbidden" genres, for example an all-women Mystery Group and a mostly male Science Fiction Group.
18. [Reported] "Discussability" is introduced as the central criterion for choosing book-group titles. Part 2 is said to define it; that part was not read.

## Limitations or bias
- Part 1 only. The definition of discussability and the role of non-appeal in group discussion are deferred to Part 2 (RUSQ 47-1), which was not read.
- Much of the evidence is one two-day mailing-list thread plus the author's dissertation fieldwork (six groups). The column gives no counts or systematic findings.
- Books and book groups only. The cross-media application is my inference.

## Summary-only items
- The editor's intro says Part 2 applies non-appeal to choosing book-group titles. I know this only from that summary.

## Best used for
- Treating dislikes ("non-appeal") as first-class signals and asking "good for what?" (enjoyment versus discussion or debate) when someone asks for a recommendation.
