## Learned points
1. [Documented] NoveList describes books through combined "story elements": genre, appeal (the "feeling" a reader seeks, e.g., inspiring or gritty) and theme (plot or trope, e.g., fish out of water, opposites attract). No single heading is enough. Using them together lets readers narrow to the "reading experience they want."
2. [Documented] Appeal categories in the 2021 edition, with audience flags (ages 0–8, 9–12, teen, adult) and fiction-only or nonfiction-only flags on some terms: Pace, Storyline, Tone, Character, Writing Style, Illustration, and Audio.
3. [Documented] Pace has only three terms: Fast-paced, Intensifying (builds momentum to a gripping end), and Leisurely paced. The rationale is that pace is only an "active appeal" at the extremes; nobody seeks "medium-paced."
4. [Documented] Storyline terms (focus and structure): Action-packed, Character-driven, Intricately plotted, Issue-oriented, Nonlinear, Open-ended, Own voices, Plot-driven, Sweeping, Unconventional, World-building.
5. [Documented] Tone is "the feeling that a book evokes in the reader" and best answers "What are you in the mood for?" The list has roughly 60 terms, e.g., Atmospheric, Bittersweet, Bleak, Creepy, Darkly humorous, Disturbing, Dramatic ("cinematic... larger-than-life"), Emotionally intense, Feel-good, Haunting, Heartwarming, Heartwrenching, Hopeful, Melancholy, Menacing, Moody, Nostalgic, Offbeat, Reflective, Sardonic, Strong sense of place, Suspenseful, Thought-provoking, Upbeat, Violent, Whimsical.
6. [Documented] Fine distinctions in tone are deliberate. "Feel-good" means a gentle, hopeful feeling, "not just a happy ending." Humor is split into light (amusing, funny, silly) and edgy (darkly humorous, sardonic). "Haunting" means memorable and stays with you, not scary.
7. [Documented] Content-intensity terms are part of tone. Romance heat runs Chaste → Mildly sensuous → Steamy → Explicit. Horror runs on a "spectrum of dread (think The Haunting of Hill House) to gore (think Clive Barker)": moody, creepy and menacing sit at the dread end; violent, gruesome and disturbing sit at the gore end.
8. [Documented] Setting is handled through tone ("Atmospheric," "Strong sense of place") plus a location subject heading. For a "passion for place," pair strong sense of place with a location subject "anything from Paris to Middle Earth."
9. [Documented] Character appeal is fiction only. Terms include Complex, Flawed, Likeable, Unlikeable, Sympathetic, Relatable, Brooding, Introspective, Quirky, Snarky, Sassy, Strong female, Twisted, Well-developed, Large cast of characters, Character duos, and diversity terms (ability, culturally, LGBTQIA, and religiously diverse). Tip: readers who don't mind "uncomfortable people" can try unlikeable or twisted characters.
10. [Documented] Writing Style terms cover language complexity and detail: Banter-filled, Candid, Compelling, Conversational, Descriptive, Dialect-filled, Gritty, Lush, Lyrical, Richly detailed, Spare, Stream of consciousness, Stylistically complex, Well-crafted dialogue, Witty, Wordplay-filled, and others.
11. [Documented] A format-specific Illustration vocabulary is used for picture books and graphic novels, manga included. Examples: Black-and-white, Cartoony (can lighten disturbing themes), Dark, Detailed, Fanciful, Impressionistic, Inventive (non-standard use of line, panels or color), Lavish, Muted, Nightmarish, Realistic, Raw, Surreal. This shows visual style treated as its own appeal axis that also carries tone.
12. [Documented] A format-specific Audio vocabulary covers performance, voice quality and delivery. Examples: Full cast, Audio drama, Read by the author, Multiple narrators, Sound effects, Musically enhanced; Brisk and Unhurried (pace through delivery); Chilling, Comedic, Deadpan, Wry, Intense, Somber (tone through performance); Immersive (captures time and place). Each format gets its own appeal terms for pace, tone and setting.
13. [Documented] Starter questions for the advisory conversation include asking about a book enjoyed, which movies and TV shows the person has enjoyed, what they are in the mood for today, and which favorite authors or series they wish had a new book. "Making Comparisons" uses paired contrasts: plot- vs character-driven, fast vs leisurely, "Amusing and upbeat, or creepy and gruesome?"
14. [Documented] Genre chapters pair genre with appeal and theme. Examples: thriller fans can modify "suspenseful" with fast-paced or action-packed (high-octane) or with intricately plotted or disturbing (psychological). For Game of Thrones fans: world-building, dramatic, large cast; for something "less grim," combine world-building with offbeat. Science fiction: intricately plotted plus world-building plus thought-provoking.
15. [Documented] A genre-differentiation heuristic: SF asks what if "our scientific theories are right," fantasy asks what if "magic exists," and horror asks what if "our nightmares are real."
16. [Documented] Themes are reader-community trope labels grouped by genre and by Characters, Plot or Setting (romance: enemies to lovers, fake relationship, second chance; SF: time loop, first contact; horror: final girls, found footage, cosmic horror). Many recur across genres ("Vengeance is mine," "Women of steel," "Band of survivors"), which makes them cross-genre matching hooks.
17. [Documented] Comics and Graphic Novels is treated as a format-genre with subheadings that include Manga, Manhwa, Manhua, Light novels and Webcomics. Library Search helpers include "Books to movies," "Books to TV," "Manga to Anime," "Media tie-ins" and "Franchise books," which are cross-media adaptation links.
18. [Documented] Boolean search combos use prefixes GN (genre), AP (appeal) and TH (theme), e.g., "GN science fiction AND AP fast-paced AND TH human 2.0" and "GN romance AND AP steamy AND TH marriage of convenience" (swap steamy for chaste to lower heat).
19. [Inferred] The structure maps directly to a recommender schema: genre + appeal facets (with intensity scales) + trope themes + format-specific facets (visual style, performance) + adaptation links.

## Limitations or bias
- Vendor marketing and reference material from 2021. Category names and several terms were changed or removed in 2024 (see S9: Tone→Mood, Pace moved into Style, Dramatic→Cinematic, Quirky, Compelling and Engaging deleted).
- Book-centric. There is no film, TV or game vocabulary, only links such as Books to movies, Books to TV and Manga to Anime.
- Some terms are US-political (Conservative, Liberal) or audience-specific (kids' tone words), which are less relevant for adults.
- The extracted PDF layout was jumbled, so a few examples could not be cleanly attributed.
