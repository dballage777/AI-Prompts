## Learned points
1. [Reported] Thesis: the appeal-factor model focuses on factors "intrinsic to the materials." That limits understanding of how readers experience reading, so RA should move toward a "more nuanced and reader-focused model."
2. [Reported] Timeline: the 1997 second edition of Saricks and Brown added "Articulating a Book's Appeal," listing pacing, characterization, storyline, frame and tone, and style. The opening question "tell me about a book you've read and enjoyed" was codified by Saricks in 1989.
3. [Reported] Appeal factors shaped guides, conferences, workshops, and "even the structure of RA databases like NoveList." Nancy Pearl's version calls them "doorways," or "experiential elements": story, characters, setting, language.
4. [Reported] Strength, quoting Keren Dali: appeal is concrete, easy to break into parts, easy to teach, and "readily adapted for the purpose of information retrieval in various RA tools". That is why it suits databases and recommenders.
5. [Reported] Core critique, quoting Beard and Thi-Beard via Dali: "a strange faith that, if we find better ways to describe the object, we can more easily connect the object to patron". Better item metadata alone does not equal a better match.
6. [Reported] Genre and appeal are both "intentional simplification[s]" built from properties of the material, not the reader. Ferri does not want them dropped, only supplemented with "why we read."
7. [Research] Browsing research (Baker 1996): "there is a lot of substitution going on." Frustration is a primary emotion for library visitors, and browsing may be a fallback when directed searches fail.
8. [Research] Goodall (1989), comparing eight 1980s UK studies: libraries assumed fiction readers know what they want, so readers developed the "browsing habit" and relied on serendipity rather than staff.
9. [Research] Mikkonen and Vakkari (2012): known author or title search is the most common tactic, and browsing is second (correlated with frequent visits and fiction-heavy reading). People over 45 and those with mid-level education were most likely to ask a librarian. Demographics and reading volume "did not account for a large proportion of the variance" in search strategies.
10. [Research] Catherine Ross's qualitative work with avid readers: mood ("what do I feel like reading") and the desired experience strongly guide choices, and many "personal factors interact" to define "a good book" at a given moment. In the Ross and Chelton (2001) model, appeal factors of the material are only one of five aspects of selection.
11. [Research] Ooi and Liew: mood was not influential for every reader, but life events (for example, a vacation) were. Other factors included personal networks, mass media consumption, money, busyness, and children.
12. [Research] Ross: avid readers build a selection system from "behind the eyes knowledge" and meta-knowledge (publishers, cover art, marketing conventions) plus trusted friends' recommendations. Ferri calls this a literacy skill.
13. [Research] Ross: good choices feed a "self-reinforcing system," while "disappointing choices kill the desire to read". Occasional readers choose less well and so read less. Ferri questions whether the data fully support this.
14. [Research] Saarinen and Vakkari (2013): some avid readers search the catalogue with index terms from novels they enjoyed. Occasional readers browse less and have a smaller mental repertoire. The study was small and mixed with Lukin's escapist/esthete/realist reader typology.
15. [Research] Dali (2014): replace "book appeal" with "reading appeal." It is a two-dimensional function of book-related and reader-driven elements, with appeal defined as "the power to invoke interest in reading and to set off an action of reading." It centers the reader's "anticipated effects."
16. [Research] Dali: appeal elements "are not static qualities of reading materials". They shift across pre-reading, reading, and post-reading. Ross similarly describes selection as a series of choices, starting with whether to read at all.
17. [Research] Dali (2013) calls for a "contextually grounded approach" to gathering appeal, rather than a keyword approach suited to reference questions. Duncan Smith (1996) urged a shift from book contents to the reader's experience.
18. [Reported] Ferri criticizes treating RA as an extension of reference (Beard and Thi-Beard 2008). That assumes the same librarian-guided search tools work for both.

## Limitations or bias
- Student literature review: secondary summaries of the studies, with no original data. Citation details are inconsistent (for example, Ooi and Liew dated 2010 in the text and 2011 in the bibliography; Saarinen and Vakkari dated 2012 and 2013).
- Argues one side (pro reader-focused). Books and libraries only.
- Dali's actual lists of book-related and reader-driven factors are not given here, only described.

## Summary-only items
- All findings attributed to Ross, Dali, Ooi and Liew, Mikkonen and Vakkari, Saarinen and Vakkari, Goodall, and Baker come through Ferri's summary. I did not read the primary studies.

## Best used for
- The main critique of item-only appeal metadata, and the case for modeling reader-side context (mood, life events, anticipated effects, selection skill) beside content appeal.
