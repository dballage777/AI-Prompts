## Learned points
1. [Reported] "Players' advisory" is presented as an emerging practice: connecting people with games they may need, want, or enjoy (Carl DiNardo, University of South Florida). It reuses RA skills but adds its own challenges. Covers board and tabletop games as well as other games.
2. [Reported] Games are harder to pitch on surface cues than books: a book's cover and summary make it easy to sell, but "it's a lot harder to be a poser" with games (Carolyn Vibbert).
3. [Reported] Recommendations often hinge on mechanics rather than look, which requires deeper familiarity with specific titles than RA usually does (Molly Porter).
4. [Reported] Staff can't know every game; a game café owner (Dana Buxbaum) asks staff to find "a game or 10" they are passionate about, runs monthly staff game nights to teach games, and keeps information in a shared spreadsheet and the point-of-sale system.
5. [Reported] Tools named for filling knowledge gaps: BoardGameGeek (information), Board Game Arena (play online), conventions, parent gamer blogs, the Dice Tower YouTube channel, and TikTok videos that show play or demonstrate mechanics.
6. [Reported] Common mistake: relying too much on subject matter or theme. "There are games with themes for almost anything"; for many games the learning is metacognitive (using and reflecting on mechanics to build strategy and problem-solving), though some, like Votes for Women, are historically accurate.
7. [Reported] Observation is part of the advisory interview: a student who took losing hard was steered to cooperative and "press your luck" games where he made his own decisions and success did not depend on other players.
8. [Reported] Example of translating an observed appeal: a teen in a Dungeons & Dragons campaign handled mechanics but "really ran with" role-playing, so was steered to Snake Oil, where portraying characters is central.
9. [Inferred] For a recommender, the panel's examples map to interaction-mode and emotional-tolerance appeals (cooperative vs. competitive, tolerance of losing) and to role-play/performance as an appeal distinct from theme.
