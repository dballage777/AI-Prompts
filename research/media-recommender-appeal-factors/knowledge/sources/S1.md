## Learned points
1. [Documented] The book defines the goal of readers' advisory (RA) as helping readers "discover titles they are currently in the mood to read" — mood/current state, not fixed taste, is the target.
2. [Documented] The "appeal framework" is the set of terms advisors use to classify works by features readers respond to: pacing, characterization, story line, frame/setting, tone, and language/style (six factors). Detailed definitions are deferred to Saricks's *Readers' Advisory Service in the Public Library* (3rd ed., 2005, pp. 40–73) and her Booklist "At Leisure" columns on tone/mood (2010) and language/style (2013); those definitions are not in this sample.
3. [Documented] Core thesis: instead of treating genre and appeal as two separate classification axes, the authors merge them and classify genres themselves by appeal ("Genre-Appeal framework").
4. [Documented] The framework has four appeal-based groupings: Adrenaline (Adventure, Thrillers, Suspense); Intellect (Psychological Suspense, Mystery, Literary Fiction, Science Fiction); Landscape (Fantasy, Westerns, Historical Fiction); Emotion (Relationship Fiction, Romance, Horror).
5. [Documented] Groupings are not read-alike lists: Relationship Fiction and Horror are "fundamentally different," but both draw fans through "visceral emotional engagement." The grouping explains shared underlying appeal, not interchangeability.
6. [Documented] One title carries different appeals for different audiences. Example: Dan Simmons's *The Terror* (also a limited TV series) — horror readers get mounting fear, historical-fiction readers get 1840s detail, literary readers get layered story, adventure fans get survival grit, TV viewers get tension, eeriness, setting and costuming. The advisor should describe the same work differently to each.
7. [Documented] "Post-genre" claim: genres blend, overlap and spawn short-lived "pop-up" niches (e.g., Laboratory Lit); authors (Grisham, Lehane, Jemisin, Carriger, Miéville, Butcher) cross genres; readers "follow authors and appeal elements that please them."
8. [Documented] Umbrella terms Crime Fiction, Speculative Fiction and Urban Fiction are used but judged "too broad to capture appeal at a granular level" (e.g., Horror and Magical Realism can be close or "light-years apart").
9. [Documented] Genres still matter: many readers like the core of a favorite genre and "dislike the infiltration by others," so the best answer for a "Mysteries only" reader may be a tightly conventional book or one that ranges beyond conventions. The advisor listens to how the reader describes enjoyed books.
10. [Documented] Each genre chapter lists appeal characteristics "in order of importance for each genre," so factor weight varies by genre (in Adrenaline, pacing is first and "all else is secondary").
11. [Documented] Each chapter has a "Reading the Whole Collection" section linking genre fiction to nonfiction and to film, audiobooks, music, podcasts, games and graphic novels; the series intro says the focus is appeal and "how appeal crosses genre, subject, and format," and the series serves "readers, viewers, and listeners."
12. [Documented] Cross-media pairings range from tight to loose: a tight appeal match between two Space Operas, a classic British domestic novel paired with the TV series *Downton Abbey*, or Science Fiction film soundtracks to accompany the genre.
13. [Documented] Pacing in Adrenaline is created by on-page clocks or time/date stamps, narrow time frames (days), telescoped events, short chapters, alternating points of view, cliffhanger chapter endings, reversals, plot twists and sudden revelations. This is a concrete list of devices that signal "fast-paced."
14. [Documented] Adrenaline story line: built event to event, the central event is always dangerous, and the common element is survival. Violence is common, but for fans the pace means violence "sometimes goes practically unnoticed" unless extended.
15. [Documented] Adrenaline tone usually "veers toward gritty darkness," often melancholy or bleak even with a satisfying resolution, but spans upbeat or comic to grim by subgenre. Tone varies within a genre and must be matched separately.
16. [Documented] Adventure/Thriller/Suspense distinctions (Saricks 2nd ed.: Suspense depends on the reader knowing the danger the protagonist does not) are now "far more technical than actual." LC often assigns all three headings, and NoveList merged Thrillers and Suspense. The shared appeal (gripping, page-turning) outweighs labels.
17. [Documented] Advisor practice: most readers "appreciate the conversation and the process more than they do a quick and standard answer." Advisors should make readers feel safe asking and should suggest as expansively as possible. Readers "take from books what strikes them," even against expert opinion.
18. [Documented] Keeping current: follow genre awards (wins and shortlists show what appeals fans value), reviews (Booklist, Kirkus, LJ, PW, Entertainment Weekly), social media, fan sites, Fiction_L, hold/reserve trends, and genre fans themselves.
19. [Documented] Sections per genre include "Sure Bets" (titles that please both die-hard fans and newcomers) and "Expanding Readers' Horizons: Reading among Genres." Both are useful patterns for cold-start and stretch recommendations.
20. [Documented] The sample's Author/Title Index shows how far the whole-collection approach goes. It has about 41 "video game" entries (e.g., *Mass Effect*, *BioShock*, *Dragon Age*, *The Legend of Zelda*, *Bloodborne*), about 43 "television series" entries, 17 podcast entries, 13 soundtrack entries and well over 100 film entries. These are rough counts from the index text (some include "film series" and "film soundtrack"). No anime entries appear.
21. [Inferred] For a recommender, the genre-appeal groupings give a coarse "experience family" layer (adrenaline/intellect/landscape/emotion) above genre, with per-genre factor weighting.

## Limitations or bias
- Sample only: the per-factor definitions, the RA conversation guidance in each chapter, the actual "Reading the Whole Collection" pairings, and the Subject/Appeal Index were not available. Only Adrenaline's pacing, story line and tone were read.
- Practitioner expertise, not empirical research; no data on whether the groupings predict reader satisfaction.
- US public-library and print-fiction centric; film, TV, games and podcasts appear as add-ons to book genres, not as primary media.
- The authors say the framework is "suggestive," not prescriptive.

## Summary-only items
- The six factor definitions come only from the Introduction's one-line list and its note pointing to Saricks 2005. The media counts come from the index, not from reading the pairings.

## Best used for
- The canonical six-factor appeal list, the four genre-appeal "experience families," and the principle that appeal crosses format (whole-collection advisory).
