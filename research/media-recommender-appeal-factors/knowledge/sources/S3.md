# S3 — Appeal Factors: Enabling Cross-media Advisory Services (Lee, Windleharth & Cho, OCLC/ALISE report)

- Access status: OPENED (full 16-page report) · Date read: 2026-11-14
- Pages read: https://www.oclc.org/content/dam/research/grants/reports/2015/lee2015.pdf
- Published / last updated: July 30, 2016 (final report, 2015 OCLC/ALISE LIS Research Grant)
- Type: funded research grant report (exploratory qualitative study)

## Learned points
1. [Research] Problem statement: RA practice and tools focus on books and audiobooks, and recommendation engines rely on topic and genre metadata, which the authors say limits recommendation quality. Media studied: books, films, video games, graphic novels, music.
2. [Reported] Context cited from a Library Journal/NoveList/RUSA survey of 694 public librarians (Thornton-Verma & Schwartz 2014): RA seen as "mission-critical and an area of growth"; one in five expressed angst providing RA; 23% said their institution provides no RA training; librarians rely on NoveList (61%) or Goodreads (34%), which cover only books and audiobooks.
3. [Research] Core premise: "appeals" mean certain people are attracted to certain types of materials regardless of format. Research questions: RQ1 the most common appeal factors across media; RQ2 how to describe values for those factors so they are efficient to index and helpful to users.
4. [Research] Three-phase design: Phase I domain analysis (literature in LIS, psychology, marketing, media studies; online resources; advisory board of Nancy Pearl, John Vallier, Judi Windleharth); Phase II semi-structured interviews with 30 participants; Phase III (future) large online survey to build controlled vocabularies.
5. [Research] Phase I produced a combined taxonomy of 19 appeals, split into content-based and experiential groups (with alternate names in parentheses):
   - Content-based: Characters (Characterization); Setting (Frame, Background); Language (Style); Narrative (Story, Story line); Pacing ("how the story unfolds"); Genre (Style, Format); Theme (Subject, Topic).
   - Experiential: Accomplishment (Achievement, Completion, Reward structures, Satisfaction); Arousal (Excitation, Stimulation, Adrenaline, Exhilaration); Sensation (Sense-pleasure, Audiovisuality, Graphics, Sound); Fantasy (Imaginative fiction, Make-believe); Mood (Tone, Feel, Emotion); Challenge (Obstacle course); Fellowship (Social framework, Cooperation, Teamwork, Social reward); Discovery (Exploration, Uncharted territory, Curiosity); Expression (Self-discovery, Ego); Cognitive (Thinking, Knowledge, Learning); Submission (Pastime, Recreation, Abnegation); Interactivity (selecting and/or modifying experiences users are exposed to).
6. [Research] Mood definition in the taxonomy: core reason is to achieve a particular emotional state and/or immerse oneself in media that expresses a particular mood (two senses: felt vs. expressed mood).
7. [Research] Selection criteria for which appeals to study as cross-media: transferability across media types, degree of subjectiveness, and ability to measure and describe. After describing sample objects across media, the team chose Mood, Setting, and Complexity (Cognitive).
8. [Research] Phase II participants were 18+ and active users of at least two media types. A pre-interview questionnaire asked which media they consume (books, video games, films/animations/TV shows, graphic novels/manga, music, other), 2–3 liked examples per type, what they liked, and common characteristics across examples. The interview probed mood, setting (our/imaginary/alternate world), complexity, and any other cross-media appeals. Coded in NVivo by four coders.
9. [Research] Five dominant cross-media appeals by frequency: Mood (24), Theme (23), Setting (21), Complexity (20), Character (16).
10. [Research] Mood: most mentioned overall and dominant for music (20 of 24 said mood was especially relevant to music), often tied to the task while listening (studying, writing, exercising, manual labor). Moods sought: calming (18), humorous/light-hearted (17), exciting/intense (14), sad/melancholy (13), serious (12), unsettling (12), dark (8), happy (8).
11. [Research] Mood avoidance: some participants named moods to avoid more easily than moods they seek; the authors say avoidance may matter as much as selection and should be designed into recommenders.
12. [Research] Setting (21 participants) seen as broadly relevant across media with strong narrative (movies, TV, books, games), rarely music. Three-value facet: our world (reality as it is now), imaginary world (usually fantasy), alternate world (mostly real with a twist, such as zombies or vampires). Preferences: imaginary 20, alternate 14, our world 10.
13. [Research] Complexity (20 participants) splits into three facets: intellectual complexity (12; elaborate writing, many characters with complex relationships, complex plots, complicated subject matter), structural complexity (11; non-linear or multi-layered presentation: framing, non-linear timelines, flashbacks, stories within stories, multiple alternate points of view), and time commitment (4; time needed to consume the whole work).
14. [Research] Character (16) plus character development (4): relevant to all narrative media, not music. Participants want specific character types and relatable characters; one avoided any media with no relatable characters; some focus on how characters change, so character and narrative should be considered together. Open question: which character attributes (personality, occupation, gender, appearance) help.
15. [Research] Theme is the best-represented appeal in existing systems (themes, subjects, topics); examples were post-apocalyptic themes in games and comics and historical themes in film and books. A theme can matter "at a given time," acting as context-dependent content appeal.
16. [Research] Conclusion: users could articulate mood, setting, and complexity across media; these could serve as filters in recommenders alongside other access points and bridge users to new media beyond genre and theme.
17. [Research] Recommended future steps: survey to test categories; extract candidate descriptors from survey data; card sorting with facet analysis to build a controlled vocabulary with term definitions and relationships; user evaluation (users' own terms as preferred or lead-in terms, applying the vocabulary, feedback on ease of use and accuracy); prototype HTML5 web app to browse at least four media types by appeal, tested with 20–30 average-to-heavy multimedia consumers.
18. [Research] Observation for future work: specific moods seem sought in certain media more than others, suggesting users seek media for specific purposes or gratifications beyond general preferences.

## Limitations or bias
- Small exploratory sample (30 adults recruited via university lists and researchers' networks); counts are mentions, not prevalence.
- Interviews prompted mood, setting, and complexity directly, which likely inflated their frequency relative to unprompted appeals.
- Phase III and the prototype were plans, not results. Note the report labels the 19-appeal table as "Table 1" in text but "Table 2" in the document.

## Best used for
- A cross-media appeal schema: mood (with avoidance), three-value setting, three-facet complexity, character, theme, plus a 19-appeal taxonomy.
