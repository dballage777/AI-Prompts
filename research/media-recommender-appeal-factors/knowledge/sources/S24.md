# S24 — The Emotional Arcs of Stories Are Dominated by Six Basic Shapes (Reagan, Mitchell, Kiley, Danforth, Dodds)
- URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07772
- Access status: OPENED (abstract page plus full paper PDF, main text read; the 81-page supplementary appendices were not read in detail) · Date read: 2026-11-14
- Pages read: https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07772 ; https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.07772 (v3)
- Published / last updated: submitted 24 Jun 2016; last revised 26 Sep 2016 (v3). The abstract page lists a related DOI, 10.1140/epjds/s13688-016-0093-1.
- Type: academic research paper (computational text analysis; arXiv preprint linked to a journal DOI)

## Learned points
1. [Research] Corpus: 1,327 books from about 50,000 in Project Gutenberg. Filters: English; 20,000–100,000 words; more than 40 downloads; LoC classes PN, PR, PS, PZ; titles with keywords like "poems" or "collection" removed. Results were "qualitatively unchanged" at 10, 20, 40, and 80 download thresholds.
2. [Research] Method: score sentiment in 10,000-word windows slid through the text, using the Hedonometer with the labMT word list. The authors warn that dictionary methods do worse than random on single sentences, which is why they use large windows.
3. [Research] Definition: an emotional arc is the reader-perceived emotional trajectory of a story. It is not the plot. The same falling arc can come from very different plots and structures.
4. [Research] Six core arcs found: "Rags to riches" (rise), "Tragedy" or "Riches to rags" (fall), "Man in a hole" (fall-rise), "Icarus" (rise-fall), "Cinderella" (rise-fall-rise), "Oedipus" (fall-rise-fall).
5. [Research] Triangulation: the six arcs appear across three independent methods: SVD modes, Ward's hierarchical clustering, and a self-organizing map (8x8, 64 nodes). The SOM found seven spatial groups with five arcs.
6. [Research] SVD: the first 12 modes explain 80% (mean-centered) and 94% (raw) of variance. Mode 1 (rags-to-riches versus tragedy) alone captures 30%. Fewer than 10 modes reconstruct an arc to visible accuracy.
7. [Research] Clustering shares of the corpus: "Man in a hole" 30%, "Tragedy" 32%, "Rags to riches" 5%, "Oedipus" 31%.
8. [Research] Null-model check: "word salad" (shuffled words) and 2-gram Markov "nonsense" versions gave a flatter spectrum (45% of real-story variance), less distinct clusters (linkage cost 1400 versus 7000), and incoherent SOM nodes. So the arcs are not an artifact of the method.
9. [Research] Popularity by arc, measured by downloads: the most common arcs are not the most downloaded. "Icarus," "Oedipus," and two back-to-back "Man in a hole" arcs (SV 4) had the highest median downloads. The authors say individual high-download books influence this.
10. [Research] Example: Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows has a visible arc for each sub-narrative, but the method misses brief emotional moments such as a single kiss. It captures broad shape, not local beats.
11. [Research] Examples of closest stories: The Winter's Tale (rags to riches), Romeo and Juliet (8th most tragic), The Magic of Oz (man in a hole).
12. [Research] Stated caveat: downloads are "only a rough proxy for success." Sales or other measures would be needed.
13. [Research] Lineage: Kurt Vonnegut's rejected master's thesis drew story shapes on "Beginning–End" and "Ill Fortune–Great Fortune" axes. The paper tests that idea at scale.
14. [Research] Proposed extensions include character networks, place, and analogues of Munroe's Movie Narrative Charts. The authors note stories now run "from books to television series to news," but they analyze only written text.
15. [Inferred] For a cross-media recommender, the arc shape is a candidate content-side mood or tone trajectory feature, separate from plot or genre. Computing it for films or TV would need a different signal, such as subtitles or scripts; the paper does not test that.

## Limitations or bias
- Older, public-domain, English-language Gutenberg texts of 20k–100k words; the authors call the filter rough. The corpus includes some non-fiction (for example, a "Book of Wise Sayings" cluster).
- Uses a dictionary sentiment score (happy/sad valence only). It does not capture other tone dimensions such as tension, humor, or dread.
- Popularity uses Gutenberg downloads only, and outlier books drive it.
- Text only; no film, TV, anime, or game data.
- The supplementary appendices (plot theories, cluster details) were skimmed, not studied.

## Best used for
- Evidence that stories cluster into a few emotional trajectory shapes that can be computed and kept separate from plot: a possible "emotional arc" facet beside tone and mood.
