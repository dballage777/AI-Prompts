# S11 — "I'm looking for something like ...": Combining Narratives and Example Items for Narrative-driven Book Recommendation (Bogers & Koolen, KaRS 2018)
- URL: https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-2290/kars2018_paper7.pdf
- Access status: OPENED (full text; some figures and tables are garbled by PDF extraction) · Date read: 2026-11-14
- Pages read: https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-2290/kars2018_paper7.pdf
- Published / last updated: KaRS Workshop 2018 (co-located with RecSys 2018), 7 Oct 2018, Vancouver; CEUR-WS Vol-2290
- Type: peer-reviewed workshop paper (algorithm experiments)

## Learned points
1. [Research] Definition: narrative-driven recommendation (NDR, Bogers & Koolen 2017) combines (1) a narrative description of the desired aspects of items and (2) user preference information, either a transaction log or a user-provided "mini-profile" of positive and/or negative example items.
2. [Research] The mini-profile is a temporary representation of the user's current preferences, made up of the example books and authors in the request. Its items are examples that illustrate the need, not candidates to recommend.
3. [Research] Prevalence: the earlier work analyzed 974 complex LibraryThing (LT) book requests. The authors estimate over 25,000 such requests exist in the LT forums alone, and an order of magnitude more across the web for many domains. Kang et al. (2017) coded 498 natural-language chatbot interactions about movies and found a mix of objective, subjective, and navigational aspects.
4. [Research] NDR sits between recommendation and information retrieval (IR). The authors describe these as needs with "a strong IR element".
5. [Research] Example request (Figure 1): "manly books about manly issues, that aren't too gritty, but make you think as much as you laugh", with seeds About a Boy, High Fidelity, and Train Man. It shows how tone or intensity limits ("not too gritty") and an emotional-effect balance are packed into one request.
6. [Research] Setup:
   - 331 requests (from 2006–2012) matched to crawled LT profiles.
   - 298 have at least one example book (mean 2.6, median 2); 121 have at least one example author (mean 2.0).
   - Collection: 2.65 million book records (1.9 million works), from the Amazon/LibraryThing dataset with metadata, curated subject terms, LT tags, and Amazon reviews.
   - Relevance: thread suggestions score 1; suggestions the requester later cataloged score 8.
   - Main metric: nDCG@10. 166 train and 165 test requests.
7. [Research] Baseline: collaborative filtering (CF) on the whole profile (LightFM, WARP loss) does badly (nDCG@10 0.017). It recommends what similar users like, "but not necessarily within the confines of the information need."
8. [Research] Narrative as query (content-based filtering, Indri): user-generated content beats curated metadata. Scores by representation (nDCG@10 / MRR):

   | Representation | nDCG@10 | MRR |
   |---|---|---|
   | Reviews + tags | 0.074 | 0.181 |
   | All fields | 0.074 | 0.180 |
   | Reviews | 0.067 | 0.164 |
   | Tags | 0.048 | 0.111 |
   | Curated metadata | 0.026 | 0.052 |
   | Core metadata | 0.024 | 0.059 |

   The authors suggest reviews "reflect the language of requesters better" and use a broader vocabulary than tags.
9. [Research] Popularity: CF recommends from the popular end. Content-based filtering on curated metadata favors the long tail. User-generated content strikes a balance: popular books have more reviews and tags, but topic drift in large review sets limits how much this favors them.
10. [Research] Example-driven algorithms:
    - EDR-1 (similar books): best was all fields, nDCG@10 0.046.
    - EDR-2 (similar authors): far weaker (about 0.006 over all requests). Similar-author recommendations "cause topic drift", possibly because prolific authors cover many topics.
    - Example books give more focus than example authors.
11. [Research] Hybrid: narrative plus EDR-1 (weights .9/.1) significantly improved nDCG@10 to 0.088 vs 0.074. Requests with non-zero scores rose from 55 to 68. Narratives and examples complement each other, but the narrative is the stronger signal.
12. [Research] User-preference (CF item-item) signals help early precision, but "beyond that the signal gets so weak that popular items pollute the ranking."
13. [Research] No correlation was found between narrative length or number of examples and NDR performance.
14. [Research] Known gap: all examples were treated as positive. The authors expect this hurts performance and call for sentiment detection to separate "like this" from "not like this" examples.
15. [Research] Absolute accuracy is low across the board (best nDCG@10 under 0.09). The authors frame NDR as a hard problem that needs multiple data sources and algorithms. They call for testing on movies, games, and music.
16. [Inferred] For an LLM-era recommender: describe candidate titles in reader or viewer vocabulary (review-like appeal language) rather than catalog subject terms. Weight the narrative over the seeds. Treat negative seeds explicitly. Avoid expanding by creator alone.

## Limitations or bias
- Small test set (165 requests) and a single train/test split, which the authors acknowledge. Books only, and the LT data are from 2006–2013.
- The relevance judgments come from forum suggestions and later cataloging, which is an imperfect proxy for satisfaction.
- Pre-LLM methods (Indri language models, LightFM). The absolute numbers are not comparable with S13's movie F1 scores.
- PDF extraction scrambled the figures and parts of the tables. Table values were read from the text where possible.

## Best used for
- The formal definition of narrative-driven recommendation, plus evidence that reader-language descriptions (reviews and tags) and narrative-plus-example hybrids beat catalog metadata and plain CF.
