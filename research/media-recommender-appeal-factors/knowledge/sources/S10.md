## Learned points
1. [Documented] The RA conversation has four parts (interview, determination of appeal factors, suggestions, and follow-up), both immediately and long term. The toolkit's sequence is Approach, then Conversation/Interview, then Actions to offer suggestions, then Closing and follow-up.
2. [Documented] Contrast with reference: a reference interview usually ends in one answer; RA "requires not one answer but a range of suggestions." Readers often don't know what they want or how to say what they like.
3. [Documented] "Good" is relative (a Grisham novel for one person, Dickens for another), so the advisor must ask what the person has liked before.
4. [Documented] Recommended open-ended prompts: "Tell me about a book (or film or audio recording) you have enjoyed," "Tell me what you enjoyed about this book," "Tell me about any favourite authors you have," "Tell me why you like this book or author," and "Can you tell me more about. . .?" Avoid closed questions like "Do you like mysteries?"
5. [Documented] Readers rarely use appeal terms. They say things like "I could not put it down" or "I love books set in New York," and the advisor's job is to translate these into appeal terms that can be applied to other suggestions. This is the core translation step for a recommender's intake.
6. [Documented] History: in the 1990s Saricks, Pearl, and others noticed readers cared more about a book's "feel" than its subject or genre. The toolkit says appeal "can be applied to the entire collection, regardless of format."
7. [Documented] Appeal vs. subject headings: subject = topic; appeal = what draws readers (e.g., pacing, frame). "Most novels are driven by one or two appeals," which you find by asking what you'd emphasize when describing the book.
8. [Documented] The toolkit compares frameworks. Pearl's four "doorways" are story, character, setting, and language, plus a secondary doorway per book. Saricks's are pacing, characterization, storyline, and frame (setting, atmosphere, background, tone). NoveList uses storyline, pace, tone, and writing style. Whichbook offers sliders between paired opposites.
9. [Documented] Some elements limit appeal: "violence, sex, profane language, or style of writing." In other words, negative or exclusion factors.
10. [Documented] Sample vocabulary (Saricks): pacing (breakneck, leisurely paced, measured, relaxed); characterization (detailed, lifelike, quirky, recognizable, series, vivid, well drawn); story line (action oriented, complex, episodic, multiple plotlines, plot twists); frame and tone (detailed setting, bittersweet, exotic, heartwarming, small town, suspenseful, upbeat); style (conversational, graceful, flamboyant, polished, sophisticated, unpretentious, poetic).
11. [Documented] Sample NoveList searchable terms: storyline (action-packed, character-driven, intricately plotted, issue-oriented, plot-driven, world-building); pace (fast-paced, relaxed pace); tone (funny, gruesome, haunting, menacing, nostalgic, reflective, steamy, violent); writing style (accessible, descriptive, experimental, lyrical, richly detailed, thoughtful, witty).
12. [Documented] Listed advantages of appeal: it, more than subject, usually decides like or dislike; it is reader-centric; it lets advisors talk about books they dislike; it helps cross genres; and it frees advisors from the limits of their own reading.
13. [Documented] Present suggestions briefly in appeal language, and if appropriate mix fiction and non-fiction and formats. Model blurb (from Wyatt): Garden Spells is "comforting and enchanting… set in North Carolina… language is lovely and it moves along nicely… a great feel-good book."
14. [Documented] Suggestion etiquette: don't overwhelm with choices; make rejection free and private; follow up and ask what worked or didn't, using appeal-focused questions to learn from misses.
15. [Documented] Wyatt's non-fiction model lists appeal (pacing, characterization, storyline, detail, learning/experiencing, language, setting, tone), narrative context, type (memoir, biography, etc., which works like subgenre), and subject. For pleasure reading, subject is "not always the main consideration."
16. [Documented] Graphic novels and manga: pacing depends on art style, number of captions, amount of dialogue, and panel layout. Visual setting establishes tone and mood quickly. Format-specific appeal factors include art medium, colour vs. black and white, lettering, visual style (cartoonish, realistic, clear line), layout, image/text balance, and fanbase (licensed character). The toolkit warns that a fan of one graphic novel type may not enjoy others, notes that popular manga often become anime or live action, and cautions about mature images.
17. [Documented] Format changes appeal. With e-books, there's no sense of pages remaining (pacing) and page layout cues to tone are lost. Audiobook appeal factors: production value and narrator traits (gender, accent, author vs. actor narrator). Strong language or graphic content can feel worse spoken aloud. Long descriptive passages may be risky for drivers. Group or family listening changes content suitability.
18. [Documented] Ontario context: up to 10% of Canadians have difficulty reading print due to disability (PALS survey, Statistics Canada).
19. [Documented] Personalized reading-list form (an intake template): fiction or non-fiction; favourite authors (to find style and to exclude known authors); three liked titles and why ("you may list movies or TV shows instead"); disliked titles and why; most important focus (characters, issues, actions, relationships); genres; places or eras; lead-character gender; length; pacing; coarse language, sex, and violence (avoid, include, no preference); format. Use everyday language, not library terms.
20. [Documented] List norms: 5–8 titles per list (Seattle PL's "Your Next 5 Books," 2011); each with a short description and why it was chosen, citing the person's appeal factors; a no-guarantee disclaimer; turnaround of about 1 to 1.5 weeks. The form helps surface backlist titles and suits people who express taste better in writing.
21. [Documented] Practice tips: log your own reading with appeal notations (e.g., Goodreads shelves); hold staff book-sharing sessions; use NoveList for genres you don't read; stay neutral and non-judgemental ("Never apologize for your reading tastes," attributed to Betty Rosenberg).

## Limitations or bias
- Training guidance, not research; no evaluation data. Book-centric: film and TV appear only as interview prompts and substitutes on the intake form.
- Tool and site lists are dated (Shelfari, e-reader models, 2012-era social media). The Whichbook paired-opposites list was an image and was not captured.
- Framework comparisons are summaries of Pearl, Saricks, NoveList, and Wyatt, not the primary texts.

## Summary-only items
- None (full text read). The vocabularies are described as "representative examples," not complete lists.

## Best used for
- A ready-made intake script and questionnaire (open prompts, likes and dislikes with reasons, limiting factors) plus side-by-side appeal frameworks and the graphic novel/manga appeal facets.
