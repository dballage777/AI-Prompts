## Learned points
1. [Research] Method: large-scale survey (28 questions) distributed via the Seattle Interactive Media Museum game exhibition, game forums, university mailing lists, and researchers' social media; 2,163 respondents participated and 1,257 completed. Appeals came from an open-ended question (Q4: favorite games and why), coded by two independent coders with a consensus model and an iteratively revised codebook seeded from prior literature; the first author arbitrated disagreements.
2. [Research] Working definition of appeals used for cross-media work: "elements of media that draw people in by evoking certain emotional and intellectual experiences when a user interacts with the media."
3. [Research] The sixteen appeals, with definition ("core reason for playing a game is to...") and coded frequency (Table 1):
   - Narrative (628): experience and appreciate the story.
   - Challenge (292): feel the joy of overcoming obstacles or challenges.
   - Sensation (287): appreciate visual and/or auditory content / enjoy physical stimulation.
   - Fellowship (215): enjoy the company of others.
   - Nostalgia (183): revisit or rethink a past experience.
   - Fantasy (176): immerse oneself in a fantasy world and do things not possible in real life.
   - Exploration (173): explore and/or discover something new.
   - Mood (162): experience and/or appreciate a particular emotion.
   - Depth (136): appreciate the scale and choices/consequences.
   - Creativity/Innovation (102): appreciate innovative, novel, or experimental aspects.
   - Expression (101): express oneself and/or create things in the game world.
   - Accomplishment (66): satisfaction of achieving, acquiring, accomplishing, often via rewards/status, and/or completing all options.
   - Competition (62): satisfaction of competing and/or winning against others.
   - Submission (50): pass time and/or "turn off one's brain" and unwind/relax.
   - Mastery (21): satisfaction from perfecting one's skills.
   - Learning (14): learn something new.
4. [Research] User terms per appeal (Table 4, "Relevant Terms and Phrases"): Narrative = good and compelling story, well-developed characters, detailed universe and lore. Challenge = difficult, requires skill development, steep learning curve. Sensation = beautiful, great aesthetics, art games, body movement or rhythm, good music. Fellowship = great to play with friends or family, co-op modes, working together, teamwork, team play, bonding through playing. Nostalgia = nostalgic, remastered, reminds you of childhood, old school, "retro." Fantasy = escaping to another world, doing what you cannot in real life, supernatural, mythical, magical. Exploration = rich and expansive world, open-world, sandbox, nonlinear plot, free to explore anywhere. Depth = deep and complex plot or mechanics, game actions matter, many choices. Creativity/Innovation = innovative, creative, unique, unusual gameplay, new kind of game, games that define a genre. Expression = build or create anything, share your creation, express yourself. Accomplishment = significant time investment, many badges/trophies/achievements, completionism, set collecting. Competition = competing against each other, e-sport, testing skills. Submission = turn your brain off, unwind, relax, forget about other things, simple mechanics. Mastery = perfect your skills, memorize the moves, perfect score, easy to learn but hard to master. Learning = learn about a topic, teachable moment, learning languages, educational games, significant tangential learning.
5. [Research] Table 4 example titles per appeal: Narrative (Final Fantasy VI, Persona 5); Challenge (Ninja Gaiden, Dark Souls); Sensation (Dance Dance Revolution A, Flower); Fellowship (Mario Kart 8, Overwatch); Nostalgia (The Legend of Zelda, Cave Story); Fantasy (Oblivion, Dragon Age: Origins); Exploration (GTA V, Etrian Odyssey II); Depth (Homeworld 2, Ogre Battle 64); Creativity/Innovation (Portal 2, The Stanley Parable); Expression (Minecraft, Super Mario Maker); Accomplishment (World of Warcraft, Pokémon GO); Competition (Starcraft, League of Legends); Submission (Abzû, Pokémon: Magikarp Jump); Mastery (Street Fighter IV, Guitar Hero III); Learning (The Oregon Trail, Civilization IV).
6. [Research] Narrative was the top appeal and had longer-lasting appeal over time; respondents wanted story integrated with gameplay and relatable characters, and some said story outweighed bugs or functional faults. Authors suggest metadata on the centrality of narrative, character types, and character tropes (to seek or avoid).
7. [Research] Challenge has physical (speed, precision) and intellectual/moral facets; ideal difficulty varies by person (hardcore vs. easy pick-up; "just right" difficulty; gentle learning curves; adjustable difficulty). Recommend describing kind of challenge and whether multiple difficulty levels exist.
8. [Research] Sensation was coded as visual (192 mentions), auditory (82), physical (13). Visual descriptors were subjective ("pretty," "beautiful," "aesthetic," "artistic," "interesting"); music builds atmosphere and recalls emotion; senses work together for immersion. Suggested metadata: visual style, user interaction types, soundtracks.
9. [Research] Mood: most frequent moods were humor and silliness, intense excitement, and horror and darkness; peacefulness, sadness, and anxiety appeared rarely. Some valued games that made them feel miserable (Spec Ops) or that helped through hard times. Authors cite Ross and Chelton treating mood as the "bedrock" of fiction choice.
10. [Research] Nostalgia is very personal and hard to use as an access point; better to find games sharing characteristics with what a player is nostalgic for, or games that evoke target moods.
11. [Research] Fantasy keywords were "immersive" and "escapism"; RPGs were the common vehicle. Escapism links to media and reading research (escaping life pressures).
12. [Research] Exploration and Depth are two facets of discovery. Exploration links to setting/"world" but is an active, player-controlled affordance (unlike setting as backdrop in books/film/audiobooks). Depth reflects agency, choice, consequences, non-linearity, openness, and variety of mechanics/systems; suggested metadata: whether a game offers multiple versions of story/events.
13. [Research] Expression is about demonstrating personality and creativity (customization, open world/sandbox) rather than self-understanding; Creativity/Innovation covers risk-taking or genre-defining mechanics, visual style, or narrative.
14. [Research] Accomplishment (achievement for its own sake, often instant gratification), Learning (experiential and content-based, unpredictable per player), and Mastery (slow, deliberate skill perfection via practice) are distinct goal-based appeals. Competition differs from accomplishment by comparing and displaying prowess to others (including self/high scores) and often co-exists with fellowship; some players actively avoid competition or co-play.
15. [Research] Submission was refined beyond "passive pastime" to include purposeful disengagement; "mindless" is not intrinsic to the game: complex games (RPGs, shooters) can serve it via familiarity or absorbing focus, so it is hard to recommend on.
16. [Research] Appeal-by-genre analysis (Q6–Q7, respondents picked favorite of 11 genres and checked reasons from a list of simple sentences; Table 2 uses partly different labels such as Aesthetics, Physical Interaction, Peer pressure). Top appeals: Action = Challenge; Action/Adventure = Exploration, Narrative, Fantasy; RPG (n=529) = Narrative (89.0%), Exploration (79.8%), Fantasy (75.2%); Puzzle = Submission, Challenge, Mastery; Simulation = Submission, Expression, Challenge/Exploration; Fighting = Challenge/Competition/Mastery tie; Strategy = Challenge, Mastery, Narrative; Sports and Driving/Racing = Fellowship first; Music/Dance = Physical interaction.
17. [Research] Key finding: appeals reveal similarities across genres not visible from theme or mechanics (e.g., puzzle and simulation share submission; strategy and action share challenge), so appeals are a complementary access point enabling more diversified recommendations across genres.
18. [Research] Proposed implementations: crowdsource appeal indexing; display appeal proportions per game (example: 60% narrative, 20% exploration, 10% fantasy, 10% fellowship); add supporting metadata (character types for narrative, visual style for sensation, customization options for expression).
19. [Research] Some appeals cluster and might merge under broader concepts (accomplishment/learning/mastery; expression/creativity-innovation). Content-related appeals (narrative, depth) vs. experience-related (fantasy, mood).
20. [Research] Background frameworks cited: Saricks's appeal elements (pacing, characterization, story line, tone and mood, style and language, frame and setting); Pearl's four doorways (story, character, setting, language); Wyatt's eight nonfiction appeals (pacing, characterization, story line, detail, learning and experiencing, language, setting, tone); Ross's five-element book-choice model; MDA's eight aesthetics (sensation, fantasy, narrative, challenge, fellowship, discovery, expression, submission).
21. [Research] Stated future work: study mood and narrative across media (are game moods like music or film moods?) toward cross-media appeals and "whole-collection RA."

## Limitations or bias
- Convenience sample recruited via game venues, forums, university lists, and researchers' networks; demographics not reported in this article (referred to an earlier paper).
- Genre analysis uses a different, pre-set list of appeals (Table 2 labels differ from the sixteen); small cells (e.g., Driving/Racing n=12, Fighting n=12) make some percentages unstable.
- Frequencies count mentions in free text; not weighted by importance. Video games only; cross-media claims are future work.
