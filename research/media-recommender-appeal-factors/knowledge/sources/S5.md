## Learned points
1. [Research] Method: the authors collected 1,597 anime genre terms from 36 English-language anime databases and websites. They also looked at 3 Japanese sources (Kinokuniya, Tsutaya, d Anime store) for reference but left them out of the analysis. They then card-sorted the terms inductively, bottom-up. Individual sources ranged from 7 to 283 genre terms.
2. [Research] Data cleaning ran in five stages: (1) merge duplicates; (2) merge variant forms (spacing, hyphens, romanization, typos); (3) merge translations and romanizations (for example, magical girl = mahou shoujo); (4) merge similar terms that differ in specificity under a broader preferred term (for example, afterlife, underworld, heaven, hell, and makai become "afterlife"); (5) standardize. This left 643 terms: 538 single or compound terms and 105 sub-genre terms. Foci were the preferred terms that appeared more than once.
3. [Research] Result: 9 facets, 13 sub-facets, and 153 foci. Each focus was assigned to exactly one facet, which tested mutual exclusivity. Compound and sub-genre terms were then used to test exhaustivity. Table 2 gives these foci counts per facet: Audience 6, Setting 15, Mood 7, Character 32, Plot/Narrative 21, Subjects 47 (the body text says 33), Association with Other Works 5, Feature 7, Production 13.
4. [Research] The **Audience** facet is the target demographic, and its terms usually come from creators, publishers, or distributors. Its foci are Kodomo (children, up to late elementary or junior high age), Family (all ages), Shōnen (teenage boys), Shōjo (teenage girls), Seinen (young men), and Josei (women). The intended demographic shapes content tropes: shōnen tends toward fighting and male protagonists, shōjo toward romantic subplots and female protagonists. The actual audience can differ from the intended one.
5. [Research] People use "Seinen" inconsistently. Some mean mature audiences, some mean complex plots or serious issues, and some mean violent or sexual content. As a result, databases return conflicting results for the same term. Crunchyroll, Anime-Planet, and MyAnimeList disagree on whether Mushi-shi and Haven't You Heard? I'm Sakamoto are seinen. The line between Kodomo and Family is also subjective.
6. [Research] The **Setting** facet is the environment where the anime takes place, split into three sub-facets. Setting-Time covers Historical, Steampunk, and Cyberpunk. Setting-Place covers Isekai (parallel universe), Western, Space Opera, School, Restaurants/Stores, Afterlife, and Space. Setting-Situational, "an abnormal state" of place or time that gives the aesthetic or circumstances, covers Fantasy, Apocalypse, Science Fiction, Science Fantasy, and Dystopia.
7. [Research] Several setting terms span more than one sub-facet. Cyberpunk implies both a future time and a technological or social situation. A Western implies place, time, and conventions. Cowboy Bebop borrows from the Western without its time or place. The research team itself disagreed about what "fantasy" and "science fiction" mean. "Historical" is too broad to be useful, but narrower terms like "medieval" smuggle in a place (Europe). Open question: should only the most prevalent setting be tagged, or every applicable one?
8. [Research] The **Mood** facet is defined as "the emotional state of the audience in relation to the feelings an anime elicits." This is the audience-feeling sense of mood, not the work's atmosphere. Its foci are Tragedy, Thriller, Comedy, Psychological, Horror, Suspense, and Iyashikei (healing). Most mood genre terms are nouns, not adjectives.
9. [Research] Mood definitions: "Psychological" emphasizes the mind and characters' motivations or mental states, and it raises thought-provoking, agitated, unsettling, or disturbing feelings. "Iyashikei" means the audience feels healed and soothed. These shows are often calm, slow-paced, and focused on small daily delights, which overlaps with "slice of life." One title can carry several moods. The authors note that a viewer may want a tragic-mood work to ease their own sadness, so the two senses of mood interact.
10. [Research] The **Character** facet covers human, non-human, and imaginary figures: occupations (detectives, police, students, idols, pirates, maids, samurai, ninja), traits (bishōjo, bishōnen, gender bender, delinquents), and creatures (robots/mecha, monsters, vampires, demons, aliens, gods). It also includes protagonist type (Hero, Heroine). Similar concepts are merged, for example "gangs" for mafia and yakuza and "monsters" for yōkai. Systems can split or merge foci to suit local needs.
11. [Research] The **Plot/Narrative** facet is "how the story unfolds." It is kept separate from Subjects, which are themes or topics. Foci include Slice of Life, Coming-of-Age, Drama, Adventure, Mystery, Conspiracy, Absurd, Abstract, Mind-Twisting, Survival, Biography, Sekai-kei, Gekiga (plot), and Romance. Romance has sub-facets for types (Bara, Shōnen Ai/Yaoi, Shōjo Ai/Yuri, Age Gap) and for multiple people (Love Triangle, Harem/Reverse Harem).
12. [Research] Two hard cases. Sekai-kei ("world-type") has no agreed definition and no good translation. It usually links the relationship between two main characters to a world crisis, with the mediating social setting left out (for example, Neon Genesis Evangelion), so the authors kept the Japanese term untranslated. Gekiga means both a serious, mature, realistic, darker story and a dramatic, realistic visual style, so the authors split it into "Gekiga (plot)" and "Gekiga (style)" under different facets.
13. [Research] The **Subjects** facet covers the themes or topics an anime mainly conveys: game, cooking, time travel, magic, VR, tournaments, politics, work life, crime, music, arts, war, military, superpowers, supernatural, mythology, bullying, gambling, sexual content, violent content, and more. "Sports" has its own sub-facet with 14 or more child terms (baseball, boxing, archery, ice skating, and others), which shows how popular sports are in anime.
14. [Research] The **Association with Other Works** facet records referential links to other works: Parody, Novel, Comics, Manga, Documentary. Much of it reflects manga as source material, and it captures connections across media.
15. [Research] The **Feature** facet covers clichés or recurring elements that can be the whole work or just inserts: Action Scenes (sub-types Proxy Battles, Swordplay, Gunfights), Fan Service (sexualization "largely for the audience rather than the show"), Power Suits, and Henshin (transformation scenes).
16. [Research] The **Production** facet covers how the anime was made. Techniques: CG Animation. Styles: Chibi (super deformed), Gekiga (style). Regions: Foreign Anime, Cartoon. Agents: Voice Actors. Formats: ONA, Special, OVA, Short, Movies, Series. Languages: Dubbed. This facet carries the visual and audio style and the length or format information.
17. [Research] Worked examples (Table 3). One Punch Man: Seinen; Science Fiction; Comedy; Hero/Cyborgs/Monsters/Aliens; Coming-of-Age/Adventure; Superpowers/Violent Contents; Manga; Action Scenes; Series/OVA. Ristorante Paradiso: Josei; Restaurants/Stores; Iyashikei; Romance-Age Gap/Slice of Life/Drama; Cooking/Work Life; Fan Service. Attack on Titan: Seinen; Apocalypse; Tragedy/Thriller/Horror/Suspense; Gekiga (plot)/Survival; War/Military; Swordplay; Gekiga (style). Where a facet is ambiguous, it is marked N/A.
18. [Research] Why facets beat a hierarchy: a single title can hold several genres. A "romantic comedy" becomes Mood = Comedy plus Plot = Romance. Facets are easier to extend, since new terms slot into existing facets. They support both breadth (many aspects) and specificity (many labels per aspect). Of the 5 commercial sites in Table 1, only IMDb and Crunchyroll allowed Boolean AND across genres. IMDb had the most genre terms (27) and Amazon the fewest (6).
19. [Research] On vocabulary, English-speaking fans normally use Japanese genre terms. The schema pairs the romanized Japanese term with an English gloss, for example "kodomo (children)" and "iyashikei (healing)". This respects user and literary warrant while staying intuitive for newcomers.
20. [Research] Background evidence cited: in the authors' earlier study of anime recommendation requests (Cho et al. 2017), genre ranked third of 19 information features. Lee, Shim, and Jett (2015) found genre was the second most mentioned feature among Korean anime users seeking recommendations. Users disagreed over whether a label was a genre, theme, or trope, and they expected different elements even from the same genre term.
21. [Research] Cited claims about anime's appeal: Napton (via Davis 2016) says plot and narrative style are anime's main appeals, with an audience that includes adult women. Levi (2013) names sadness, environmentalism, and terror as themes unique to anime. Anime is "a medium, not a genre" (Richmond 2009), and it spans all the genres found in cinema (Poitras 2002).
22. [Inferred] For a cross-media recommender, the nine facets map onto readers'-advisory appeal factors. Mood corresponds to tone or mood, Setting to setting/frame, Character to characterization, Plot/Narrative to story, and Production-Styles to style. Audience, Feature, and Association with Other Works add axes that general RA lists lack.

## Limitations or bias
- The terms come from English-language websites gathered in 2017. Japanese sources were only observed, not analyzed. The authors say the term list is not exhaustive and that genre terms keep changing.
- Facets were built from what systems label as "genre," not from viewer interviews. The mood facet is a short list of 7 foci.
- The counts conflict internally: the Subjects facet has 47 foci in Table 2 but "thirty-three" in the body text. Some setting terms break mutual exclusivity, as the authors acknowledge.
- The foci include some problematic fandom terms (for example, rorikon and shotakon). A recommender should handle these as content flags, not as appeal descriptors.

## Best used for
- A ready-made, defined facet schema (audience, setting, mood, character, plot, subjects, associations, features, production) for tagging anime, adaptable to other screen media.
