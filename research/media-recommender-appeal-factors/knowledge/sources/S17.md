## Learned points
1. [Documented] The authors call manga and anime formats, not genres. In Japan, "manga" means any comic and "anime" any animation. As English loanwords, they mean Japanese works or works in a Japanese-influenced style. Manga "can encompass many genres."
2. [Documented] What gets licensed in the West is skewed. The most-licensed category is shōnen battle manga, mainly from Weekly Shōnen Jump. The authors describe it as action-packed but with storylines about honor, friendship, family, and personal growth, "highly formulaic" yet broadly appealing, especially to adolescents. Other genres were long overlooked because publishers thought they would sell less, though this has recently widened.
3. [Documented] Manga magazines are organized by demographic: shōjo (adolescent girls), shōnen (adolescent boys), josei (adult women), seinen (adult men), and kodomo or kodomomuke (children). These are audience labels, not genre labels.
4. [Documented] Manga and anime are tightly linked across media. There is a long history of manga adapted into anime and some adaptation in the reverse direction, which the authors call crucial to the format's popularity. A hit can become a "media juggernaut." The example is One Piece, which spawned anime, video games, cookbooks, films, light novels, a trading card game, a Kabuki play, theme-park attractions, restaurants, and a Netflix TV show.
5. [Documented] The authors present right-to-left, unflipped printing as part of the format's appeal. Experiencing manga "as it was created" gives a view into another culture. TOKYOPOP started unflipped printing and untranslated sound effects in 2002, and it became the industry standard.
6. [Documented] Series length and continuity matter. Manga usually has to be read in order. A complete work can run from one volume to hundreds (Golgo 13 has run since 1968 and is over 200 volumes). Reading all 103 English volumes of One Piece is about 18,540 pages. Manga readers "read deeply, at high volume, and tend to reread favorite volumes and scenes."
7. [Documented] Most Japanese manga purchases are now digital, while magazines remain the main serialization channel. (The authors cite Anime News Network for a 677 billion yen market in 2022.)
8. [Documented] Chapter 2 argues an advisor does not need to be a fan. The author's expertise grew from listening to readers. Readers "don't care if I know everything about their favorite manga; they love to tell me all about it anyway." Showing interest in what readers love is presented as the core of the relationship.
9. [Documented] Practical ways to build advisory knowledge without being an expert: a student advisory group or advisory board of student experts; passive input (a suggestion clipboard or an online survey via QR code); noticing fandom signals such as merch and asking about them; comic conventions, which often have education tracks and educator passes; and publisher newsletters and guides that list series and age ratings. The authors warn to always check age ratings on learner suggestions.
10. [Reported] A case example: a single badly worn copy of Dragon Ball vol. 4 with over 60 checkouts led to a manga collection that grew to more than 600 volumes in five years. The author says it circulated more than any other items in the library. Readers came "in packs" and argued about favorite characters, battles, or love stories, which hints at character, action, and romance as discussion hooks.
11. [Documented] The index shows what the full book covers, though these pages were not in the sample: readers' advisory (pp. 59–66); "age-fit vs. age-appropriate" (39–40); manga bibliotherapy (65); "anime bumps" (51, 60–61); research tips using Anime News Network, Anime-Planet, and streaming services; and genre entries including iyashikei/healing, isekai, magical girl, mecha/kaiju/tokusatsu, gag/comedy, edu-manga, cooking, historical, horror and thrillers, slice-of-life, sports, Boys' Love, Girls' Love/yuri, and "cinnamon roll characters."

## Limitations or bias
- The sample leaves out the readers'-advisory chapter and the genre chapter, which are the parts most relevant to this topic. The appeal and advisory content here is indirect.
- It is aimed at K–12 school librarians, so the focus is youth, curriculum standards (AASL), and age-appropriateness, not adult viewers.
- It is practitioner advocacy: manga enthusiasts promoting manga collections. The evidence is anecdotal.

## Summary-only items
- Point 11 and point 12 come only from the index and table of contents. The chapters themselves were not read.

## Best used for
- Treating manga and anime as formats, not genres, with demographic labels, cross-media adaptation, and series continuity as recommendation considerations. Also a reader-led, non-expert advisory stance.
