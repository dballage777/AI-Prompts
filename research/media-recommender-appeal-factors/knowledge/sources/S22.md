## Learned points
1. [Documented] The goal is to advise people whose tastes differ from your own, without having read the book, by using NoveList's story elements: genre, theme and appeal.
2. [Documented] Genre is a shortcut that signals a reader expectation, though there is "a lot of variety within each genre."
3. [Documented] Appeal is defined as the "feel" of a book and a way to see why people enjoy what they read (e.g., dark, heartwarming, laugh-out-loud funny).
4. [Documented] Themes (then the newest NoveList element) are popular recurring plot elements described in "language that a reading community uses."
5. [Documented] Worked example, *The Death of Mrs. Westaway*. Genres: gothic fiction plus psychological suspense, which together suggest heroines in creepy, lonely places with twists. Theme: "Too good to be true." Character: flawed and sympathetic (imperfect but you root for them). Storyline: nonlinear (jumps in time). Writing style: compelling and descriptive. Tone: atmospheric, menacing, suspenseful.
6. [Documented] Method: look up the record and read each element in turn (genre → theme → review gist → character → storyline → style → tone), then turn the terms into a plain-language pitch about what the experience will feel like.
7. [Documented] Training drill: at team meetings, someone who has not read a named title looks it up and reports back "as if they were talking to a patron." It also supports form-based RA and catalog-side conversations (NoveList Select).
8. [Documented] NoveList maintains help-page definitions for appeals, genres and themes; the terms are meant to be self-explanatory.
9. [Inferred] Translating terms into a pitch is the step a recommender's explanation layer should copy: show the matched appeal terms and say what each means for the experience.
10. [Inferred] The example uses the pre-2024 vocabulary ("compelling," "tone," "writing style"); "Compelling" was later deleted (see S9).
