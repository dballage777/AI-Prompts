## Learned points
1. [Reported] Origin: in 1982–83 the Downers Grove (IL) Public Library ran out of shelf space and created a fiction and AV department, run by Saricks and Brown, which opened in July 1983. Appeal came from their trouble training staff to suggest books.
2. [Reported] What went wrong before appeal: matching on surface genre or subject. Ruth Rendell was offered to Mary Higgins Clark fans because both had female detectives, and le Carré to Ludlum fans because both wrote spy thrillers. These pairs fail because their pacing and atmosphere do not overlap.
3. [Reported] First tool: a "Popular Fiction List" of twelve authors in twelve genres. It helped orient staff, but it implied only listed authors were good picks, and it ran dry once readers had read all twelve. Shows the limits of fixed lists.
4. [Reported] Method that worked: intensive study of one genre at a time, plus reviews (watching for repeated key words), patron feedback, and daily staff discussion. The goal was to find the features that made a reader enjoy one author in a genre but not another.
5. [Reported] First appeal found: story-driven versus character-driven. Saricks preferred story and Brown preferred character, and staff and patrons split the same way. Ludlum is action-focused; le Carré focuses on characterization.
6. [Reported] Pacing emerged as a "base-line determiner of reader enjoyment." le Carré fans disliked Ludlum's speed ("too much happened in too much of a blur"), and reviews kept mentioning Ludlum's quick pace. This is an early case of a dislike defining an appeal factor.
7. [Reported] "Frame" (background frame) came from romance and mystery subgenres. Readers sought a particular kind and level of descriptive background, such as Regency dress and manners, or rich period detail in the Brother Cadfael mysteries.
8. [Reported] Setting is not geographic locale. Contemporary romance set in New York or Dallas reads the same way, and a Ludlum thriller could be set almost anywhere. What mattered more was whether the mystery was funny or the thriller "dark and gritty."
9. [Reported] The "ah ha" moment: setting really meant mood. The final setting term merged background frame, mood, and location. It helped separate cozy from darker English village mysteries.
10. [Reported] "Story line" became the umbrella term for point of view (for example, whether a thriller switches between hero and villain), the author's treatment of a subject (Paretsky versus Christie; Tyler versus Steel), and the amount of dialogue. It is meant to be about how the story is told, not a plot summary.
11. [Reported] White space on the page signals the amount of dialogue. Brown believed dialogue-heavy books are character-centered, so white space was a quick visual cue for action-focused versus character-focused books.
12. [Reported] Evolution: in later editions Saricks moved point of view under characterization and dialogue (white space) under pacing. The 1989 order was pacing, characterization, story line, setting. Characterization was articulated first, but pacing was put first because it is the easiest to determine.
13. [Reported] The first iteration of appeal took about three years. The process zigzagged: ideas often failed when tested against more titles, other genres, or readers. A term was kept when it "laid out," meaning it made sense alone and worked with the others.
14. [Reported] Design purpose: appeal was built to triage, quickly ruling titles in or out. That is why it is "so strongly binary": quick or leisurely, action-oriented or character-driven, richly set or generally situated.
15. [Reported] Appeal also had to be easy to learn and to work for titles staff had not read. So it had only four elements and avoided story-specific details.
16. [Reported] Known gaps from its origins: literary fiction got little attention, so style was underweighted. SF was not studied, so world-building, accuracy of detail, and plausibility and rigor never entered the framework.
17. [Reported] The 1989 book spent only four pages on appeal. Most of it covered setting up an RA department, resources, the RA interview, annotations, reading plans, genre study, and promotion.
18. [Reported] Lineage: Betty Rosenberg's Genreflecting and her philosophy of unapologetic reading shaped their genre studies and suggested appeal terms. The Adult Reading Round Table (1984) and suburban Chicago peers also shaped the work.
19. [Reported] Legacy: the book moved suggestion away from popularity and the advisor's own tastes toward a "systematic and tested framework focused on patron preferences," and helped define the RA job.

## Limitations or bias
- The history rests on the founders' memories, decades later, as the author acknowledges ("subject to odd omissions"). The tone is celebratory.
- Books only, in one 1980s suburban library. The genres studied were thrillers, romance, and mystery; SF and literary fiction were not covered.
- No quantitative evidence.
