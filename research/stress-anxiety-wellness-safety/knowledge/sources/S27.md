## Learned points
1. Definition: GAD is "a common mental health condition where you often feel very anxious about lots of different things." The page covers adults aged 18 and over. — [Documented]
2. The main symptom is stress or worry that affects daily life and is hard to control. Other symptoms include poor sleep, restlessness, irritability, poor concentration, tiring easily, tension, stomach problems, palpitations, light-headedness or dizziness, and low mood. — [Documented]
3. Normal anxiety vs GAD: "Most people feel anxious sometimes. It does not always mean you have GAD." GAD is more likely if anxiety is present most of the time and affects everyday life. A bot should normalize everyday anxiety and avoid labeling it. — [Documented]
4. Diagnostic pattern a GP looks for: worry about a wide range of things, anxiety affecting daily life, difficulty controlling the feelings, and anxiety a lot of the time for at least 6 months. — [Documented]
5. It is common to have more than one anxiety disorder, such as panic disorder or phobias. — [Documented]
6. Treatment: talking therapies, usually CBT with a therapist, either alone or in a group; medicines, usually an SSRI antidepressant; or referral to a community mental health team. A GP will usually advise trying talking therapies before medicine. — [Documented]
7. Self-help "Do" list: talk about feelings with friends, family, a health professional, or a counsellor; try recommended self-help books, online tools, or courses; use calming breathing exercises, yoga, or pilates; get enough sleep; exercise regularly (running, walking, swimming, yoga); eat regular healthy meals. — [Documented]
8. Self-help "Don't" list: don't focus on things you cannot change; don't avoid anxiety-provoking situations, but build up time in them gradually; limit caffeine (coffee, tea, cola, energy drinks); don't use alcohol, cigarettes, gambling, or drugs to relieve anxiety. — [Documented]
9. The Anxiety, fear and panic page adds two more "Don'ts": "do not try to do everything at once – set small targets," and "try not to tell yourself that you're alone." Its "Do" list adds free wellbeing audio guides and breathing or meditation. — [Documented]
10. Panic attacks: sudden intense fear with racing heart, faintness, sweating or shaking, shortness of breath, tingling, and nausea. The page says one "usually lasts 5 to 20 minutes" and that panic attacks are frightening but "not dangerous and should not harm you." — [Documented]
11. Anxiety symptoms are listed in three groups: physical (for example chest pains, breathlessness, headaches, GI upset), mental (for example fear of the worst, intrusive traumatic memories, obsessive thoughts), and behavioural (for example avoidance, compulsive checking, struggling in relationships). — [Documented]
12. Physiology explanation: when you are anxious, the body releases stress hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol, which can raise heart rate and sweating. — [Documented]
13. "Do not self-diagnose – speak to a GP if you're worried about how you're feeling." Recurrent anxiety can be the main symptom of several health conditions. — [Documented]
14. When to get help: see a GP if you think you have GAD, if treatments are not helping, or if self-help is not working. In England, adults can refer themselves to NHS talking therapies. (These are UK pathways. A US bot should translate them to a primary-care doctor or licensed therapist.) — [Documented; US translation Inferred]
15. Common stress triggers: work (pressure, unemployment, retirement), family, money, health or bereavement, and difficult past experiences. Even positive life events, such as a new house, a baby, or a wedding, can cause stress. — [Documented]
16. GAD risk factors: another anxiety disorder, a family history of mental health conditions, stressful or traumatic experiences, painful long-term health conditions, and drug or alcohol misuse. GAD is more common in women. — [Documented]
17. Both pages lead with an "Important: Get urgent help" box for mental health crises. This models putting crisis routing first, ahead of self-help content. — [Documented]
18. Avoid UK-only resources for a US audience: Samaritans 116 123, NHS talking therapies, and the GP referral routes. — [Inferred]
