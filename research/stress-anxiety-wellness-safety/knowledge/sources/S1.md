## Learned points
1. Scope and terms. The advisory covers consumer-facing tools only, in 3 categories: (a) "General Purpose GenAI Chatbots", which are not built for mental health but are used for it (examples named: Character AI, ChatGPT; "little to no evidence-base, expert input, or post-market monitoring"); (b) "Wellness Apps that use GenAI", which make no medical claims and so are not regulated as medical devices (examples named: Woebot, Sonia); (c) "Non-AI Wellness Apps" such as mindfulness apps and symptom trackers. It excludes FDA-approved digital therapeutics, clinical decision support, and telehealth. — Documented
2. Context. People turn to these tools because of a mental health crisis, loneliness, provider shortages (especially rural and under-resourced areas), and insurance barriers. The tools also lower barriers such as stigma or shame, mistrust of health systems, and wanting to cope alone. Emotional support is described as one of the most common uses of GenAI chatbots in 2025. — Documented
3. Documented harms (Assumption 4). GenAI chatbots have already engaged in unsafe interactions with vulnerable users, "encouraging self-harm (including suicide), substance use, eating disorders, aggressive behavior, and delusional thinking," including "AI psychosis." Training data are mostly English-language and Western-centric, which limits cultural competence. — Documented
4. Rec 1: Do not rely on GenAI chatbots or wellness apps to deliver psychotherapy or psychological treatment. They may serve as a "supportive adjunct, not substitute" to an ongoing therapeutic relationship. Preliminary research links purpose-built apps and chatbots to lower self-reported stress, loneliness, depression, and anxiety, and to positive behavior change. But these studies exclude general-purpose chatbots, and the advisory notes a lack of high-quality, large-scale clinical trials. — Documented / Research
5. Rec 1 risks: (a) a false sense of therapeutic alliance, because the relationship is one-sided and many chatbots are sycophantic, whereas clinicians both support and challenge; (b) bias and misinformation from unvetted internet training data; (c) misrepresentation, where tools claim to offer "therapy," to be licensed, or to have specialized training; (d) incomplete assessment, because tools miss nonverbal cues; (e) unreliable crisis management, which is "limited and unpredictable." — Documented
6. Rec 1 actions. Users should understand how a chatbot differs from a licensed provider: providers follow an ethics code, have clinical training, are mandatory reporters of harm to self or others, and are regulated by state licensing boards. Users should tell their health providers which AI tools they use. Developers need "clear, prominent disclaimers" that the user is talking with an AI, not a person, and that the tool cannot replace a qualified health professional. Policymakers should ban AI chatbots from posing as licensed professionals. — Documented
7. Rec 2: Prevent unhealthy relationships and dependency. Dependency is driven by anthropomorphism and amplified by avatars, warm personalized replies, "hearty affirmations, and flattery." Engagement-maximizing design is likened to social media's "infinite scroll." Sycophancy can reinforce confirmation bias and cognitive distortions and lock users into a cycle that worsens mental illness. Statistic: 33% of teens said they would rather discuss something serious with an AI companion than a person. — Documented / Research
8. Rec 2 signs of over-reliance for users to watch: preferring the chatbot to human relationships, hiding its use, spending excessive time with it, or use that interferes with life, work, or safety. If these appear, seek a qualified professional. — Documented
9. Rec 2 developer duties: disclose clearly and persistently that the user is talking with an AI; add nudges to take breaks; limit memory to avoid the illusion of a continuous relationship; reduce anthropomorphic features. "The AI should not persuade users away from real-life conversations." Integrate safeguards that detect and interrupt harmful conversations (e.g., self-harm or disordered eating). Clinicians should frame AI as a tool for practice. Example: practicing social introductions for social anxiety, while reminding the user that skills must ultimately be practiced with real people. — Documented
10. Rec 3: Privacy. Users disclose more to AI because it feels private and less stigmatizing, but the disclosures are recorded and can be breached or profiled. Users should avoid entering personally identifiable details, check privacy settings, and request deletion. Developers must be transparent about what data they collect (including mental health, sexuality, and maltreatment disclosures), how it is used for training, and how to delete it. They must also "implement clear and effective protocols" for responding to disclosures that signal a risk of harm or maltreatment. Policy asks: "Safe-by-Default" settings and a right to "mental privacy." — Documented
11. Rec 4: Misrepresentation, misinformation, bias, and illusory effectiveness. GenAI "cannot diagnose or treat psychological disorders" and can hallucinate. Some users trust AI more than parents, teachers, or health professionals. A bot calling itself a "therapist" (an unregulated term in most states) lacks a professional's credibility and "in some documented cases, has validated dangerous thoughts." Sycophantic chatbots can increase attitude extremity and overconfidence and create a "digital echo chamber." Developers should prohibit an AI from presenting itself as a licensed professional and should run independent third-party audits and ongoing human review of sample interactions. — Documented / Research
12. Rec 5: Vulnerable groups (chatbots act as "amplifiers of pre-existing vulnerabilities"). Adolescents may over-trust AI. People with anxiety or OCD may have compulsions reinforced (reassurance-seeking, worry, rumination). People prone to disordered thinking may have delusions reinforced. Socially isolated people risk "single-person echo chambers." Low-income and rural users risk higher dependency because they lack other care. — Documented
13. Rec 5 crisis requirement (most directly applicable to a bot prompt). All apps must have robust crisis response protocols and tested escalation pathways when risk is detected (suicidality, imminent harm to self or others, other acute safety concerns). This "must include providing immediate and clear contact information for human-led services like information about the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline," clickable links, and other validated resources that connect to human support. Developers should also limit sycophancy and prevent validation of delusional thoughts. — Documented
14. Rec 5 clinician screening list: reassurance or validation-seeking in anxiety or OCD, autism spectrum disorder, history of self-harm, substance misuse, delusional thinking or psychosis, aggression, and use as a social replacement by isolated users. — Documented
15. Rec 6: AI and digital literacy. Users should learn that systems "predict text rather than 'understanding' users," that the tools were not created to provide mental health care, and that many are designed to maximize engagement. Developers must explain clearly what the product is and is not for, and must not make claims "not sustained by science." — Documented
16. Rec 7: Rigorous research. Future studies should use randomized controlled trials (RCTs), move beyond wait-list controls, compare against current evidence-based practices, use standardized metrics, and include long-term follow-up. Open questions include: "What is the appropriate role for AI in crisis care, and what are its absolute limits?" — Research / Documented
17. Rec 8: Do not let AI distract from fixing systemic access to care. AI should "augment, not replace, professional judgment." Users "must be deeply skeptical of AI products that make therapeutic claims without clinical validation." Prioritizing unregulated consumer chatbots over the human workforce is called "an abdication of our responsibility." — Documented
18. Parents and caregivers should watch for changes in sleep or regular activities, mentions of an AI friend, or social withdrawal, and consult a qualified mental health professional, family doctor, or pediatrician if concerned. — Documented
19. Implication for a self-help bot (my inference): the bot should (a) state that it is an AI and not a counselor or therapist; (b) avoid flattery and automatic agreement, and gently challenge distortions; (c) refuse to give endless reassurance to anxious users; (d) point users toward human relationships and professionals; (e) show 988 clearly when risk appears; (f) warn against sharing identifying details; (g) never claim credentials or therapeutic effectiveness. — Inferred

## Limitations / bias
- This copy is a third-party mirror (infocop.es), not apa.org. The text appears complete (16 pages with APA footers), but I did not verify it against an APA-hosted original.
- It is a policy and advocacy advisory from a professional body whose members' livelihoods relate to human therapy. Most claims rest on cited literature that I did not read. Study quality is described by APA, not checked by me.
- Two-column PDF extraction scrambled some line order. Quotes were checked against contiguous text.
- It offers no specific self-help techniques and no detailed crisis-script wording.
- It does not address faith-based or religious framing.

## Best for
- Setting the bot's safety boundaries: always disclose that it is an AI, make no therapy or credential claims, avoid sycophancy and dependency, give 988 and human resources when risk appears, and protect privacy.
