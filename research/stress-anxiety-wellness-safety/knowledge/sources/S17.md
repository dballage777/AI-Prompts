## Learned points
1. MIND is a free library of mental health apps evaluated by trained raters at BIDMC's Division of Digital Psychiatry, assessed on cost, privacy, supporting evidence, features, and conditions supported, using the American Psychiatric Association's app evaluation framework (per homepage). — Documented
2. Scale on the read date: "All evaluated apps (503)"; categories include Stress & Anxiety, Sleep, Mood Disorders, PTSD, Self-Harm, Substance Use, and "Non-Specific." Several listed apps describe themselves as AI or chatbot products (e.g., "Ash - AI for Mental Health", "Counselr: Mental Health AI"). — Documented
3. Raters "will be prompted through 105 different questions" about an app's features, privacy settings, clinical foundations and more; filter dimensions are Cost, Developer Type, Supported Conditions, Functionalities, Uses, Features, Engagements, Clinical Foundations, Inputs, Outputs and Privacy Options. — Documented
4. Privacy questions include: Is there a privacy policy? Does the app declare data use and purpose? Does it report security measures? Is data stored only on the device or on a server? Can you delete your data? Can you opt out of data collection? Does it claim to meet HIPAA? Tooltip notes some apps may retain data permanently even if the user deletes the app. — Documented
5. MIND defines PHI as personal health information entered into the app (name, birthday, message content, mental health information) and counts data as "shared if it leaves the app in any way." — Documented
6. Clinical-foundation / safety questions include: Does the app appear to do what it claims to do? Is the content well-written, correct, and relevant? Does it contain supporting studies? Does it specify that it is not a replacement for medical care? "In the case of an emergency does the app appropriately advise the patient?" "Can the app cause harm?" — Documented
7. The "Can the app cause harm?" tooltip operationalises harm as recommendations that directly defy clinical guidelines or overtly false information, with the example of a suicide hotline number that doesn't work. Implication: a bot must only give verified crisis numbers. — Documented (implication Inferred)
8. Feature checklist relevant to stress/anxiety self-help: deep breathing, mindfulness, journaling, mood and symptom tracking, goal setting/habits, psychoeducation, CBT, ACT, DBT, iCBT or sleep therapy, peer support, screeners/assessments, and "chatbot interaction" (tooltip example: interaction with a virtual character). — Documented
9. Developer-type questions ask whether an app comes from a for-profit company, non-profit, trusted healthcare company, academic institution, or government; raters are told "When in doubt, put for profit." — Documented
10. Care-integration questions: Is the app intended as an adjunct to care (built-in way to connect with a provider)? Does it connect the user with a licensed therapist? Can data be exported or sent to a medical record? A teletherapy app is described as replacing in-person care rather than hybrid care. — Documented
11. Access/equity questions: free vs. in-app purchases vs. subscription; works offline; Spanish and French support; accessibility (adjustable text size, text-to-voice). — Documented
12. Disclaimer shown on reviews: each review "represents the view of the app rater and is not an endorsement by MIND." — Documented
13. Implication for a wellness-bot prompt: the same yardstick MIND applies to apps can be turned into bot rules: state clearly it is not a replacement for medical care, give correct emergency guidance, avoid advice that contradicts clinical guidelines, be transparent about data use, and avoid overclaiming. — Inferred

## Limitations / bias
- Framework questions were taken from UI strings in the site's code, not from a narrative methods page; grouping into APA framework levels and any scoring rules could not be confirmed.
- No individual app ratings were opened; no data on how many apps pass or fail safety items.
- Database covers apps, not general-purpose AI chatbots; applying its criteria to a bot is an inference.
- Last-updated date not stated.

## Best for
- A ready-made checklist of safety, privacy, and evidence criteria (not a replacement for care, correct emergency advice, no guideline-defying or false info) to mirror in a wellness bot's rules and when recommending apps.
