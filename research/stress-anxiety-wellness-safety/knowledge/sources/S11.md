## Learned points
1. Definition: mental health "includes emotional, psychological, and social well-being" and is more than the absence of mental illness. Self-care can help maintain mental health and support treatment and recovery for people who have a mental illness. — [Documented]
2. NIMH's 8 self-care tips: (1) get regular exercise; (2) eat healthy, regular meals and stay hydrated; (3) make sleep a priority; (4) try a relaxing activity; (5) set goals and priorities; (6) practice gratitude; (7) focus on positivity by identifying and challenging negative and unhelpful thoughts; (8) stay connected with friends or family. — [Documented]
3. Exercise specifics: "Just 30 minutes of walking every day can boost your mood." Small amounts add up, so people should not be discouraged if they cannot do 30 minutes at once. — [Documented]
4. Diet and substances: watch caffeine and alcohol intake and how they affect mood; for some people, cutting back on caffeine and alcohol helps. — [Documented]
5. Sleep: keep a schedule, get enough sleep, and reduce blue light from phones and computers before bedtime. — [Documented]
6. Relaxation: try relaxation or wellness programs or apps that may include meditation, muscle relaxation, or breathing exercises. Schedule regular times for these and for enjoyable low-stress activities such as music, reading, and time in nature. NIMH gives no step-by-step breathing protocol on these pages. — [Documented]
7. Goals and priorities: decide what must get done now and what can wait, learn to say "no" to new tasks when you are taking on too much, and appreciate what you accomplished at the end of the day. Gratitude: name specific things daily, either written down or replayed in your mind. — [Documented]
8. Self-care is individual. Finding what works "may take trial and error." — [Documented]
9. When to seek professional help: severe or distressing symptoms lasting 2 weeks or more, such as difficulty sleeping; appetite or unplanned weight changes; difficulty getting out of bed because of mood; difficulty concentrating; loss of interest in things you usually enjoy; inability to complete usual tasks; irritability, frustration, or restlessness. — [Documented]
10. How to find help: start with a primary care provider, who can refer you to a psychologist, psychiatrist, or clinical social worker. SAMHSA's website can help locate services in your area. — [Documented]
11. Crisis wording: if someone is struggling or having thoughts of suicide, call or text 988 or chat at 988lifeline.org. The service is described as confidential, free, and available 24/7. In life-threatening situations, call 911. — [Documented]
12. Stress vs. anxiety (fact sheet): stress is "the physical or mental response to an external cause" and generally goes away once the situation resolves; it can be positive (it may help you meet a deadline) or negative. Anxiety is "your body's reaction to stress" and can occur even with no current threat; it involves persistent apprehension or dread that interferes with life. — [Documented]
13. Shared symptoms of stress and anxiety (fact sheet): excessive worry, uneasiness, tension, headaches or body pain, high blood pressure, and loss of sleep. Anxiety that persists may affect sleep and the immune, digestive, cardiovascular, and reproductive systems, and may raise the risk of anxiety disorders or depression. — [Documented]
14. Possible anxiety-disorder risk flags (fact sheet): it feels like you can't manage the stress, and the symptoms interfere with everyday life, cause you to avoid doing things, or seem always present. — [Documented]
15. Coping list (fact sheet): keep a journal; use an app with relaxation exercises (deep breathing, visualization) or mindfulness, which it defines as "actively paying attention to the present moment"; exercise and eat regular meals; keep a sleep routine; avoid excess caffeine; identify and challenge negative thoughts; reach out to supportive friends or family. — [Documented]
16. Treatment (fact sheet): psychotherapy (talk therapy) and medication are "the two main treatments for anxiety." Many people benefit from a combination of the two. If symptoms won't go away or you are struggling to cope, it may be time to talk to a professional. — [Documented]
17. NIMH points to "GREAT," a featured video on practices to manage stress and anxiety developed by an NIMH clinical psychologist. The video content was not retrieved, so its steps are not recorded here. — [Documented; content not read]
18. For a bot: these pages support a gentle, non-diagnostic tone. Offer general self-care, use the 2-week, severity, and interference thresholds to suggest seeing a professional, and always include 988 and 911 for crisis. — [Inferred]

## Limitations / bias
- These are high-level consumer pages. They give no step-by-step breathing, CBT, or muscle-relaxation protocols and cite no effect sizes.
- The "I'm So Stressed Out!" fact sheet is written partly for teens, with school examples, so adapt the examples for adults.
- A site banner says NIH is replacing "Artificial Intelligence" with "Super Intelligence" under an executive order. This does not affect the health content.

## Best for
- An authoritative US government baseline for a bot's self-care tips, its "when to see a professional" thresholds (2+ weeks, interference), and its standard 988/911 crisis wording.
