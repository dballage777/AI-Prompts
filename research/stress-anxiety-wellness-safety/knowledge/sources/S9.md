## Learned points
1. Normal anxiety vs. disorder: feeling anxious is a normal part of life (worry about health, money, school, work, family); anxiety disorders involve more than occasional worry — anxiety "does not go away, is felt in many situations, and can get worse over time." Types named: generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, various phobia-related disorders. — Documented
2. Prevalence (hub): "About a third of U.S. adolescents and adults" experience an anxiety disorder at some point in life; symptoms can interfere with job performance, schoolwork and relationships; in severe cases a person may fear common situations, avoid social encounters or refuse to leave home. — Documented
3. Prevalence (stats page, NCS-R 2001–2003, DSM-IV): 19.1% of U.S. adults had any anxiety disorder in the past year (females 23.4%, males 14.3%; ages 18–29 22.3%, 30–44 22.7%, 45–59 20.6%, 60+ 9.0%); 31.1% lifetime. Of past-year cases, impairment was mild 43.5%, moderate 33.7%, serious 22.8% (Sheehan Disability Scale). — Research
4. Adolescents (NCS-A 2001–2004): 31.9% lifetime prevalence of any anxiety disorder among ages 13–18 (females 38.0%, males 26.1%); 8.3% of those had severe impairment. — Research
5. Data caveats: estimates are 20+ years old, use DSM-IV and include PTSD and OCD, which DSM-5 no longer classifies as anxiety disorders; NCS-R response rate 70.9%. — Documented
6. GAD definition: people "feel extremely worried or nervous more frequently or more intensely" — even when worry is out of proportion; persistent anxiety/dread that interferes with life, lasting months or years; develops gradually, usually starting in early adulthood; more common in women (including pregnancy/postpartum). — Documented
7. GAD signs: excessive worry about everyday things; trouble controlling worry; irritable/"on edge"; restless/trouble relaxing; poor concentration; sleep problems or fatigue; headaches, muscle aches, stomachaches, tension; trembling/twitching; sweating, lightheadedness, feeling out of breath; trouble swallowing; frequent bathroom trips. Adults commonly worry about job security or performance, health, finances, family wellbeing, being late, chores. Symptoms worsen during stress. — Documented
8. Diagnostic threshold (for professional use, not for a bot to apply): difficulty controlling worry on most days for at least 6 months plus at least three of: restless/on edge, fatigue, trouble concentrating, irritability, muscle tension, sleep problems. A provider may do a physical exam to rule out an unrelated physical cause. — Documented
9. Who to see: a health care provider such as a primary care doctor, psychiatrist, psychologist or clinical social worker; a primary care provider can refer to a mental health professional. SAMHSA's site helps locate services. — Documented
10. Co-occurring conditions: depression, other anxiety disorders, PTSD, chronic pain, cardiovascular problems, suicidal thoughts or behaviors; GAD commonly associated with alcohol and substance misuse. — Documented
11. Treatment: psychotherapy, medication or both; may take trial and error and time to work. CBT is "research-supported," well studied and called the "gold standard" psychotherapy for GAD (become aware of inaccurate/harmful automatic thoughts, question them, understand their effect, change self-defeating behaviors). ACT (acceptance, mindfulness, engagement in meaningful activities) has "a growing body of research." Therapy works in person or via telehealth. — Documented
12. Medications named (prescriber decisions): SSRIs/SNRIs (several weeks to work; side effects like headache, nausea, sleep difficulty), benzodiazepines (rapid relief but tolerance/dependence risk; often short-term), buspirone (non-sedative, less addictive, 3–4 weeks for full effect). FDA site for current warnings. — Documented
13. Self-help limits: support groups can help but advice "should be used cautiously and does not replace treatment"; healthy habits (less caffeine, enough sleep) and stress management (exercise, mindfulness, meditation) can reduce symptoms but alone "cannot replace treatment." — Documented
14. Support guidance: educate yourself; talk honestly with someone you trust; seek professional help when anxiety causes problems at school, work, or with friends/family; "Be kind and patient with yourself," acknowledge small progress, expect setbacks. — Documented
15. Crisis: "call or text the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline at 988 or chat at 988lifeline.org. In life-threatening situations, call 911." (repeated on hub and GAD pages; site banner "In Crisis? Call or Text 988"). — Documented
16. Implication for a bot: it can name common anxiety signs and normalize worry, but should not diagnose; when worry is persistent, hard to control, or interferes with work/relationships, recommend a primary care provider or mental health professional and note CBT/ACT as evidence-based options; never advise on medications. — Inferred
