## Learned points
1. What CBT is: a talking therapy done with a trained therapist, based on the idea that thoughts, feelings, and actions are connected. Example: the thought "no one likes me" leads to withdrawing from friends, which makes the thought feel truer. Some CBT ideas can be practised on your own. — Documented (summary-only)
2. Evidence claim: CBT is "one of the most effective talking therapies." Research shows it can help with anxiety, low mood, stress, and sleep problems. The page gives no figures or citations. — Reported (summary-only)
3. Reframing ("catch it, check it, change it"), step 1: know the unhelpful patterns: always expecting the worst; ignoring the good and focusing on the bad; black-and-white thinking; seeing yourself as the sole cause of negative situations. Step 2: practise catching them through the day. — Documented (summary-only)
4. Reframing, step 3: check the thought. How likely is the feared outcome? Is there good evidence? What other explanations or outcomes are possible? What evidence supports alternative views? What would you say to a friend? Step 4: change it to a neutral or more positive thought. Example: "I'm prepared. I've put a lot of work in and I'm going to do my best." A 7-prompt thought record is offered. — Documented (summary-only)
5. Reframing caveat: "there are no right or wrong answers." Reframing builds flexibility and perspective but "will not resolve the problems you face." — Documented (summary-only)
6. Tackling worries: write worries down. Set a regular "worry time" of "10 or 15 minutes" (the NHS suggests before bed). When a worry appears outside that time, say "I'll set that aside for my worry time." Use a "worry tree" to separate practical problems from hypothetical worries. For practical ones, make a specific plan (what, how, when) and act now or schedule it. For hypothetical ones, acknowledge them, don't dwell, and refocus. For build-up, use exercise, yoga, breathing, mindfulness, or meditation. — Documented (summary-only)
7. Facing fears (graded exposure): (1) notice your physical feelings and behaviours (example: panicky, hot, and shaky on public transport, getting off early); (2) understand that the fear response can be over-sensitive, and "the best way to overcome this fear is not to avoid these activities but to gradually expose yourself to them"; (3) list feared situations and rate each from 0 to 100, then order them (example: a short bus trip with a friend scores 10, alone scores 50); (4) start with the easiest and stay "until you rate your fear as having reduced by half"; (5) avoid distractions, phone use, or reassurance-seeking during exposure; (6) move up the list, and "do not be discouraged" if some steps take longer. — Documented (summary-only)
8. Problem solving: (1) focus on your values (relationships, finances, career or study) to pick a priority; (2) sort solvable problems from hypothetical worries ("what if…" or past events); (3) brainstorm solutions for 5–10 minutes, then weigh pros, cons, feasibility, and resources; (4) make a specific plan (what, who can support, how long, when); (5) use worry time; (6) make time to relax (exercise, hobbies, mindfulness, PMR); (7) review what worked and tick off solved problems; (8) journal. — Documented (summary-only)
9. Related hub guides I did not read: Bounce back from life's challenges, Tackle your to-do list, Stay on top of things. Also offered: a video on challenging thoughts and a 5-question "Mind Plan" quiz. — Documented (summary-only)
10. The technique pages I read contain no "when to get help" guidance and no safety cautions. Escalation lives only in a separate "Urgent support" section. — Documented (absence)
11. Urgent support (UK only, not usable for US adults): "if you or someone else is in danger, call 999 or go to A&E now." For urgent but non-emergency help, use NHS 111 (mental health option). Listening lines: Samaritans 116 123, Childline, CALM. See a GP urgently for first-time hallucinations or delusions. NHS talking therapies accept self-referral for stress, anxiety, and depression. These are useful only as a structural model: emergency, then urgent, then listening line, then routine care. — Documented (summary-only)
