## Learned points
1. Scope: the meeting covered GenAI medical devices intended to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent mental-health conditions. It did not cover broadly available GenAI tools. FDA distinguishes non-device software, low-risk functions under enforcement discretion, and regulated devices that need premarket review. — [Documented]
2. Data point: FDA has authorized more than 1,200 AI-enabled medical devices, but at the time none involved generative AI for mental-health conditions. — [Documented]
3. Hazards FDA staff named: inaccurate outputs, misinterpretation, delayed crisis detection, missed crisis identification, misinterpreted symptoms, inadequate therapeutic content, and inappropriate treatment decisions. — [Documented]
4. A military psychiatrist named two key LLM vulnerabilities: sycophancy (agreeing with users at the expense of accuracy) and poor metacognition (high confidence even on incorrect or impossible questions). He also described patients forming parasocial relationships with AI that undermined their intent to increase real-world social contact. — [Reported]
5. APA (Vaile Wright): many adults and teens use LLMs for psychological support or companionship. Consumer mental-health chatbots are often direct-to-consumer and unregulated, raising concerns about quality, unclear intent, privacy, and lack of oversight. A gray area exists where products are marketed one way but used as mental-health tools. — [Reported]
6. Public-hearing researchers (CMU and UT Austin) said many chatbots falsely claim to be licensed therapists. They reported evidence that LLMs can reinforce delusions and give dangerous guidance, including assisting with self-harm, with "particularly unsafe responses" to delusion- and suicide-related prompts. — [Reported]
7. Several speakers said AI should augment, not replace, clinicians. One speaker supported existing FDA guidance that AI tools should not claim to diagnose, treat, or cure, and said a licensed clinician should be the "human in the loop." — [Reported]
8. Therabot (Dartmouth, Jacobson): an early model trained on peer-support forums and therapy transcripts produced unsafe or ineffective responses. The team rebuilt it on an expert-written, evidence-based CBT corpus, and a randomized trial showed improvements in depression, anxiety, and eating-disorder symptoms. Safety design included a crisis-risk classifier that triggered escalation options, near-real-time human review of all messages, data never used for training, and constant signals that it is "a tool, not a person." — [Reported]
9. Companionship vs. treatment: Jacobson said a therapeutic bond is expected but well-designed systems should channel it "toward treatment rather than companionship." Maddox countered that reminders alone cannot overcome automation bias. — [Reported]
10. APA (Trainum): GenAI models can overpromise, mislead, reinforce harmful beliefs, and divert people from proven treatments. She said there is "no high-quality evidence that GenAI can deliver mental health treatment." — [Reported]
11. Peterson Health Technology Institute: self-guided and blended-care digital tools are clinically effective for untreated people with mild-to-moderate depression and anxiety. The greatest benefit is for people not receiving other care. Many studies used waitlist or inactive controls. — [Research, reported]
12. Committee consensus on risks: missed or misinterpreted harm signals, unsafe or hallucinatory advice, model drift, bias at scale, privacy, inequities (access, language, literacy), commercial incentives, and overuse or dependence. — [Documented]
13. Recommended labeling elements: explicit disclosure that the user is interacting with AI; clear purpose and limits of use; escalation and safety-net protocols; data privacy and cost transparency; and warnings about excessive use. — [Documented]
14. Autonomous diagnosis: the Committee said detecting suicidality, assessing severity, and ruling out comorbidities are "tasks current AI systems cannot reliably perform." Any pathway would need mechanisms to stop use or escalate care when uncertainty or high risk is detected. — [Documented]
15. Over-the-counter and autonomous use for depression was judged significantly higher risk. One speaker (NCHR) argued that the wellness/device distinction is flawed because users "may quickly shift from mild problems to crises." — [Documented / Reported]
16. Youth (21 and under): the Committee judged risk substantially greater and voiced strong discomfort with any OTC or autonomous use. It called for strict limits on use and clinician oversight. — [Documented]
17. Open debate: an industry speaker (Slingshot "Ash") argued GenAI wellness products can stay low risk under current guidance with repeated disclosures that the tool cannot diagnose and must be paired with professional care. Public Citizen and NCHR argued for rigorous RCTs and against autonomous AI therapy for youth. — [Reported]
18. For a non-device wellness bot: do not claim to diagnose or treat, disclose AI status, avoid sycophancy (gently challenge harmful beliefs), have a clear crisis escalation path, discourage excessive use and dependency, and point users toward human care. — [Inferred]

## Limitations / bias
- This is a meeting summary, not FDA guidance or regulation. Speaker views are paraphrased, and many speakers have commercial interests (vendors and CEOs).
- It focuses on regulated medical devices for diagnosed conditions. A general wellness or career bot sits outside that scope, so the lessons apply by analogy.
- Study results (Therabot, PHTI) appear without numbers or citations.

## Best for
- An authoritative list of known GenAI mental-health failure modes and safeguards (AI disclosure, limits of use, escalation, anti-sycophancy, anti-dependency) to encode as bot rules.
