## Learned points
1. Definition (hub): worry becomes a problem, as in generalised anxiety disorder (GAD), when it is excessive, uncontrollable, persistent, and distressing or impairing. GAD worry spans several life areas (health, work, relationships, finances), unlike situation-specific anxieties such as social anxiety or phobias. — Documented (summary-only)
2. Workbook "What? Me Worry? Mastering Your Worries" has 10 modules, to be done in order: (1) Overview of Generalised Anxiety, (2) Overview of Worrying, (3) Challenging Uncontrollability Beliefs, (4) Attention Training, (5) Challenging Danger Beliefs, (6) Challenging Positive Beliefs about worry, (7) Problem-Solving, (8) Helpful Thinking, (9) Accepting Uncertainty, (10) Self Management Plan (including planning for setbacks). — Documented (summary-only)
3. The module order shows the underlying model. Worry is maintained by negative beliefs (that it is uncontrollable or dangerous), positive beliefs (that worry helps or prepares me), and intolerance of uncertainty. Treatment targets these beliefs, along with attention training and problem-solving, rather than only the content of worries. — Inferred from module titles
4. Other hub resources: info sheets (What is Generalised Anxiety?, What is Mindfulness?, Mindfulness and Letting Go, How Worry Works, Postpone Your Worry, Accepting Uncertainty, AI and Your Mental Health), 4 worksheets, and guided audio ("Mindfulness of the breath", "Watching thoughts"). — Documented (summary-only)
5. Worry postponement, step 1: create a worry period with the same time, place, and length each day (example: "6pm, study, 20 min"). Choose a distinct, comfortable, distraction-free place used only for worrying, not a regular spot such as a lounge chair. The time should be convenient and not close to bedtime. — Documented
6. Step 2: postpone. As soon as you notice a worry, jot it down in a couple of words (a small notebook helps). Remind yourself there will be time later, that you will be better placed to deal with it then, and that there are more important or pleasant things now. Turn your focus to the present, using mindfulness if helpful. Then do something "practical, positive, pleasant, active or nurturing." — Documented
7. Step 3: during the worry period, review the list. Worry only about the items you feel you must. Drop items that no longer matter. Stay within the set time, and writing on paper may help. People often predict they cannot postpone worry and are surprised that they can, which builds a sense of control. The skill requires repeated practice. — Documented
8. Note the difference from S5 (NHS): CCI advises the worry period should not be close to bedtime, while the NHS page suggests worry time "before bed." This is a practical point of disagreement between sources. — Documented (cross-source)
9. Intolerance of uncertainty: some people see uncertainty as "awful and unbearable." Worry feels useful as preparation and gives an illusion of control, but it does not make outcomes more certain. It leaves the person worked up and often unable to act. — Documented
10. Strategy 1, challenge intolerance of uncertainty with written questions. Is it possible to be certain about everything? What are the advantages and disadvantages of requiring certainty? Do you predict bad outcomes just because things are uncertain, and how likely are positive or neutral outcomes? Can you live with a small chance of something negative? How do you already tolerate uncertainty elsewhere? Ask a friend how they cope. — Documented
11. Strategy 2, acceptance and mindfulness in 3 steps: (a) be aware of current thoughts and body sensations, using the breath to stay present, and acknowledge them ("ah, so that's how it is"); (b) let go of the need for a quick fix ("its only a need for certainty thought, just let it go"); (c) stay non-judgmental with gentle curiosity, then return attention to sounds, body sensations, breath, or the task at hand. — Documented
12. Bot implication (my inference): for chronic worriers, the bot can coach worry postponement and uncertainty questions rather than trying to resolve each worry. Answering every "what if" is reassurance, which S3 shows maintains anxiety. If worry seems excessive, uncontrollable, and impairing across life areas, the bot should suggest a professional assessment instead of naming a diagnosis. — Inferred
