## Learned points
1. Hub: feeling tense, stressed, or worried under pressure is normal. Anxiety becomes a problem when it is "intense and prolonged" and interferes with daily functioning. The hub offers about 21 information sheets, including vicious cycle of anxiety, unhelpful thinking styles, behavioural experiments, situational exposure, safety behaviours, coping with stress, anxiety and exercise, CBT for anxiety, anxiety surfing, and media literacy. It also offers 10 worksheets: symptom record, breathing rate record, relaxation monitoring, exposure diary, ABC/ABCD/ABCDE thought diaries, and a behavioural experiment worksheet. — Documented (summary-only for hub)
2. Psychoeducation: anxiety is like fear but occurs "in the absence of real danger." The person believes they are in danger. The fight/flight response affects physical, cognitive, and behavioural systems. The sheet's estimate is that 1 in 5 people experience significantly anxious mood at some point in life. The sheet stresses that anxiety is "not in itself, harmful." Unhelpful comments like "just get yourself together" do not help. — Documented
3. Physical symptoms explained: faster and stronger heartbeat; blood redirected away from skin, fingers, and toes (pale, cold, tingling); faster, deeper breathing (breathlessness, chest tightness, and dizziness, light-headedness, or feelings of unreality from reduced blood supply to the head); sweating; widened pupils (blurred vision). Arousal tapers off gradually, which leaves people "keyed up." — Documented
4. Breathing rationale: anxious overbreathing without exertion expels too much carbon dioxide, causing dizziness, light-headedness, headache, weakness, tingling, and muscle stiffness. Overbreathing is "not specifically dangerous." The normal rate is 10–14 breaths per minute. — Documented
5. Breathing Retraining steps (exact): (1) sit in a comfortable chair or lie on a bed; (2) breathe in for 4 seconds, through the nose if possible; (3) hold for 2 seconds; (4) breathe out over 6 seconds, through the nose if possible, then pause slightly; (5) "Practise, practise, practise!" — Documented
6. Breathing tips: beginners can start at 3-in, 1-hold, 4-out. Use stomach breathing, not chest breathing; check with one hand on the stomach and one on the chest, and the stomach hand should rise on the in-breath. Practise once or twice a day when relatively free of distraction. With practice, it can help in anxious situations too. — Documented
7. Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR) preparation: people with injuries or physical problems that may cause muscle pain should consult a doctor first. Minimize distractions, use soft lighting, a supportive chair, loose clothes, and no shoes. Avoid practising after heavy meals or after alcohol or other intoxicants. — Documented
8. PMR procedure: slow your breathing and give yourself permission to relax. Tense each muscle group for about 5 seconds, enough to feel tension but not a great deal of pain. Release and stay relaxed for about 10 seconds, perhaps saying "Relax." At the end, stay seated for a few moments to become alert. 18-group sequence: right hand and forearm, right upper arm, left hand and forearm, left upper arm, forehead, eyes and cheeks, mouth and jaw, neck (be careful), shoulders, shoulder blades and back, chest and stomach (deep breath), hips and buttocks, right upper leg, right lower leg (go slowly to avoid cramps), right foot, then the same three for the left leg. — Documented
9. Reassurance-seeking is normal, but it becomes a problem when it is frequent: distress when you cannot ask, spreading to small daily choices, short-lived relief, indecision, and relationship conflict. The cycle: worry, urge, ask, short relief, anxiety returns. Over time the urge grows, confidence falls, and relationship stress rises. — Documented
10. Breaking the reassurance cycle with "Stop; Drop; Delay." STOP the behaviour completely, which works best for infrequent or situation-specific asking. DROP it gradually with a stepladder, for example fewer people or fewer times per week. DELAY the urge, first for hours, then days or weeks. Also practise tolerating uncertainty, "urge surfing," and returning attention to the present. Self-talk example: "Of course I want to seek reassurance, but I am choosing not to at this time." Be kind to yourself, and involve supports. — Documented
11. CCI on AI (useful for bot design): AI can help with practical problem-solving, breaking tasks into steps, designing graded exposure hierarchies, motivation and routines, journaling prompts, and simplifying information. It also says AI "does not replace speaking to a psychologist or using self-help materials based on scientific evidence." — Documented
12. CCI on AI, cautions: AI hallucinates and can be biased. What you type can be saved and reviewed, so avoid identifying information. Relying on AI to tell you what to do or to make you feel better can function as a safety behaviour, like reassurance-seeking. Do not use AI in place of therapy, for personal advice, diagnoses, or treatment plans, for important medical, legal, or relationship decisions, in a crisis or emergency, or for emotional comfort or validation. If distressed or unsafe, reach out to a real person: a trusted friend, a GP, or a crisis service. — Documented
13. Bot implication (my inference): a self-help bot can teach the 4-2-6 breath and PMR, explain fight/flight to normalize symptoms, and help build exposure ladders or worry plans. It should notice when a user repeatedly asks for the same reassurance, gently name the pattern, and offer Stop/Drop/Delay rather than endless reassurance. The PMR injury caution should be repeated. — Inferred

## Limitations / bias
- This is an Australian government source. It refers to a GP and uses Australian spelling and services; US equivalents (988, a primary care doctor) must be substituted.
- The handouts are short and give practice guidance without citations. The "1 in 5" figure has no source or country. The PMR sheet has typos and a cut-off sentence.
- The hub page was read only as a condensed extraction. I did not read the exposure, safety-behaviour, thinking-styles, or CBT sheets.
- No explicit crisis or suicide guidance appears in the sheets I read, apart from the AI sheet's "reach out to a real person."

## Best for
- Exact, clinician-authored steps for breathing retraining, progressive muscle relaxation, and reducing reassurance-seeking, plus a government clinic's list of safe versus unsafe uses of AI for mental health.
