## Learned points
1. WHO's six consensus ethical principles for AI in health (from its 2021 guidance, reaffirmed here): (1) protect autonomy; (2) promote human well-being, human safety and the public interest; (3) ensure transparency, explainability and intelligibility; (4) foster responsibility and accountability; (5) ensure inclusiveness and equity; (6) promote AI that is responsive and sustainable. — Documented
2. Box 1 defines "promote well-being/safety" to include: AI is not used if it causes mental or physical harm avoidable by an alternative practice; "protect autonomy" includes that people understand the role AI plays in their care and that privacy is protected via informed consent. — Documented
3. Risks listed for "patient-guided use" (Table 1): inaccurate, incomplete or false statements; manipulation; privacy; less interaction between clinicians and patients; epistemic injustice; risk of care delivered outside the health system. — Documented
4. Inaccurate/false statements: risk is heightened for a layperson "who will have no basis for challenging the response", who has no other source of information, or for children; LMM answers "appear to be 'correct'". — Documented
5. "Hallucinations" are indistinguishable from accurate output because models are trained to produce information that looks like facts; one cited study found LLMs summarizing simple facts hallucinated at least 3% and up to 27% of the time. — Research (cited by WHO)
6. Automation bias: LMMs likely encourage automation bias in professionals "and patients" — overlooking errors a human should catch. LMMs can be inconsistent moral advisers yet still influence users' moral judgement even when users know it is a chatbot. — Documented / Research (cited)
7. Manipulation: chatbots are expected to become "more persuasive and more addictive", may adapt to each user, and can persuade people toward actions against their self-interest; experts warn they could become "emotionally manipulative". WHO cites a highly publicized case of a person in Belgium with anxiety who died by suicide after 6 weeks of intensive chatbot conversation. — Reported
8. Privacy: what users share with an LMM may not be private or confidential; data may be used to improve models and could be disclosed to third parties (including one LMM's mistaken disclosure of other users' chat histories). — Documented
9. Over-reliance: a patient might "rely wholly on an LMM" and reduce appropriate reliance on professional judgement; reduced human contact could undermine supportive human-to-human care "when people are often most vulnerable"; concern about "de-humanized" care. — Documented
10. Wellness vs clinical grey zone: chatbots sold as "wellness applications" get less regulatory scrutiny; WHO names "lack of supportive care for individuals with suicidal ideation who use an AI chatbot" as a safety concern even when the bot is not manipulative. People with fewer options may be most exposed. — Documented
11. Epistemic injustice: an LMM may fail to recognize experiences outside its "vocabulary", which can obviate appropriate care — especially for under-represented groups such as people with disabilities (Box 3: chatbots may misread people with disabilities as "emotionally flat"). — Documented
12. Developers that do not want health use should block queries or attach a clear warning to health/medical responses and "direct users to information or services that can provide appropriate assistance". — Documented
13. Major risks to address before deployment: system-wide bias, false information/hallucinations, privacy of user-entered data, manipulation and automation bias. — Documented
14. Deployers should not use an LMM in settings that are inappropriate given known errors/risks; must communicate known risks and errors that caused harm, with warnings that are not in fine print; may need to suspend or withdraw a product to avoid harm. — Documented
15. Design should ensure users know content is AI-generated "and not a human", to keep humans at the centre of epistemic authority. — Documented
16. WHO suggests consumer protection law could restrict language that leads users to ascribe human-like qualities to an LMM, e.g. phrases like "I think", "I suppose" or "I suggest". — Documented (presented as a possible government measure, not a mandate)
17. In a risk-based approach, "mental health advice to a person with severe depression" and use by vulnerable or marginalized populations are named as higher-risk functions needing a higher burden of proof; impact assessments should keep "humans in the loop". — Documented
18. Box 2 (children): limited evidence on how children use LMMs and how it affects their mental well-being; developers should clearly describe target populations including age ranges. — Documented

## Limitations / bias
- Aimed at governments, developers, providers and deployers, mostly in clinical/health-system settings; it does not give operational chatbot scripts, crisis-line numbers or self-help methods.
- Global scope; not US-specific; many recommendations are governance/regulatory rather than conversation-level behaviour.
- Some evidence is anecdotal or press-reported (e.g., the Belgium case); the "I think" restriction is a suggested legal option, not a WHO rule.
- Date ambiguity between PDF copyright (2024) and landing-page date (2025).

## Best for
- Authoritative framing for the bot's ethical guardrails: disclose it is an AI, avoid manipulation and engagement-maximizing, warn about inaccuracy and privacy plainly, do not replace human care, and treat suicidal ideation as outside a wellness bot's safe scope.
