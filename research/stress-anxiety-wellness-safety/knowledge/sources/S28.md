## Learned points
1. Anxiety disorders are the most common mental disorders worldwide. They affected 470 million people in 2023, about 5.8% of the global population (source: GBD 2023, IHME). — [Research]
2. Treatment gap: "highly effective treatments" exist, but only about 1 in 4 people in need (27.6%) receive any treatment (source: Alonso et al., Depress Anxiety 2018, World Mental Health Surveys, 21 countries). — [Research]
3. Barriers to care: not knowing anxiety is a treatable condition, low investment in services, too few trained providers, and stigma. A bot can lower the first barrier by telling users anxiety is treatable. — [Documented]
4. Definition: unlike everyday anxiety, anxiety disorders involve fear and worry that are "both intense and excessive." They are hard to control, cause significant distress, and interfere with family, social, school, or work life. Symptoms usually last at least several months. — [Documented]
5. Symptoms: trouble concentrating or deciding, irritability, tension or restlessness, nausea or abdominal distress, palpitations, sweating or trembling, trouble sleeping, and a sense of impending danger or doom. Avoiding feared situations is typical. — [Documented]
6. Types: generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, agoraphobia, separation anxiety disorder, specific phobias, and selective mutism. People can have more than one. — [Documented]
7. Safety-relevant fact: anxiety disorders raise the risk of depression, substance use disorders, and suicidal thoughts and behaviours. This is why a bot should screen for these when anxiety is severe. — [Documented]
8. Contributing factors: social, psychological, and biological factors interact. Abuse, severe losses, and other adverse experiences raise the risk. Anxiety and physical illness, such as cardiovascular disease, affect each other in both directions. — [Documented]
9. Prevention: exercise programmes can help prevent anxiety disorders in adults. For young people, parental education and school social-emotional learning programmes help. — [Documented]
10. Psychological interventions are "essential treatments." They can be delivered individually or in groups, in person or online, and "through self-help manuals, websites and apps." CBT-based approaches, including exposure therapy ("learn to face their fears"), have the most evidence. — [Documented]
11. Stress-management skills, specifically relaxation and mindfulness, "can help reduce symptoms of anxiety disorders." — [Documented]
12. Medication: SSRIs can be useful for adults, taking side effects, availability, and preference into account. Benzodiazepines are "generally not recommended" because of dependence risk and limited long-term effectiveness. A bot should never advise on starting or stopping medication. — [Documented; bot rule Inferred]
13. Self-care list: avoid or cut down on alcohol and avoid illicit drugs; exercise regularly, "even if it's just a short walk"; keep regular eating and sleeping habits; learn relaxation techniques such as slow breathing and progressive muscle relaxation; and practise mindfulness meditation, "even if it's just a few minutes per day." — [Documented]
14. WHO frames self-care as supporting treatment, not replacing it: "Self-care can play an important role in supporting treatment." — [Documented]
15. WHO resources: Problem Management Plus (PM+), a CBT-based manual covering stress management, problem solving, and social support that lay helpers can deliver; Self-Help Plus (SH+), a group stress course; and the self-help book "Doing What Matters in Times of Stress." GAD and panic disorder are priority conditions in the mhGAP programme. — [Documented]

## Limitations / bias
- Global perspective, with no US-specific figures or services.
- A summary fact sheet: it names techniques (slow breathing, PMR) without giving steps.
- WHO tends to promote its own programmes (PM+, SH+).

## Best for
- Headline prevalence and treatment-gap numbers, a WHO-endorsed self-care list, and the link between anxiety and suicide risk that justifies screening.
