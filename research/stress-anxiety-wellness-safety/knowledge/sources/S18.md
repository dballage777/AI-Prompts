## Learned points
1. Scope: adults 18+ with generalised anxiety disorder (GAD, "chronic anxiety") or panic disorder (with or without agoraphobia or panic attacks); aim is full remission, linked to better functioning and lower relapse. — Documented
2. Core principle (1.2.1): follow the stepped-care model, "offering the least intrusive, most effective intervention first." — Documented
3. GAD Step 1 (all known/suspected GAD): identification and assessment, education about GAD and treatment options, and active monitoring; NICE says education and active monitoring may improve less severe presentations and avoid further intervention (1.2.10). — Documented
4. Identification tool: consider asking about anxiety and ability to stop or control worry using the 2-item GAD-2 (1.2.2). Assessment should weigh distress and functional impairment, not just symptom counts (1.2.6). — Documented
5. GAD Step 2 (not improved after Step 1): low-intensity psychological interventions, chosen by preference: individual non-facilitated self-help, individual guided self-help, or psychoeducational groups (1.2.12). — Documented
6. Non-facilitated self-help spec (1.2.13): written or electronic materials of suitable reading age, based on CBT principles, worked through systematically over at least 6 weeks, with minimal therapist contact (e.g., an occasional call of no more than 5 minutes). — Documented
7. Guided self-help spec (1.2.14): CBT-based materials supported by a trained practitioner who facilitates and reviews progress; usually 5 to 7 weekly or fortnightly sessions of 20 to 30 minutes. Psychoeducational groups (1.2.15): CBT-based, interactive, about 1 therapist per 12 participants, usually 6 weekly 2-hour sessions. — Documented
8. Implication: NICE's self-help is structured (CBT-based, multi-week, progress reviewed by a person); a chatbot's coping tips are not the same as guided self-help and should not be presented as treatment. — Inferred
9. GAD Step 3 (marked functional impairment or inadequate response to Step 2): offer either individual high-intensity psychological intervention (CBT or applied relaxation, usually 12 to 15 weekly 1-hour sessions, delivered by trained practitioners) or drug treatment; choose by preference since there is "no evidence" either mode is better (1.2.17–1.2.20). — Documented
10. Medication cautions (for prescribers): SSRI first-line (sertraline considered first for cost); do not offer benzodiazepines for GAD except short-term in crises; no antipsychotics in primary care; people under 30 starting SSRIs/SNRIs should be warned of increased risk of suicidal thinking and self-harm in a minority, seen within 1 week, and monitored weekly for the first month (1.2.23–1.2.30). Implication: a bot should never advise starting, stopping, or changing medication. — Documented (implication Inferred)
11. Referral to Step 4 (specialist) is considered for severe anxiety with marked functional impairment plus risk of self-harm or suicide, significant comorbidity (e.g., substance misuse), self-neglect, or inadequate response to Step 3 (1.2.37). — Documented
12. Comorbidity: treat the more severe primary disorder first; harmful or dependent substance misuse should be treated first as this may significantly improve GAD symptoms (1.2.8–1.2.9). — Documented
13. Panic disorder stepped care has 5 steps: recognition and diagnosis; treatment in primary care; review and alternative treatments; review and referral to specialist services; care in specialist services (1.3). — Documented
14. A panic attack does not necessarily mean panic disorder. People with a panic attack in emergency settings should get minimum investigations to exclude acute physical problems, written information about panic attacks, and referral to primary care (1.3.6). Implication: chest pain or similar symptoms warrant medical checking before assuming "just anxiety." — Documented (implication Inferred)
15. Panic Step 2: for mild to moderate panic disorder, offer non-facilitated or facilitated self-help (1.3.9); offer information about support groups (1.3.10); discuss benefits of exercise (1.3.11). — Documented
16. Panic Step 3: for moderate to severe panic disorder consider CBT (optimal total 7 to 14 hours, weekly 1–2 hour sessions completed within 4 months) or an antidepressant; benzodiazepines, sedating antihistamines, and antipsychotics should not be prescribed for panic disorder (1.3.12–1.3.21). — Documented
17. Panic Step 4: if 2 interventions (any combination of psychological, medication, or bibliotherapy) have been provided and significant symptoms remain, offer referral to specialist mental health services (1.3.35). — Documented
18. People doing self-help for panic should be offered contact with primary care professionals so progress can be monitored, likely every 4 to 8 weeks (1.3.44). Implication: self-help should be paired with a human check-in, not done alone indefinitely. — Documented (implication Inferred)
19. Stopping antidepressants abruptly can cause discontinuation/withdrawal symptoms (dizziness, numbness/tingling, nausea, headache, sweating, anxiety, sleep disturbance); people should seek advice from their doctor (1.3.30–1.3.33). — Documented

## Limitations / bias
- UK guideline (NHS service model); not US-specific, though the clinical principles transfer.
- Written for clinicians; many recommendations are from 2004–2011 and NICE itself says an update is needed.
- Concerns diagnosed GAD/panic disorder, not everyday stress.
- Medication details are for prescribers only and must not be relayed as advice by a bot.

## Best for
- The evidence-based stepped-care logic (self-help first for milder cases, then therapy/medication, then specialist referral) and concrete triggers for when to refer.
