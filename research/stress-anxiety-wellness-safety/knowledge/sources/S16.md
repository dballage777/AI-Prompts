## Learned points
1. Definition: psychotherapy ("talk therapy") is a variety of treatments that help a person identify and change troubling emotions, thoughts, and behaviors; usually one-on-one with a licensed professional or in a group. Goals: symptom relief, maintaining or improving daily functioning, better quality of life. — Documented
2. Reasons to seek therapy listed by NIMH include severe or long-term stress from a job or family situation, grief, relationship problems, and persistent symptoms such as sleep/appetite changes, low energy, loss of interest, irritability, excessive worry, or hopelessness that won't go away. Relevant to a career + self-help bot: job stress is named as a legitimate reason to see a therapist. — Documented
3. NIMH says a health care provider exam first can rule out physical causes, because symptoms like mood change or poor concentration are sometimes due to a medical condition. A bot should not assume every symptom is psychological. — Documented
4. Elements of psychotherapy (plain-language menu): CBT (notice automatic inaccurate/harmful thoughts, question them, see how they affect emotions/behavior, change self-defeating patterns); coping and problem-solving strategies; social and communication skills; mindfulness and relaxation (meditation, breathing exercises); exposure therapy for anxiety disorders; tracking emotions and behaviors; supportive counseling; and safety planning for thoughts of self-harm or suicide. — Documented
5. Exposure therapy (a type of CBT) is described as brief periods in a supportive environment learning to tolerate distress from feared items, ideas, or imagined scenes until fear goes down over time. This is a clinician-delivered technique in NIMH's framing. — Documented
6. Safety plan components named by NIMH: recognize warning signs and use coping strategies, such as contacting friends, family, or emergency personnel. — Documented
7. No formal FDA-style approval process exists for psychotherapies, but many are evidence-based, shown in large patient studies to reduce symptoms of depression, anxiety, and other disorders. — Documented
8. Choosing a therapist: rapport and trust are "essential." Suggested questions: credentials and specialty; approach used and its evidence base; experience with the age group and condition; goals, timeframe, how progress is assessed; medication options; confidentiality and its limits. If not improving after a reasonable time, talk to the therapist and consider other professionals or approaches. — Documented
9. Who provides therapy: psychiatrists, psychologists, social workers, counselors, psychiatric nurses. Ask about fees, insurance, and sliding-scale fees. Low-cost options: university training clinics and state/county health department programs; insurer provider lists. — Documented
10. Directories NIMH lists (without endorsement) include ABCT, APA (both associations), AAMFT, NASW, Psychology Today, ADAA, Mental Health America, NAMI, and others. — Documented
11. US hotlines listed: 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988, chat at 988lifeline.org, 24/7, free, confidential; Spanish: "Para ayuda en español, llame al 988"); Disaster Distress Helpline (call or text 1-800-985-5990); SAMHSA National Helpline 1-800-662-HELP (4357) for treatment referral, 24/7; Veterans Crisis Line (dial 988 then press 1, text 838255, or chat). Call 911 in life-threatening situations. — Documented
12. Self-triage infographic: mild symptoms for less than 2 weeks (sad, stressed or worried but still able to care for self and others; tired but completing tasks; less interest; some trouble sleeping) -> try exercise, healthy eating, 7–9 hours of sleep, time with loved ones, meditation or breathing exercises, enjoyable low-stress activities, volunteering; if these don't help or symptoms worsen, talk to a health care provider. — Documented
13. Severe symptoms for 2 weeks or more (hopeless, irritable, worthless/guilty, low energy, loss of interest, poor concentration, trouble completing tasks, changes in appetite, weight, sleep, or sex drive) -> "Seek professional help." Thoughts of suicide or urges to self-harm -> get immediate help via 988; 911 if life-threatening. This gives a bot a simple, sourced escalation ladder. — Documented
14. Digital tools: NIMH says apps and web programs can give information and feedback without a therapist, but there is "very little industry regulation and very little information on app effectiveness." Listed pros: convenience, anonymity, first step into care, lower cost, 24-hour service, complementing therapy. Listed concerns: effectiveness, audience fit, privacy, lack of quality standards, regulation, and "overselling" that may turn people away from more effective therapies. — Documented
15. NIMH app-choice tips: ask a trusted provider; check whether the app says what to do if symptoms worsen or in a psychiatric emergency; decide between fully automated vs. contact with a trained professional; check developer credentials; beware misleading logos (NIMH endorses no apps); check PubMed; see whether it's based on a tested treatment; try it for a few days. — Documented
16. Evidence claim: NIMH states research has shown internet-based CBT is as effective as conventional CBT for depression, anxiety, social anxiety disorder, and panic disorder (no specific study cited on the page). — Research (as reported by NIMH)
17. NIMH has funded over 400 grants on technology-enhanced mental health interventions, including AI tools, and links a research highlight titled "Chatbot Encourages People With Eating Disorders to Seek Care" (not opened). — Documented
18. Implication for a bot: NIMH frames self-help techniques (breathing, mindfulness, problem-solving, tracking) as legitimate for mild, short-lived symptoms, but frames safety planning, exposure therapy, and diagnosis as part of professional care; a bot can teach the simple skills and should hand off the rest. — Inferred
